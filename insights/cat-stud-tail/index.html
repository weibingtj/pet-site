Cat Stud Tail: Why the Tail Base Gets Greasy and Bald

Skin & Coat · 2026-10-21

Bottom line: Wash the tail base two to three times a week with a degreasing or benzoyl peroxide pet shampoo, rinse thoroughly, dry it well, and leave the blackheads alone - and see a vet if the skin is red, infected, itchy elsewhere, or the hair loss is spreading beyond the tail base.

A greasy, thinning patch of hair at the very top of the tail is stud tail - an overactive scent gland, not a hygiene failure, and not contagious. It is manageable at home with a degreasing shampoo two to three times a week. It only needs a vet if the skin is inflamed, infected, or the baldness is spreading past the tail base.

Owners usually spot it while stroking the cat: a waxy, dark, slightly smelly patch right where the tail meets the back. Some assume the cat has stopped grooming. Most of these cats are grooming perfectly well everywhere else, which is the clue that this is a gland problem in one small area.

What stud tail actually is

The top of the tail carries a dense cluster of sebaceous glands, the supracaudal gland. In some cats these glands enlarge and produce far more oil than the skin needs. The result is a build-up of waxy sebum, blocked pores, and hair that falls out or is groomed out from the greasy patch.

It is most common in entire male cats, which is where the name comes from, but it is regularly seen in neutered males and in females too. Hormones influence it rather than fully explain it, which is why neutering helps some cats and changes nothing in others.

Signs you are probably looking at

  • A dark, waxy or greasy patch at the base of the tail, on the upper surface.
  • Thinning hair or a bald strip over that patch - often a neat line along the top of the tail.
  • Blackheads - small dark dots in the skin, sometimes called tail comedones.
  • A stale, oily smell on your hand after stroking the tail base.
  • Brown staining on light-coloured bedding where the tail rests.
  • Occasional licking or biting at the spot. Mild interest is normal, constant chewing is not.

Why it is not just dirt

It is worth being clear, because the wrong treatment makes it worse. Stud tail is sebum your cat's own glands over-produce, not grime from outside. Two consequences follow:

  • Bathing more often helps; scrubbing harder does not. You are removing oil, not grit, so a degreasing shampoo and thorough rinsing do the work.
  • Dish soap and human shampoo are the wrong tools. They strip the coat, leave the skin tight and irritated, and can trigger a rebound of oil production plus dandruff.

It is also not the same as ordinary feline dandruff, which is dry and flaky rather than waxy - our guide to cat dandruff covers that picture separately, and the whole coat-care picture sits in the skin and coat guide.

The home routine that works

Consistency beats intensity. Two to three washes a week for the first few weeks, then once a week for maintenance.

1. Wet only the tail base and the last third of the back. A full bath is unnecessary and stresses most cats.

2. Use a degreasing or benzoyl peroxide pet shampoo made for cats. Benzoyl peroxide dissolves sebum and helps clear the blocked follicles. Follow the contact time on the label - usually five to ten minutes.

3. Massage gently with fingertips or a soft rubber brush. Do not pick or squeeze the blackheads; squeezing ruptures follicles and causes infection.

4. Rinse for twice as long as you think you need to. Residual shampoo is a common cause of the irritation people then blame on the condition.

5. Dry thoroughly with a towel, and keep the cat warm afterwards. Damp fur against skin encourages bacterial overgrowth.

6. Brush the area daily with a soft brush to lift loose hair and spread the coat's natural oils.

If bathing is a genuine battle, medicated wipes or a chlorhexidine wipe used daily are a reasonable compromise - see how to bathe a cat for the handling side.

What makes it flare

  • Long gaps between washes once the gland is already overactive.
  • Obesity, because a cat who cannot reach the tail base physically cannot groom it, and the oil accumulates unchecked.
  • Arthritis in older cats, which limits the same reach for a different reason. A stiff cat with a greasy tail base often needs pain management more than shampoo.
  • Stress and hormonal fluctuation, which is why some cats flare at particular times and not others.
  • Flea and allergy itch, which adds licking and self-trauma on top of the gland problem.

When it is something else

Stud tail is common enough that it is easy to mislabel everything at the tail base as stud tail. Consider alternatives when:

  • The baldness spreads up the back or down the hind legs - think cat hair loss causes rather than a single gland.
  • There is intense itching, scabbing or a rash - cat flea allergy causes exactly this distribution, and so do food and environmental allergies.
  • The skin is red, weeping, or has pus - a secondary bacterial infection needs treatment beyond shampoo.
  • There are circular scaly patches with hair loss - check for cat ringworm, which is contagious to people.
  • The cat is over-grooming the area to the point of bare skin or sores - this crosses into a behaviour problem as well as a skin one.

Does neutering fix it?

Sometimes, not reliably. In an entire male cat with a recent onset, neutering often improves stud tail over a few months, because the gland is hormone-responsive. In a cat who has had it for years, or in a neutered cat, surgery changes little and the answer is maintenance washing. Neutering is still worth discussing for other reasons, but do not expect it to be a cure.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: book an appointment if the tail base is red, hot, weeping, painful to touch or has pus, if there are open sores from licking, if the hair loss extends well beyond the tail base or the cat is losing weight and grooming less overall, if the cat cannot reach the area because of stiffness or pain, or if six weeks of consistent degreasing washes has made no difference - infected follicles, ringworm, allergies and, rarely, hormonal disease all need a diagnosis rather than more shampoo.

The bottom line

Wash the tail base two to three times a week with a degreasing or benzoyl peroxide pet shampoo, rinse thoroughly, dry it well, and leave the blackheads alone - and see a vet if the skin is red, infected, itchy elsewhere, or the hair loss is spreading beyond the tail base.

Sources: Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Feline Health Center; VCA Animal Hospitals - Feline Stud Tail (Supracaudal Gland Hyperplasia); Merck Veterinary Manual - Seborrhoea and Tail Gland Hyperplasia in Cats.

FAQs

Is stud tail in cats contagious to other pets?

No. Stud tail is an overactive sebaceous gland on the cat's own tail, not an infection, so it cannot spread to other cats, dogs or people. Ringworm and some bacterial infections at the same site are contagious, which is one reason a spreading or scaly patch should be checked by a vet rather than assumed to be stud tail.

How often should I wash a cat with stud tail?

Two to three times a week for the first few weeks while the oil build-up is brought under control, then once a week for maintenance. Use a degreasing or benzoyl peroxide shampoo formulated for cats, allow the labelled contact time, and rinse for longer than feels necessary. Daily brushing between washes helps keep the coat from matting.

Will neutering cure my cat's stud tail?

It can improve it, especially in a young entire male with a recent onset, because the gland responds to hormones. It does not reliably cure older cats or cats who have had the problem for years, and neutered males and females get stud tail too. Maintenance washing is the dependable part of the plan.

Can I squeeze the blackheads on my cat's tail?

No. Squeezing ruptures the follicles and pushes bacteria deeper, which turns a cosmetic problem into an infected one. Shampoo with benzoyl peroxide and gentle brushing do the same job safely over a couple of weeks. If the area becomes red, swollen or painful, that is a vet visit rather than more squeezing.
