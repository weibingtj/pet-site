Cat Constipation: Why Your Cat Strains and What Helps

General · 2026-10-23

Bottom line: Rule out a urinary blockage first, then raise water intake with wet food, add soluble fibre only alongside more water, and see a vet if there has been no stool for 48 to 72 hours - because repeated bouts lead to a stretched colon that is far easier to prevent than to reverse.

Cat constipation is what you are looking at when a cat passes small, hard, dry stools with effort - but a male cat straining with nothing coming out can have a urethral blockage that becomes fatal within 24 to 48 hours, so telling the two apart comes before any home treatment. Soft-to-firm stool in small amounts points to constipation. No urine at all, crying in the box and a hard belly point to the emergency clinic.

Owners usually describe it as "he is in the litter box all the time and nothing happens". That sentence needs one more question answered: is anything coming out, and is it stool or urine?

First, rule out a blockage

This step matters most, and it takes thirty seconds.

  • Repeated trips, posturing, nothing produced, crying, or licking at the penis - especially in a male cat - is a urethral obstruction until proven otherwise. Same-hour emergency. Cat peeing outside the litter box covers the wider range of litter box changes.
  • Small, hard, dry stools passed with effort, then normal behaviour - constipation.
  • Straining produces a little clear jelly or liquid around a hard stool - that is tenesmus from constipation, not diarrhoea. It gets mistaken for diarrhoea constantly.

If you are unsure and the cat is male, go to the vet. The cost of being wrong in that direction is a dead cat.

What normal looks like

  • One to two stools a day for most adult cats on wet or mixed feeding.
  • Firm but not hard, log-shaped, moist enough to leave a clean scoop.
  • Passed in under a minute, without crying or circling first.

Anything drier, smaller, less frequent or more effortful than that is worth tracking. Photograph the stool - it genuinely helps the vet.

Why cats get constipated

1. Not enough water. Cats evolved as desert hunters with a weak thirst drive, and dry-food-only cats are the classic cases.

2. Hair. Self-grooming sends hair through the gut, and a heavy hair load slows transit, especially in long-haired cats.

3. Obesity and inactivity. A heavy cat moves less, and the colon moves less with him.

4. Arthritis and box design. A stiff senior cat may be holding on because the box has high sides or sits down a flight of stairs.

5. Stress and box hygiene. Too few boxes, a dirty box, a new location, or a new cat in the house.

6. Medications, particularly opioids and some antacids.

7. Dehydration from another illness - kidney disease and diabetes both leave a cat dry.

8. Megacolon, where a stretched colon stops pushing effectively.

9. Pelvic narrowing after an old fracture, which mechanically narrows the exit.

  1. Less common but serious: a mass, a foreign body, neurological disease, or low potassium.

Home steps that actually help

Work through these in order, and give each change three to five days before judging it.

1. Move toward wet food, or add two to four tablespoons of warm water to every meal. This is the highest-yield change you can make.

2. Offer a fountain and more water bowls, placed away from the food. Many cats drink more from moving water.

3. Add soluble fibre gently - a teaspoon of plain canned pumpkin, or a veterinary psyllium product. Fibre only helps if water intake rises with it; otherwise it makes the stool bulkier and the problem worse.

4. Brush your cat daily if he is long-haired, to cut how much hair he swallows.

5. Play for ten minutes twice a day. Movement genuinely speeds gut transit.

6. Fix the box: one per cat plus one, low entry for seniors, scooped daily, unscented litter.

7. Keep a stool log - date, size, consistency. Patterns show up within a week.

What not to do

  • Never give a human enema. Phosphate enemas cause severe electrolyte crashes and kill cats.
  • No mineral oil or liquid paraffin by mouth. It is easily inhaled and causes pneumonia.
  • No human laxatives without a vet's dose. Several contain stimulants that are not appropriate for cats.
  • No "let us wait another few days". Past 48 to 72 hours without a stool, impaction risk climbs quickly.
  • No suppositories unless your vet has advised one for this specific cat.

When constipation becomes a long-term problem

Some cats, after repeated bouts, develop megacolon - a colon that is permanently stretched and has lost the ability to contract. The pattern is recurrent constipation, then a cat producing huge stools less and less often, losing weight with a distended abdomen. It is managed with prescription fibre diets, laxatives, prokinetics and sometimes colonic surgery, and it is far easier to prevent than to reverse. That is the argument for treating the first episode properly instead of waiting it out.

If your cat is also off his food, read cat not eating before you decide to wait - a cat who stops eating for two days is at risk of liver damage regardless of what the gut is doing. Fountains, low-entry litter trays and grooming tools are in the shop.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: go now if a male cat is straining and producing no urine at all, if there has been no stool for 48 to 72 hours, if the abdomen looks distended or feels hard, if the cat is vomiting, off food, crying when handled or hiding, or if you see blood or worm segments in the stool - and book a non-urgent appointment for any cat who has needed more than two courses of treatment in a year, or whose stools have been small and hard for weeks, since repeated constipation is how megacolon starts.

The bottom line

Rule out a urinary blockage first, then raise water intake with wet food, add soluble fibre only alongside more water, and see a vet if there has been no stool for 48 to 72 hours - because repeated bouts lead to a stretched colon that is far easier to prevent than to reverse.

Sources: Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Feline Health Center: Constipation; VCA Animal Hospitals - Constipation in Cats; Merck Veterinary Manual - Megacolon in Cats.

FAQs

How long can a cat go without pooping before it is an emergency?

Most cats pass stool at least once a day. After 48 hours it is worth a vet call; by 72 hours without a stool, impaction becomes a real risk and treatment is harder. If the cat is also straining, vomiting, off food or has a hard distended belly, do not wait for the 72-hour mark - go sooner.

How do I tell constipation from a urinary blockage?

Look at what is produced. Constipated cats pass small, hard, dry stools with effort. A blocked cat postures repeatedly and produces nothing at all, often crying, licking at the penis, and going off food. Blockage is almost always in male cats and is fatal within 24 to 48 hours, so any male cat straining with no urine needs an emergency clinic immediately.

Is canned pumpkin safe for constipated cats?

Plain canned pumpkin, not pie filling, is a reasonable gentle soluble fibre source - about a teaspoon mixed into wet food. It only works if water intake also increases, so pair it with wet food or added water. Stop and call your vet if it causes gas, vomiting or diarrhoea, and do not rely on it if the cat has had no stool for two days.

Can I use a human enema on my cat?

No. Phosphate enemas cause dangerous electrolyte changes and can be fatal in cats. Mineral oil by mouth is also unsafe because it is easily aspirated into the lungs. If your cat is impacted, the stool needs to be removed by a vet, often with fluids, sedation and a careful enema made for cats, followed by a plan to stop it recurring.
