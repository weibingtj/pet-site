How to Tell If Your Dog Is in Pain: 8 Signs to Watch

General · 2026-10-08

Why dogs hide pain so well

In a pack, the animal that shows weakness gets pushed away from food and safety. That instinct did not disappear when dogs moved onto our sofas. A dog in pain will often keep wagging, keep eating, and keep greeting you at the door while quietly compensating somewhere else. This is why owners so often hear "I had no idea" after a diagnosis of arthritis, a cracked tooth, or a spinal problem. The pain was there; the vocabulary was not.

8 signs of pain most owners miss

1. Posture changes. A hunched or roached back, a tucked-up belly, or a head carried unusually low. A dog who holds a "prayer stretch" (front down, rear up) over and over is often stretching a painful abdomen or spine.

2. Reluctance to move. Stiffness when getting up, refusing the stairs, hesitating before jumping onto the couch or into the car — especially after rest.

3. Changes while eating. Dropping food, chewing on one side only, drooling more, or suddenly preferring soft food. That pattern points at the mouth or teeth.

4. Sleep that changed. Pacing at night, circling before lying down, or waking and shifting position repeatedly. Discomfort is often loudest when the house is quiet.

5. Licking one spot. Constant licking at a paw, flank, wrist or tail base is a classic pain signal, not just an itch habit.

6. Panting at rest. Heavy panting in a cool room, with no exercise and no heat, is frequently pain rather than stress.

7. Flinching when touched. Tensing or pulling away when you pick your dog up, put on a harness, or run a hand down the back.

8. Personality changes. A friendly dog who suddenly growls when lifted, a bold dog who starts hiding, or a dog who becomes clingy. Behaviour change is one of the most reliable pain indicators vets look for.

Match the sign to the likely area

  • Stiff, slow to rise, will not jump — joints, hips or spine. Very common in older and large-breed dogs.
  • Dropping food, chewing one side, drooling — mouth or teeth. See the warning signs in our dog dental disease guide.
  • Head shaking, rubbing the ear, tilting the head — ear canal or ear flap.
  • Squinting, pawing at the face, avoiding bright light — the eye itself.
  • Limping, licking a pad, chewing a claw — paw, nail or pad. A quick weekly check is covered in our grooming, paws and ears guide.
  • Hunched belly, unwilling to lie down, restless — abdomen. Treat this one as urgent.

How to check at home without adding pain

1. Watch before you touch. Ten minutes of quiet observation tells you more than any prodding.

2. Film a short clip. Twenty seconds of your dog walking, standing and getting up is genuinely useful for your vet and can save a return trip.

3. Write down what changed and when. Onset, which leg, after which activity — the history narrows the cause fast.

4. Do a gentle hands-on check only where it is safe. Run flat palms down the legs and along the back with light pressure. Stop at the first flinch.

5. Never press a swollen joint or a painful belly to "test" it. You can do damage, and your dog may bite out of pure reflex.

6. Do not reach for human painkillers. This is worth repeating: ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin and acetaminophen are toxic to dogs at doses a human would barely notice.

Everyday comfort while you wait for the appointment

  • Soft, thick bedding in a draught-free spot, and non-slip rugs on hard floors.
  • Short, gentle walks on the lead instead of free running, and skip the stairs where you can.
  • Raise the bowls if your dog struggles to lower the head. For food and water on the floor, see the practical picks in our shop.
  • Keep the routine predictable. Anxious dogs feel pain more. The calm and behavior guide covers settling techniques that help a hurting dog rest.
  • Hug your dog carefully — support the chest and the rear, not just the ribcage.

When to see a vet

Book an appointment the same day if your dog yelps when moved, cannot bear weight on a leg, has a hard or swollen belly, pants heavily while resting, refuses food entirely, or shows sudden aggression when lifted. Those point to acute injury, a spinal problem, or a surgical abdomen — not something to watch for a week. For stiffness, slower walks, and grumbling reluctance to jump, book a routine visit instead; early arthritis and dental pain are far easier to manage when caught before the dog has been compensating for months. Dogs hide pain by instinct, so the first clues are usually changes in posture, movement and routine rather than crying. When two or three signs appear together, or one shows up suddenly and lasts more than a day, that is a vet visit, because pain is a symptom with a cause, not a diagnosis.

FAQs

My dog is still eating and wagging - could he really be in pain?

Yes, very often. Appetite and tail wagging are not reliable pain tests, because dogs are wired to hide weakness. Watch for changes in movement, posture and sleep instead, and ask your vet for a pain check at the next visit.

Can I give my dog aspirin until I get an appointment?

No. Human painkillers including aspirin, ibuprofen and acetaminophen can cause stomach ulcers, kidney damage or fatal toxicity in dogs, and they can interfere with what your vet needs to prescribe. Call your vet for advice on safe options.

My older dog is just slowing down. Is that normal aging or pain?

Slowing down is common, but it should not be assumed to be untreatable. A large share of older dogs with stiffness have osteoarthritis that responds well to a proper pain plan. Book a mobility exam before you accept 'just old age'.

How do vets tell how much pain a dog is in?

They combine your history with a hands-on exam, an assessment of gait and posture, and often imaging or bloodwork. No blood test measures pain directly, so your observations at home carry real diagnostic weight.
