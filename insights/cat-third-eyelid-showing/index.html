Third Eyelid Showing in Cats: Causes and When to Worry

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-10-27

Bottom line: A third eyelid that stays visible in an awake cat means the eye is painful, inflamed, or the cat is unwell or dehydrated, so book a vet exam within a day or two - and go sooner if one eye is squinting, watering or being pawed at, which suggests a painful injury like an ulcer.

A third eyelid that stays visible in an awake, relaxed cat is a sign, not a disease: it usually means the eye is painful, inflamed, sunken from dehydration or illness, or affected by a nerve problem - and occasionally it is the temporary, self-limiting condition known as Haw's syndrome. The membrane should stay tucked away while your cat is awake, so when it remains out and covering part of the eye, something is pushing it forward.

What the third eyelid actually does

Vets call it the nictitating membrane - the pale pink curtain in the inner corner that sweeps diagonally across the eye. It spreads the tear film, protects the cornea and adds a layer of defence. Cats cannot move it deliberately: it rises passively whenever the eyeball retracts slightly (which is what pain causes), whenever the eye loses volume and sinks back, or when the sympathetic nerves that hold it down lose tone. Because it moves passively, a membrane that stays up is always a clue to something else rather than a fault in the membrane itself. That is why the same membrane shows up in such different situations.

The causes, one eye or both

1. Eye pain - the most common reason. A corneal ulcer, scratch or foreign body makes the cat retract the eye, and the third eyelid rides up with it. Look for squinting, watering, pawing at the face and light sensitivity.

2. Conjunctivitis - the membrane itself becomes red, thickened and visible. Herpesvirus flare-ups and chlamydia infections are classic culprits, often with discharge.

3. A foreign body behind the membrane - a grass seed or grit trapped under it keeps the eye irritated and the membrane up, usually on one side only.

4. Haw's syndrome - both third eyelids protrude at once, mostly in young cats, often a few weeks after a bout of diarrhoea or a viral gut infection. It looks dramatic but typically settles on its own over weeks once a vet has ruled out other causes.

5. A smaller or sunken eye - dehydration, significant weight loss or a long illness reduces the fat and muscle behind the eyeball, so the eye sits deeper and the membrane drapes across it. An eye that shrank after an old injury (phthisis bulbi) does the same permanently.

6. Nerve problems - Horner's syndrome raises the third eyelid on one side along with a small pupil and a droopy upper lid, triggered by ear infections, injuries or, rarely, growths along the nerve pathway.

Why one-versus-both matters

One eye = think local. Pain, ulcer, foreign body or infection in that eye. Treat it as the urgent version - a painful eye can go from scratch to sight-threatening ulcer within a day or two.

Both eyes = think systemic. Illness, dehydration, fever or Haw's syndrome. Still call the vet, and in the meantime check whether your cat is eating, drinking and behaving normally - a quiet, off-food cat with two raised membranes is telling you the whole body is unwell, not just the eyes. A vet visit is worthwhile either way: ruling out pain and infection takes minutes, and it separates a waiting game from a treatment plan.

What you can safely do at home

  • Look and photograph. Compare both eyes in daylight and note whether one or both are affected, and whether the eye is squinting or watering. A photo helps the vet judge progression.
  • Do not push the membrane back or use human eye drops. Neither fixes the cause and some human drops actively harm an ulcerated cornea.
  • Stop the rubbing. If your cat is pawing at the eye, a soft e-collar prevents self-injury on the way to the appointment.
  • Support the basics. Fresh water, tempting food and a quiet space matter when both eyes point to systemic illness. Gentle cleaning of discharge around the eyes with damp cotton is fine; our eye care guide covers routine maintenance, and tear-stain wipes keep the area dry once the eye itself has been treated.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: book an exam within a day or two for any third eyelid that stays visible while your cat is awake, and go the same day if the eye is also squinting, watering heavily, cloudy or being pawed at - a painful eye is an emergency until a vet proves otherwise, and both eyes showing at once with low energy or no appetite also needs a same-day call.

The bottom line

A third eyelid that stays visible in an awake cat means the eye is painful, inflamed, or the cat is unwell or dehydrated, so book a vet exam within a day or two - and go sooner if one eye is squinting, watering or being pawed at, which suggests a painful injury like an ulcer.

FAQs

Is it normal to see a cat's third eyelid when sleeping?

Yes. The membrane often shows when a cat is dozing or just waking, because the eye rolls back and the lid relaxes. The concern is only when the membrane stays visible while the cat is fully awake and alert.

What is Haw's syndrome in cats?

It is the name for both third eyelids protruding at once, mostly in younger cats, often following a viral gut upset. A vet diagnoses it by ruling out pain, infection and other causes first. Most cases fade on their own over several weeks without treatment.

Can I treat my cat's protruding third eyelid at home?

No. Home care stops at protecting the eye from rubbing and cleaning discharge from the fur. The membrane is a symptom, and the underlying cause - ulcer, infection, dehydration or nerve involvement - decides the treatment, which is prescription territory.

How long does it take for the third eyelid to go back?

It returns to normal once the cause resolves - within days for a treated ulcer or infection, and over a few weeks for Haw's syndrome. If it stays up beyond that despite treatment, ask the vet to look again.
