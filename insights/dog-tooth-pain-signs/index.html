How to Tell If Your Dog's Tooth Hurts: 7 Signs to Watch

Dental Care · 2026-11-16

Bottom line: Watch how your dog chews rather than waiting for a yelp, and book an exam once two or three signs stack up. Facial swelling or exposed pulp means today, not next week.

Dogs rarely cry out from a sore tooth. They eat, play, and wag through pain that would put a person in bed, which is exactly why dental disease is one of the most common problems vets find and one of the most quietly tolerated by owners. If you are wondering whether your dog's mouth hurts, do not wait for a yelp. Watch how your dog eats, and watch the small habits around food.

Why dental pain hides so well in dogs

Dogs are wired to mask weakness, and a sore tooth is easy to work around. A dog with pain on one side of the mouth simply shifts food to the other side, so dinner still disappears and nothing looks wrong.

There is also a generational layer to this. Many owners grew up being told that bad breath is normal for dogs and that chewing on a hard bone cleans teeth. Neither is true. Bad breath is a symptom of bacteria and inflammation in the mouth, not a breed trait or a fact of life.

On top of that, most periodontal damage happens below the gumline, where you cannot see it. By the time tartar is thick enough to notice, the bone holding the tooth in place has often started to recede.

The 7 signs a tooth is hurting

1. Chewing on one side only, or a head tilt that appears at mealtime and disappears afterwards.

2. Dropping food or letting kibble fall out mid-chew, then picking it back up off the floor.

3. Eating more slowly than usual, or walking up to the bowl and backing away.

4. Pawing at the face or rubbing the muzzle along carpet and furniture.

5. Fresh blood on chew toys, or a pink tinge in the water bowl after drinking.

6. Swelling under the eye or a firm lump along the jawline, which points to a tooth root abscess rather than a surface problem.

7. New reluctance to be touched near the head, including flinching when you reach for the collar.

A dog does not need every item on this list. Two or three of these signs together is enough reason to book an exam.

The signs that mean today, not next week

Some dental findings move faster than others. A dog with a swollen face, a draining tract under the eye, a fever, or a refusal to eat or drink for more than a day needs to be seen the same day. Tooth root abscesses and jaw fractures do not improve with time and do not respond to home care.

You should also treat a broken tooth with a visible pink or red center as urgent. That pink dot is exposed pulp, the living tissue inside the tooth. It is painful, it is open to infection, and it cannot heal over by itself.

What actually happens at the vet visit

You will not get a real answer from a quick visual peek at your dog's mouth, and neither will a groomer. Probing, charting, and dental X-rays are what show the true extent of the problem.

1. A conscious oral exam checks for loose teeth, fractures, and visible masses.

2. A full exam under anaesthesia allows probing around every tooth and imaging of the roots.

3. Dental X-rays reveal bone loss, resorption, and abscesses that look invisible from above the gumline.

If your dog needs a cleaning or an extraction, expect a plan built around anaesthesia with proper pain control, not a scale-and-polish on a wake dog. Anaesthetic-free dentistry is cosmetic at best and risky at worst, because it cannot treat what is happening under the gum.

Small changes that buy comfort while you wait

You cannot fix an infected tooth at home, but you can stop making it worse. Swap out any hard object your dog chews on that could fracture a weakened tooth, including antlers, hooves, and very hard nylon bones. Soften dry food with warm water if chewing appears to hurt. Keep brushing gentle, or pause on the painful side until your vet has looked at it.

What this is not

This article is general education, not veterinary advice for your specific dog. Pain and infection in the mouth cannot be diagnosed from a photo or a description, and only a veterinarian who examines your dog can tell you which tooth is involved and what treatment it needs.

When to see a vet

Book a same-day appointment if your dog has facial swelling, a draining wound under the eye, bleeding from the mouth that will not stop, a broken tooth with a pink or red center, or has refused food and water for more than 24 hours. Book promptly for any two of the seven signs above, even if your dog is still eating normally.

The bottom line

Watch how your dog chews rather than waiting for a yelp, and book an exam once two or three signs stack up. Facial swelling or exposed pulp means today, not next week.

FAQs

Can a dog have a painful tooth and still eat normally?

Yes. Most dogs with dental pain keep eating because they shift food to the other side of the mouth. Dropping food, eating more slowly, and chewing on one side are the more reliable clues.

How can I check my dog's teeth at home?

Lift the lip and look at the outer surface of the back teeth and along the gumline. Red gums, brown tartar, a broken tooth, or a strong smell all mean it is time for a professional exam. Do not probe with your fingers.

Will a broken tooth heal on its own?

No. Enamel does not regrow and exposed pulp does not seal over. A broken tooth needs treatment or extraction, and untreated pulp exposure leads to infection, pain, and eventually a root abscess.

Does bad breath mean my dog is in pain?

Not always, but it does mean bacteria and inflammation are present. Bad breath is a symptom worth investigating rather than a normal part of being a dog, and it is often the earliest sign owners notice.
