Cat Cataracts: Signs, Causes and When Surgery Can Help

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-11-23

A cloudy white or gray spot in your cat's eye is usually a cataract: an opacity of the lens that slowly blocks vision. No drop dissolves a cataract, and a vet exam is the only way to tell it from harmless aging. Catch it early and you keep more options open.

What a cataract really is

The lens sits just behind the pupil and focuses light onto the retina. When its normally clear proteins clump together, the lens turns cloudy and light can no longer pass cleanly through. That cloud is a cataract. It is different from nuclear sclerosis, a faint blue-gray haze that comes with age and barely affects a cat's sight. A cataract is denser, often uneven, and it genuinely interferes with vision.

It helps to know that a cataract is a sign of something else, not a single disease. Something has to trigger the lens to change. In cats, the usual triggers are diabetes, inflammation inside the eye, inherited traits, injury, or simple old age. Finding the trigger matters because it shapes the whole treatment plan.

Early signs owners miss

Cats are masters at hiding vision loss. They map their home by memory and smell, so a cat can lose a lot of sight before you notice anything. Watch for:

  • Bumping or hesitating near furniture, doorframes, or the litter box, especially in dim light.
  • Reluctance to jump onto favorite perches or stairs the cat used to take in stride.
  • A white or gray fleck visible deep in the pupil, sometimes only in certain light.
  • Uneven or wide pupils, or one eye that looks different from the other.
  • Clumsiness or startle when you approach from the side, with a stronger reliance on whiskers and smell.

None of these signs confirms a cataract on its own, but a cluster of them is a good reason to book an exam rather than assume it is normal aging.

What causes cataracts in cats

Diabetes mellitus is the most common underlying cause. High blood sugar changes how the lens handles glucose, and the lens gradually clouds over. Cat diabetes differs from dog diabetes: feline cataracts tend to develop more slowly and less completely, but they still deserve attention.

Uveitis, inflammation inside the eye, is the other big one. It can follow injury, infection, or immune disease, and the inflammation itself damages the lens. Treating uveitis is urgent on its own because it can also cause glaucoma and pain.

Less often, cataracts are inherited (seen in some breeds), the result of trauma to the eye, or a change of old age. Toxins and radiation are rare causes. Your vet may run bloodwork to look for diabetes and examine the eye for ongoing inflammation before naming the cause.

How a vet confirms it

There is no home test that settles this. A vet uses an ophthalmoscope or slit lamp to look through the pupil at the lens and the retina behind it. They will check eye pressure to rule out glaucoma, use a stain to rule out a corneal ulcer, and often run blood glucose or a fructosamine test to check for diabetes. If inflammation is present, they may test for underlying infections such as FeLV, FIV, or toxoplasmosis.

This is why a wait-and-see approach is risky. Several eye problems look similar from across the room, yet they need very different, sometimes time-critical, treatment.

Treatment: what works and what does not

Let me be blunt about one thing: no eye drop has been proven to dissolve an established cataract. Products that promise to cure cloudiness are marketing, not medicine. Some antioxidant drops are studied for very early changes, but nothing reverses a mature cataract.

What does help is treating the cause. Good diabetes control can slow or sometimes prevent progression. Controlling uveitis protects the lens and the rest of the eye.

Surgery is the only way to actually remove a cataract. The procedure, called phacoemulsification, breaks up and removes the cloudy lens, often with an artificial lens placed in its spot. It is a specialized microsurgical operation and works best when the retina is healthy and there is no active inflammation. Cats generally do well afterward, though it needs ongoing eye drops and checkups. Cost and the need for a referral surgeon are real considerations, so have an honest talk with your vet or a veterinary ophthalmologist about whether it fits your cat.

For cats that are not surgical candidates, the goal shifts to keeping them safe and comfortable. Gradual sight loss is something most cats adapt to remarkably well when their environment stays predictable.

When to see a vet

Book a vet appointment as soon as you notice a cloud in the eye, a change in pupil color, or new clumsiness, because early disease is easier to manage and some causes are urgent. Go the same day if the eye is red, painful, watery, or your cat is squinting, pawing at it, or suddenly bumping into everything; those signs point to uveitis, glaucoma, or an ulcer, which can cost an eye or cause real pain if untreated. A cloudy eye in a diabetic cat is a particular reason not to delay.

Helping a cat with limited sight

Keep furniture, food, and litter in the same places. Add scent cues or a soft mat near key spots so your cat can navigate by touch and smell. Talk before you approach, avoid sudden rearrangements, and supervise stairs or balconies until you know how well your cat manages. The eye care guide covers the daily wiping and discharge questions that often come up with these conditions, and how to give eye drops helps if your vet prescribes topical medication. Gentle eye care tools can keep the area clean, though none of them treat the cataract itself. These steps do not replace treatment, but they make life calmer while you and your vet decide the next move.

The bottom line

A cloudy spot in a cat's eye is most often a cataract, and no drop will dissolve it: the real options are controlling the underlying cause or lens-removal surgery, so book a vet exam early and treat sudden redness, squinting, or pain as urgent.

FAQs

Can cataracts in cats go away on their own?

No. Once lens proteins have clumped into a cataract, the cloud does not clear by itself, and no proven drop dissolves it. The cause can sometimes be managed to slow progression, but an established cataract either stays put or grows, so monitoring by a vet is the sensible path.

Is a cloudy eye in an old cat always a cataract?

Not always. Nuclear sclerosis is a faint, even blue-gray haze of aging that barely affects sight, while a cataract is a denser cloud that blocks vision. Corneal scars, edema, and uveitis can also look cloudy, so a vet exam with a light and pressure check is the only reliable way to tell them apart.

Can cataract surgery be done on cats?

Yes. Veterinary ophthalmologists remove feline cataracts with phacoemulsification, often placing an artificial lens. It works best when the retina is healthy and there is no active inflammation, and it needs ongoing follow-up drops. It is a specialized, costly procedure, so discuss candidacy with your vet.

My diabetic cat's eye looks cloudy. How fast will it progress?

Cataracts from feline diabetes usually develop more slowly than in dogs, but tight glucose control is the biggest factor in slowing them. Have your vet check the eye and review your diabetes plan promptly; sudden redness, squinting, or a rapid change always warrants an urgent visit.
