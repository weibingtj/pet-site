Cherry Eye in Dogs: Causes and When to Take Action

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-10-11

Cherry eye is the tear gland of the third eyelid popping out of place - a red, round, shiny lump sitting in the inner corner of the eye - and it is not a tumour, not cancer, and not something that eye drops alone will fix. It is uncomfortable, and it does need attention, because a gland that stays out of position can lose its ability to produce tears, and a dog that cannot produce tears ends up with dry eye for life. The most useful thing you can do in the first hour is stop your dog from rubbing it and get that eye looked at.

What is actually happening in there

Dogs have a third eyelid tucked into the lower inner corner of each eye. At its base sits a small tear gland that supplies a large share of the eye's tear film. In some dogs the tissue anchoring that gland is weak, and the gland flips out and sits on the surface like a small, wet cherry - hence the name. The lump you see is normal gland tissue in the wrong place. That matters, because people often assume it is a growth or a stye and put off the vet visit.

It tends to appear suddenly. Many owners notice it after a nap, a rough play session, or a bout of scratching at the face. One or both eyes can be affected, and the second eye follows within weeks to months in a fair number of dogs.

Which dogs are most likely to get it

Cherry eye is largely a structural problem, and it runs in families. It is most common in:

  • Young dogs under two years old, though it can appear at any age.
  • Flat-faced and brachycephalic breeds - Bulldogs, Pugs, Boston Terriers, Shih Tzus.
  • Cocker Spaniels, Beagles, Boxers, Lhasa Apsos, Pekingese and Basset Hounds.
  • Cats too, especially Burmese and Persian lines, though far less often than dogs.

If your breed is on that list and you see a red lump appear in the eye corner, cherry eye should be your first guess.

Why it matters more than it looks

The gland is not decoration. It produces a large part of the tear film that keeps the cornea lubricated and clear. When it is out of position for a long time, two things go wrong. First, the gland itself becomes inflamed and can scar down and stop working. Second, the exposed surface dries out. The end result is keratoconjunctivitis sicca - dry eye - which is a lifelong condition requiring daily medication and, in some dogs, permanent damage to the eye surface.

That is the reason vets do not simply shrug at cherry eye. It is not dangerous in the way a foreign body is, but it is genuinely a time-sensitive problem.

What you can and cannot do at home

You can keep the eye comfortable and stop it getting worse. You cannot put the gland back.

What helps:

  • Stop your dog rubbing the face. An Elizabethan collar or a soft recovery cone overnight is often the single best move.
  • Keep the eye moist with a plain lubricating drop or gel recommended by your vet - this is supportive care, not a cure.
  • Keep the area clean with a damp cotton pad and cooled boiled water. Our guide to cleaning tear stains covers the safe technique.
  • Note the time it appeared and photograph it daily, so you can tell your vet whether it is changing.

What does not help:

  • Antibiotic or steroid drops on their own. They may reduce redness temporarily and give a false sense of progress while the gland stays out.
  • Massaging or pressing on the lump. You can damage the gland and the third eyelid.
  • Waiting weeks to "see if it goes away". Occasionally a mild prolapse reduces on its own in the first days, but that decision belongs to a vet.

If the eye is dry at the same time, ask about tear testing. Our eye care and tear stain guide explains what those tests measure and why they change the plan.

The treatment vets actually use

For many dogs the answer is a short procedure to tack the gland back into its normal position, usually with a stitch that holds the third eyelid tissue in place while it heals. It is done under general anaesthesia and most dogs go home the same day.

Some vets try a course of anti-inflammatory medication first in very fresh, mild cases. Surgical replacement is preferred over removing the gland, because removal takes away a big chunk of tear production and pushes the dog towards dry eye. If a vet offers to simply cut the gland out, it is fair to ask whether replacement is possible instead.

Recovery is usually straightforward. Expect a cone, anti-inflammatory drops, and a recheck. Reported success rates for tacking are good, especially when the gland is replaced early rather than after months of chronic prolapse.

When to see a vet

Get veterinary attention for any new red lump in the eye corner, ideally within 24 to 48 hours. Same-day or emergency care is needed if the eye is squinting shut, the surface looks cloudy or blue, there is thick yellow-green discharge, the lump is dark, bleeding or ulcerated, your dog is pawing at the face constantly, or the eye looks painful to the touch - those signs suggest the surface of the eye is already damaged, and corneal ulcers worsen quickly. This article is general education and not veterinary advice, and it cannot diagnose your dog - if you are unsure, call your vet, because a missed ulcer does not wait.

Keeping the other eye in mind

Because cherry eye is often a breed-linked weakness, the second eye is at real risk. Tell your vet if the other eye ever looks red or watery, and keep up routine grooming and face care, especially in breeds with skin folds around the eyes. Folded skin traps moisture and irritates the surface, which makes an already vulnerable eye worse.

The bottom line

Book a vet appointment for any new cherry eye within a day or two, keep a cone on and the eye lubricated until you get there, and expect the fix to be a short surgical tack rather than drops - replacing the gland early protects your dog's tear production for life. Skip the wait-and-see approach, and treat squinting, cloudiness or a dark, bleeding lump as an emergency. FAQs

Is cherry eye painful for my dog?

It is uncomfortable rather than severely painful in most cases - think of a dry, gritty, irritated eye. Many dogs are bright and eating normally but rub the face and squint occasionally. Because the exposed cornea can dry and ulcerate, any sudden squinting, cloudiness, thick discharge or constant pawing should be treated as urgent rather than routine.

Will cherry eye go away on its own?

Occasionally a very fresh, mild prolapse settles in the first few days, but you cannot predict which ones will and waiting carries a real risk of permanent dry eye. Have a vet assess it. If they decide to watch it, that becomes a decision made with the eye surface properly examined, not a guess.

Can I just use drops instead of surgery?

Drops treat the redness and keep the eye comfortable, but they do not put the gland back in place. If the gland stays prolapsed for months it can scar and stop producing tears, leaving lifelong dry eye. Surgical replacement is preferred over removing the gland for exactly that reason.

Will the other eye get cherry eye too?

It can. Cherry eye reflects an inherited weakness in the tissue holding the gland, so the second eye is at higher risk, sometimes within weeks and sometimes years later. Check both eyes daily for a while after treatment and mention any new redness or watering to your vet.
