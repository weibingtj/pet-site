Dog Scooting & Anal Glands: What's Normal, When to Help

Grooming, Paws & Ears · 2026-10-05

Bottom line: Scooting is a dog dragging its bottom across the floor, and the usual cause is full or irritated anal glands — two small sacs near the anus that should empty on their own. Occasional scooting is common; frequent scooting, blood, or pain is not, and needs a vet.

Bottom line: Scooting is a dog dragging its rear across the carpet, and the usual cause is full or irritated anal glands — two small scent sacs on either side of the anus that normally empty on their own when the dog poops. Occasional scooting is common; frequent scooting, blood, swelling, or pain is not, and it needs a vet.

What anal glands are (and what they do)

Dogs have two anal sacs at roughly the 4 o'clock and 8 o'clock positions around the anus. In the wild, their smelly, oily fluid would mark territory and ease passing stool. Domestic dogs mostly kept the parts but lost the need. In a healthy dog, a firm, well-formed stool presses the glands as it passes and empties them naturally.

When stools are soft, or the ducts are narrow (common in small breeds), the glands do not get squeezed empty. Fluid builds up, thickens, and the dog feels pressure and itch right at the anus. That is the scoot.

Why dogs scoot

1. Full glands from soft stool or anatomy — the most common, straightforward cause.

2. Mild impaction — thickened fluid that will not release, causing discomfort.

3. Infection or abscess — bacteria in a blocked gland, which is painful and can rupture.

4. Skin irritation — worms, allergies, or a dirty coat around the area can also trigger dragging, so scooting is not always the glands.

5. Tapeworms — segments near the anus itch and can look like rice grains; a different fix entirely.

A quick once-over of the area and a recent stool check tell you a lot before assuming glands. Pay attention to the texture of the stool itself: firm, segmented logs squeeze the glands every time; soft or mushy stool slides through without pressure and lets fluid accumulate. That single observation explains most 'my dog needs glands expressed monthly' stories.

When glands need help

If your dog scoots once after a bout of soft stool, it may self-resolve as the stool firms up. But recurring or obsessive scooting means the glands are not emptying. At that point a professional expression is the answer — not a DIY experiment.

Leave it to the pros: groomers and vet techs express glands routinely and can spot early infection. A veterinarian should do it if there is any sign of pain, swelling, or discharge, because that changes the treatment.

Home-care limits and what actually helps

You cannot and should not squeeze the glands yourself at home — done wrong it causes pain, scarring, and chronic inflammation. What you *can* do:

  • Firm up the stool. A diet with enough fiber (pumpkin, a vet-approved fiber supplement) produces the pressure glands need to empty naturally. Loose stool is the root cause more often than the glands themselves.
  • Keep the area clean after walks and baths so fur and debris do not trap irritation.
  • Regular grooming so the region stays dry; our shop carries gentle wipes suited to sensitive skin.
  • Parasite prevention on schedule to rule out tapeworms as a cause.

If glands need expressing every few weeks long-term, ask your vet whether an underlying allergy or stool issue is the real driver.

Small breeds (Toy Poodles, Chihuahuas, Cavaliers) and couch-potato dogs with soft stool are the usual repeat customers. Large breeds with firm stools rarely need manual expression at all. Knowing which camp your dog is in saves unnecessary trips and prevents the over-expression that can itself irritate the ducts.

When to see a vet

See a vet promptly if you notice: scooting paired with blood spots on the floor or in stool; a swollen, red, or painful lump beside the anus; a foul odor with discharge; the dog crying or snapping when the area is touched; or a ruptured abscess (an open sore near the anus). An abscess is painful and can spread infection, and recurring impaction may signal allergies or a duct problem that needs a plan — not just repeated squeezing.

This is general guidance, not a diagnosis. The anus is delicate and close to the bowel; when something looks or smells wrong, a vet is the safe call.

A simple routine that prevents most issues

  • Feed for firm, formed stools — the single biggest lever for natural gland emptying.
  • Keep parasite prevention current.
  • Book grooming that includes a sanitary trim for long-haired dogs.
  • Mention scooting at annual wellness visits so trends get caught early.

Scooting is your dog's way of saying the rear end feels off. Most of the time it is a quick, routine fix — but the red flags above are the line between a minor annoyance and a problem worth a clinic visit.

FAQs

Should I express my dog's anal glands at home?

Generally no. Squeezing them incorrectly can cause pain, scarring, and long-term inflammation. If your dog scoots often, have a groomer or vet tech do it, and let a veterinarian handle any case with pain, swelling, or discharge.

How often should a dog's glands be expressed?

Ideally never on a schedule — healthy dogs empty them naturally. If yours needs it every few weeks, that points to soft stool, allergies, or anatomy, and your vet can address the underlying cause rather than just repeating the expression.

Can diet help with scooting?

Yes, often. Firm, well-formed stools are what press the glands empty. Adding fiber such as plain pumpkin or a vet-approved supplement, and keeping stool solid, resolves many mild cases without any manual expression.

Is scooting always the anal glands?

No. Tapeworms, skin allergies, matted fur, or a dirty coat can all make a dog drag its rear. Check the area for rice-like worm segments or irritation, and if scooting persists, a vet can tell whether the glands or something else are to blame.
