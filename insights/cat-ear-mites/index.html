Cat Ear Mites: Signs, Treatment & When to See a Vet

Ear mites in cats cause dry, dark, coffee-ground debris in the canal with intense head shaking and scratching, and they are very treatable - but every cat and dog in the house has to be treated at the same time, or the mites come straight back. Left alone, that scratching can tear the ear flap or rupture small blood vessels and create a painful haematoma.

What ear mites are

Ear mites (*Otodectes cynotis*) are tiny eight-legged parasites that live on the surface of the ear canal, feeding on skin debris and ear wax. They are not burrowers, which is good news: treatment does not have to chase anything deep into the skin. They are also highly contagious between pets, and they can survive briefly away from a host, which is why bedding and grooming tools matter.

Signs your cat has ear mites

1. Head shaking and scratching. The itch is intense and often one-sided to begin with.

2. Dark, dry, crumbly debris. The classic description is coffee grounds. It is dry, not waxy or runny.

3. Redness and raw patches at the ear base and around the canal opening from scratching.

4. A faint musty or yeasty smell. Mild at first; stronger once a secondary infection sets in.

5. Discharge changes. If it turns thick, waxy, yellow or pus-like, a bacterial or yeast infection has joined in.

6. An aural haematoma. The ear flap swells with blood from violent scratching - painful and usually needs veterinary drainage.

7. Occasional spread beyond the ears. In heavy infestations, mites can be found on the neck, rump and tail base.

Ear mites versus infection versus allergy

The pattern is what separates them. Ear mites are usually both ears, intensely itchy, with dry black debris and no history of swimming or food change. A bacterial or yeast infection is more often one ear, produces waxy brown or yellow discharge with a stronger smell, and may follow swimming, allergies or a floppy-ear anatomy problem. Allergies show up as both ears plus itchy skin elsewhere - paws, face, flanks - and tend to be seasonal or linked to a diet change. How vets confirm it

Diagnosis is quick and usually done in the consult room: a sample of debris examined under the microscope shows the mites and their eggs directly. In some cases a vet will also check for a ruptured eardrum before prescribing, because some medications must not go into a damaged ear. If your cat has been shaking its head for weeks with no improvement from shop products, this is the step that ends the guessing.

Treatment: what works

  • Prescription parasite treatment. Spot-on products containing selamectin, imidacloprid-moxidectin or an appropriate avermectin clear the infestation reliably. Your vet picks the product and the interval based on your cat's weight, age and health.
  • Treat every pet in the home. Cats and dogs both. Untreated carriers keep re-infesting the treated cat, which is why so many "failed" treatments were never really failures.
  • Keep going for three to four weeks. The mite life cycle means one dose is not enough, even if the ears look clean after a week.
  • Wash everything. Bedding, blankets and soft toys in a hot wash, and clean grooming brushes and carriers.
  • Treat secondary infection. If the discharge is yellow or the canal is painful, your cat needs the infection treated as well, not just the mites.

What not to do

  • Never use a dog flea or tick product on a cat. Many contain permethrin, which is highly toxic to cats and can cause tremors, seizures and death. This is the most dangerous mistake in this whole article.
  • Never push a cotton swab into the canal. You will pack debris deeper and risk perforating the eardrum.
  • Do not share ear cleaner or applicators between pets mid-treatment.
  • Do not stop when it looks better. Symptom relief comes well before the life cycle is broken.
  • Do not use human ear drops - the pH and ingredients are not designed for cats.

Cleaning the ears safely at home

1. Warm the cat-specific ear cleaner in your hand for a minute.

2. Fill the canal loosely with cleaner - never a cotton swab.

3. Massage the base of the ear for 20 to 30 seconds; you will hear a squelch.

4. Step back and let your cat shake.

5. Wipe only the visible outer flap and creases with a cotton pad.

6. Stop daily cleaning once there is nothing left to remove - over-cleaning irritates a healing canal.

When to see a vet

See a vet if the ear is painful to touch or swollen, if the discharge is bloody, yellow or pus-like, or if your cat develops a head tilt, circles, loses balance, or has rapid eye flicking (nystagmus) - those point to the middle or inner ear, or to a ruptured eardrum, and are not a home-treatment situation. Book a visit if the debris has not cleared after two to three weeks of correct treatment, if only one ear is affected and getting worse, or if the itching continues with no mites visible under the microscope, because allergy and infection need a different plan. Cats with a weakened immune system, kittens, and cats on other medication should be treated under veterinary supervision rather than with shop products. FAQs

Are ear mites contagious to other pets or to people?

They are highly contagious between cats and dogs, and they spread through shared bedding and grooming tools, so every pet in the home should be treated simultaneously. Human infestation is rare and short-lived - usually a mild itchy rash - but the mites do not complete their life cycle on people.

Can I treat ear mites at home without a vet?

Shop products rarely clear a full infestation because they miss the life cycle, and some over-the-counter products are unsafe for cats. Prescription treatment from your vet, applied to every pet in the house for three to four weeks, is the reliable route.

How long does it take to get rid of ear mites in cats?

Expect the intense itching to ease within a few days and the debris to clear within one to two weeks, but you must continue treatment for three to four weeks to break the life cycle. Stopping early is the most common reason mites come back.

Why does my cat keep getting ear mites after treatment?

Almost always because another pet in the home was not treated, or treatment stopped before the mite life cycle was finished. Bedding, brushes and carriers can also carry mites, so clean those at the same time as you re-treat every animal.
