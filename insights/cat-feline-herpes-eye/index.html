Cat Eye Herpes (FHV-1): Why the Same Eye Keeps Flaring

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-11-01

Bottom line: Treat every squinting eye as a possible corneal ulcer until a vet stains it, never use steroid drops on an unstained eye, and cut stress early because stress is what wakes the virus up.

A cat whose eye waters and squints, improves on drops, then flares again two weeks after the bottle is finished almost certainly has feline herpesvirus rather than a stubborn bacterial infection. FHV-1 is the most common cause of recurring conjunctivitis in cats, it stays in the body for life after the first infection, and the realistic goal is fewer and milder flare-ups rather than a cure.

Why the same eye keeps flaring up

FHV-1 does not leave. After the initial infection, usually as a kitten, the virus hides in nerve tissue and becomes latent. It reactivates when the cat's defenses dip, and the triggers are ordinary: a new cat in the house, boarding or a move, a cold snap, pregnancy, steroid treatment, or any illness that suppresses immunity. Many cats are infected as kittens and carry the virus without ever being tested, so owners often hear the diagnosis years later on the third or fourth eye episode.

Once reactivated, the virus travels back down the nerve to the eye surface, which is why the same eye tends to be affected each time.

What a flare-up looks like

Signs vary from a mild watery eye to a painful, closed eye:

  • Clear, watery discharge first, often turning thicker and yellow-green as bacteria move in
  • Squinting, or holding the eye half shut in bright light
  • Pink, swollen conjunctiva, the tissue lining the lids
  • Sneezing and clear nasal discharge at the same time, since the virus hits the nose too
  • The third eyelid creeping across part of the eye
  • A dull, cloudy, or greyish spot on the eye surface
  • Reluctance to be handled around the head

That last list item about a cloudy spot is the one that changes urgency. A cloudy patch suggests the virus has damaged the cornea, and corneal ulcers are the main serious complication of FHV-1 in cats.

Why antibiotic drops alone do not fix it

Antibiotics do nothing to a virus. They are sometimes prescribed because secondary bacteria colonize a damaged eye surface, and they help that part, but they do not stop the herpes cycle. This is why the pattern of short courses and repeated relapses is so common. If your cat has had three or more episodes in a year, the conversation with your vet should move from another bottle of drops to a plan for managing herpes.

The lysine question

For years, owners were told to give lysine daily to suppress outbreaks. More recent evidence has been disappointing, with several studies finding no meaningful reduction in flare-ups, and some concern that high doses may make things worse rather than better. Do not start or continue a supplement on habit; ask your vet whether it is still worth it for your cat, and review any long-term supplement during your next visit.

What a vet will check first

Most cases are diagnosed on history and exam rather than a lab test, because the pattern of recurrence is itself the clue. The single non-negotiable step is a fluorescein stain, which lights up corneal ulcers. This matters beyond diagnosis: steroid eye drops, which are genuinely useful for some inflammatory eye conditions, can melt a damaged cornea. No steroid drop should go into an eye that has not been stained. If you have leftover drops in a drawer, do not use them on a new episode.

What actually helps during a flare-up

1. Reduce stress where you can. Skip the nail trim, keep the routine steady, and avoid introducing new pets during an episode.

2. Wipe discharge with a warm, damp cotton pad, one pad per eye, wiping away from the eye rather than toward it.

3. Keep the cat eating. A congested cat that cannot smell food stops eating fast, and appetite loss in a cat is its own emergency within a couple of days.

4. Ask your vet about antiviral treatment, usually oral famciclovir for moderate to severe cases, plus lubricant drops to protect the surface.

5. Run a warm shower and let the cat sit in the steamy bathroom for ten minutes to loosen nasal congestion, if the cat tolerates it.

6. Keep the cat indoors and away from other cats until the discharge clears.

Lowering the odds of the next flare-up

You cannot remove the virus, but you can lower the frequency of reactivation:

  • Keep core vaccination current. The FVRCP vaccine does not prevent infection, but it reduces the severity of disease, which is exactly the outcome you care about.
  • Keep stress predictable. For cats, that means vertical space, hiding spots, and one litter box per cat plus one.
  • Feed a good diet and keep weight in range, since overweight cats handle illness worse.
  • Treat dental disease and other chronic problems, because anything that keeps the immune system busy makes reactivation more likely.
  • Wash food bowls and bedding during an outbreak. The virus does not survive long outside the cat, but a shared bowl at the wrong moment is an easy route to the next cat.
  • Think twice about unnecessary steroids, including long-acting injections for itching, in a cat with a known herpes history. Ask about the trade-off.

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit the same day for any eye that is squinting with yellow or green discharge, any dull or cloudy patch on the eye surface, an eye held shut, or a cat that has stopped eating. Corneal ulcers can deepen within 24 to 48 hours, and a deep ulcer is a very different problem from a watery one. This article is general guidance, not a diagnosis, and a stained eye is the only way to rule out the serious version.

The bottom line

Treat every squinting eye as a possible corneal ulcer until a vet stains it, never use steroid drops on an unstained eye, and cut stress early because stress is what wakes the virus up.

FAQs

Is feline herpesvirus contagious to other cats or to people?

It spreads easily between cats through eye and nose discharge, shared bowls, and close contact, and carrier cats can shed the virus intermittently for life. It does not infect people or dogs. A new cat should be kept separate for a couple of weeks and checked by a vet before mixing households.

Can a herpes cat live with other cats safely?

Yes. Most multi-cat homes with an FHV-1 carrier do fine with steady routines, one litter box per cat plus one, and prompt treatment of flare-ups. The risk rises with crowding and with frequent new arrivals, because stress drives reactivation.

Will my cat need the eye removed?

Almost never. Removal is reserved for eyes badly damaged by untreated ulcers or chronic severe disease. The far more common outcome is a cat that has periodic episodes managed with antivirals, lubricants, and stress control.

Does the FVRCP vaccine stop herpes flare-ups?

It does not prevent infection or eliminate the carrier state, but it reduces the severity of disease. That is why vets still recommend keeping it current even in cats with a known herpes history.
