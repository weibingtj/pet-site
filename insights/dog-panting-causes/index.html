Why Is My Dog Panting So Much? Causes and Red Flags

General · 2026-11-12

Bottom line: Normal panting has an obvious trigger and settles fast; panting at rest, at night or with thirst, cough or restlessness deserves a vet visit, and a weekly sleeping respiratory rate under 30 breaths per minute is the simplest number to track at home.

Panting is a dog's air-conditioning: rapid, shallow breathing that dumps heat. It is completely normal after exercise, in warm rooms, and during excitement. What is not normal is panting that shows up at rest, in a cool room, at night, or alongside other symptoms. That kind of panting usually signals pain, anxiety, or a medical problem, and dogs give few other clues before it.

Normal panting vs abnormal panting

Use context first. Normal panting happens for a clear reason: heat, exercise, excitement, stress in the moment. It settles within minutes once the cause is removed, and the dog looks otherwise fine: soft face, relaxed body, responsive.

Abnormal panting has one or more of these features:

  • Starts without heat, exercise or excitement
  • Continues at night or wakes the dog from sleep
  • Is louder, raspier or more effortful than usual
  • Comes with restlessness, pacing, or an inability to settle
  • Pairs with other changes: appetite, weight, thirst, coughing, or a new reluctance to exercise

A useful measurement: count your sleeping dog's breaths over 30 seconds and double it. A resting respiratory rate under 30 breaths per minute is reassuring in most dogs; a rate that keeps climbing over repeated counts on different days is a real data point for your vet, not a guess.

The everyday causes

  • Heat and exercise. Obvious, but dogs overheat faster than we do, and flat-faced breeds like Bulldogs and Pugs manage heat poorly even when they seem fine.
  • Excitement and short-term stress. Car rides, visitors, vet visits. Panting here is the adrenaline system doing its job.
  • Weight. Overweight dogs pant earlier and heavier for the same effort, because insulation and workload both go up.
  • Warm sleeping spots. Heavy coats in heated rooms, beds next to radiators, summer nights without airflow.

None of these need treatment beyond environment and routine adjustments, though weight does: a diet conversation at your next vet visit pays off in less panting, easier joints and a longer life.

Pain: the most overlooked cause

Panting is one of the most reliable quiet pain signals in dogs. A dog with a sore back, an aching hip, a dental abscess or a torn nail often pants constantly at rest while still eating and wagging. Owners report the panting; the limp gets noticed a week later, or never. If your dog pants at rest and nothing else explains it, ask your vet to run a pain-focused exam that includes the mouth, spine, hips and belly. Anxiety and stress patterns

Chronic stress produces panting too, and it looks different from heat panting: it clusters around triggers, comes with pacing, lip licking, yawning, clinginess or hiding, and improves when the trigger leaves. Separation distress, noise phobia and generalised anxiety all present this way. Behavior cases benefit from a plan, not just patience; if the panting tracks with being left alone or with specific noises, that pattern is diagnosable and treatable.

The medical causes worth ruling out

Several conditions show up early as heavy panting:

  • Heart disease. Often with reduced stamina, coughing, or a cough at night. A climbing sleeping respiratory rate is one of the earliest home-trackable signs.
  • Cushing's disease. Panting plus increased thirst, increased appetite, a pot-bellied look, thin skin or hair loss along the flanks, usually in middle-aged and older dogs.
  • Laryngeal paralysis. Classically older large breeds: the pant turns loud, raspy and roaring, and heat intolerance gets dramatic. This one narrows the airway, so it escalates.
  • Anemia. Less oxygen carried in the blood means more breathing effort; check the gums, because pale or whitish gums with heavy panting need same-day care.
  • Fever and infection. Panting as the body tries to shed heat it cannot shed.
  • Medication effects. Steroids such as prednisone make dogs pant heavily as a common side effect, and obesity amplifies every cause on this list.

Heat itself deserves its own caution: panting is how overheating starts, and it can slide into heatstroke fast in warm weather. Our heatstroke in dogs guide covers that progression and the first aid around it.

What to do at home

  • Log it. Note when the panting happens, what it follows, and count the sleeping respiratory rate weekly. Patterns are diagnostic gold.
  • Cool, calm environment, constant water access, and a cooler sleep spot for heavy-coated dogs.
  • Check the gums during a calm moment so you know your dog's normal color; pink is the baseline you will compare against on a bad day.
  • Do not change medications on your own. Never stop or adjust steroids or heart medications without your vet, because rebound can be worse than the side effect.

When to see a vet

Seek same-day care if heavy panting comes with pale or blue-tinged gums, collapse, a distended or hard belly, unproductive retching (a bloat emergency in large deep-chested dogs), loud raspy breathing at rest, or panting that will not settle in a cool room. Book a standard visit soon for panting with increased thirst, weight change, a new cough, or a climbing sleeping respiratory rate, and mention any steroid medication your dog is taking.

The bottom line

Normal panting has an obvious trigger and settles fast; panting at rest, at night or with thirst, cough or restlessness deserves a vet visit, and a weekly sleeping respiratory rate under 30 breaths per minute is the simplest number to track at home.

FAQs

How many breaths per minute is normal for a panting dog?

Panting itself runs far above the resting rate, so counting works best on a sleeping dog: under 30 breaths per minute while asleep is reassuring for most dogs, and a consistent climb over repeated weekly counts is worth reporting even before other symptoms appear.

Can anxiety alone cause heavy panting?

Yes. Stress hormones drive panting that clusters around triggers such as being left alone, noises or car travel, and it comes with pacing, lip licking or hiding. The pattern, not the panting alone, is what points to anxiety.

Why is my dog panting at night in a cool room?

Night panting at rest most often points to pain, anxiety, heart or hormone problems, or a too-warm sleep spot. Rule out the environment first, then have a vet examine for pain sources and listen to the heart.

Does prednisone make dogs pant?

Yes, heavy panting is one of the most common side effects of steroids like prednisone. Never adjust the dose yourself, but mention the panting at your next visit so the vet can weigh it against the reason for treatment.
