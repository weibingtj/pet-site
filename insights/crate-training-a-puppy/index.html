Crate Training a Puppy: A Calm Plan That Actually Works

Calm & Behavior · 2026-10-21

Bottom line: Feed every meal inside the crate, close the door for seconds before minutes, and never open it while the puppy is crying - then cap confinement at roughly one hour per month of age, and stop and get advice if the puppy panics, soils himself every time, or cannot settle after two weeks of short sessions.

Crate training works when the crate is the best place in the house and never a punishment: feed every meal inside it, close the door for seconds before minutes, and never open the door while the puppy is crying. Most puppies accept a crate within two weeks of short, well-paid sessions.

The crate is not a cage. Used properly it is the one place a puppy can be left safely, it makes house training faster, and it becomes the spot he chooses when the house is too much.

What the crate is actually for

Three jobs, in order of how much they matter:

1. Safety. A puppy left loose chews cables, skirting boards and shoes, and swallows things that need surgery.

2. House training. Puppies avoid soiling where they sleep, so a correctly sized crate teaches bladder control faster than any schedule alone.

3. A calm-down zone. A place to go when visitors arrive, when fireworks start, or when a toddler is over.

Notice what is not on that list: somewhere to park an annoying puppy. The moment the crate is used as a time-out for barking or nipping, the association flips and the whole plan stops working.

Sort the size and the position first

  • Just big enough to stand, turn around and lie flat. A crate sized for adult weight is fine if you use the divider panel that usually comes with it. Too much floor space and the puppy picks a corner to toilet in.
  • Bedroom, not utility room, for the first month. Puppies settle far faster within sight and smell of you, and you will hear when they need to go out. Move it further away once nights are quiet.
  • Away from radiators and draughts, and not directly against a window.
  • No food bowls or water buckets left in the crate for long periods - a tipped bowl soaks the bedding.

Days one to three: build the association

The goal here is boredom, not duration. Nothing is closed for more than a few seconds.

1. Feed every meal in the crate with the door open. Put the bowl at the back so he has to walk in. Do this from the first day and most of the work is already done.

2. Throw treats in at random through the day. He walks in, eats, walks out. No pressure either way.

3. Close the door for one second, feed a treat through the bars, open it. Repeat five times.

4. Build to five seconds, then ten, then thirty over two or three days. Always release before he starts to fuss, never after.

5. Add a chew - a stuffed food toy - and close the door while he is already busy with it.

Scatter this across the day in dozens of tiny repetitions rather than one long session.

Nights: the crying question

The first three nights are the hard part, and the answer is not "let him cry it out" for hours.

  • Put the crate beside your bed for the first week or two. A puppy who can smell you settles in minutes instead of screaming for an hour.
  • Take him out every two to three hours at eight weeks, on a lead, straight to the toilet spot, then straight back in. No play, no lights, no chat.
  • Answer genuine distress, ignore protest. Whining that starts the moment you leave and stops when you appear is protest. Panicking - thrashing, howling, drooling, defecating - is distress and needs a smaller step.
  • Never open the door mid-tantrum. Wait for two seconds of quiet, then open - opening during a scream teaches that screaming works.
  • Keep the room dark and boring. Any attention, including telling him off, is a reward at 3am.

How long can a puppy actually hold it?

Use age as a rough ceiling for daytime confinement, and take him out far more often than that:

  • 8 to 10 weeks - about one hour awake, and expect a toilet trip every 60 to 90 minutes.
  • 10 to 12 weeks - about two hours.
  • 3 months - roughly three hours maximum.
  • 4 months - about four hours.
  • 5 to 6 months - five to six hours at most, and only after a toilet trip and a good run.

The old "one hour per month of age, plus one" rule is a ceiling, not a target. A puppy left longer than his bladder allows will soil the crate, and that habit is hard to reverse.

Two weeks in: what good looks like

By the end of the second week most puppies will:

  • Walk into the crate on their own when tired.
  • Stay quiet for 30 to 60 minutes with the door shut while you are in the next room.
  • Sleep through a good part of the night with one or two toilet trips.

If none of that is happening, the step was too big. Go back to seconds and rebuild - it is far faster than pushing through.

What undoes the work

  • Using the crate as punishment. One "go in your bed!" in anger can cost a fortnight of progress.
  • Long confinement during the day. A puppy crated for a full working day is not crate trained, he is shut in, and the result is usually a dog who panics at the sight of the door.
  • Letting him out while he screams. The single most common reason crate training fails.
  • Crating instead of walking. A tired puppy settles; a bored one does not.
  • Bedding he shreds and swallows. Use a bare tray or one flat towel if he eats fabric.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: stop crate training and book an examination if a puppy panics every single time the door closes, injures himself or his mouth on the bars, drools heavily and soaks his bedding, defecates and then eats it, or cannot be left for even a minute after two weeks of short sessions - and see a vet promptly for any puppy who is suddenly unable to settle at night, cries when lifted, or has diarrhoea, since pain, intestinal parasites and urinary infections all look like "he hates the crate".

The bottom line

Feed every meal inside the crate, close the door for seconds before minutes, and never open it while the puppy is crying - then cap confinement at roughly one hour per month of age, and stop and get advice if the puppy panics, soils himself every time, or cannot settle after two weeks of short sessions.

FAQs

How long does crate training a puppy take?

Most puppies accept the crate within one to two weeks of short, daily sessions, and settle happily for an hour or two by the end of the first month. Nights usually quieten by night three to five if the crate is beside your bed. Puppies who were previously shut in a crate for long stretches often take much longer and need the steps made smaller.

Should I let my puppy cry it out in the crate?

Not for hours. Brief protest whining that stops when you leave is normal and should not be rewarded by opening the door. Genuine panic - thrashing, howling, drooling, soiling - means the step was too big, so reduce the time and rebuild. Leaving a panicking puppy to scream for an hour teaches fear of the crate, not independence.

Can I put my puppy in a crate at night from day one?

Yes, with the crate beside your bed and toilet trips every two to three hours at eight weeks. Young puppies cannot physically hold their bladder all night, so plan the trips rather than waiting for an accident. Put him in while he is sleepy, keep the room dark and boring, and take him out on a lead with no play or fuss.

Is it cruel to crate a dog during the working day?

A full working day in a crate is too long for a puppy and unfair for most adult dogs. Puppies need a midday break, a toilet trip and exercise, which usually means a dog walker or daycare. Crates are for short, safe confinement - overnight, during meals, or for an hour while you shower - not for replacing company and walks.
