Dog Corneal Ulcer: Signs, Causes and Why It Is Urgent

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-10-18

Bottom line: Treat any squinting, watery, red eye as an ulcer until a vet proves otherwise, and get a fluorescein stain the same day - put a cone on, never use steroid or human eye drops, and expect a recheck stain before you stop treatment.

A corneal ulcer is a scratch or erosion on the clear surface of the eye, and a dog that is squinting, tearing and holding one eye shut needs to be seen the same day - not next week. The cornea is only about a millimetre thick, and an untreated ulcer can progress to a deep defect, a rupture, or permanent vision loss within days.

The tricky part is that an ulcer looks like a dozen other red eyes. There is one test that settles it: a fluorescein stain, which turns the damaged area green under a blue light. That is why this is a vet visit rather than a wait-and-see problem.

What an ulcer actually is

The cornea is the window at the front of the eye. Its outer layer, the epithelium, is a thin sheet of cells that seals the eye against infection. When that sheet is disrupted - by a scratch, a chemical, a hair rubbing on it, or a tear film that has stopped working - the tissue underneath is exposed. Nerves are exposed too, which is why ulcers hurt far more than their size suggests.

Vets grade ulcers by depth. A superficial ulcer involves only the surface layer. A deep ulcer reaches the stroma. A descemetocele is a ulcer that has eroded almost to perforation, and a melting ulcer is one being actively digested by bacteria and enzymes. The last two are surgical or intensive-care problems.

Signs to look for

  • Squinting or holding the eye closed, often with the third eyelid partly raised.
  • Excessive tearing or a wet streak down the face.
  • Redness of the white of the eye and the conjunctiva.
  • Pawing at the face or rubbing the eye along furniture and carpet.
  • Cloudiness or a blue-grey haze over the surface - this suggests deeper damage.
  • A visible dent or crater when you look from the side, though most owners cannot see this.
  • Light sensitivity - turning away from windows or lamps.

Discharge colour is a useful but unreliable clue. A clear watery eye with squinting is more typical of an ulcer than the thick yellow discharge of infection - see dog eye discharge colors for how the two differ. For a broader look at red eyes, read dog red eye causes.

The usual causes

  • Trauma. A cat scratch, a thorn, a stick, a grooming brush, or a dog head-shaking into something sharp.
  • Foreign material. Grass seed, grit, a hair or an eyelash trapped under the lid, continually scraping.
  • Dry eye. Poor tear production leaves the surface unprotected and ulcers become recurrent.
  • Entropion or eye lashes that turn inwards, common in flat-faced breeds and in heavy-faced dogs with loose skin.
  • Brachycephalic anatomy. Pugs, French bulldogs and Shih Tzus have prominent eyes that close less completely and dry out.
  • Chemical exposure. Shampoo, household cleaners, or a squirt of anything alkaline.

Why it cannot wait

An ulcer that is treated early usually heals in a week with drops and a cone. The same ulcer left for five days can:

  • Become infected and melt, losing corneal tissue by the hour.
  • Deepen to a descemetocele, where you can see a dark bulge and perforation is imminent.
  • Perforate, which is an emergency with a guarded prognosis for the eye.
  • Heal with a scar that permanently blunts vision.

There is also a diagnostic trap. Some ulcers are caused by a bacterial infection that gets worse with certain medication, and a vet needs to see the eye to tell which type you are dealing with.

What not to do

  • Do not use leftover steroid drops. Steroids suppress healing and can accelerate a melting ulcer. This is the single most dangerous thing an owner can do to an ulcerated eye.
  • Do not use human eye drops beyond a plain saline flush, and never a drop containing a vasoconstrictor to "clear the redness".
  • Do not put a contact lens or a patch on it yourself.
  • Do not delay because the eye looks a bit better this morning. Ulcers fluctuate, and a dog that is less painful may simply have a deeper, less sensitive lesion.

Routine eye cleaning wipes in our shop are fine for tear staining on a healthy eye, but they will not treat an ulcer and they are not a reason to postpone the appointment.

What the vet will do

A fluorescein stain confirms and maps the ulcer. Your vet may also measure tear production with a Schirmer test, check for a foreign body under the lids, and assess whether the cornea is melting. Typical treatment is antibiotic drops, pain relief, and a hard cone until the eye is healed. A deep or non-healing ulcer may need a referral for surgery such as a conjunctival graft. Our eye care guide covers the routine tear-stain and discharge care that sits outside this emergency.

Expect a recheck. Ulcers are re-stained to confirm healing, because a stain that still takes up dye means treatment continues.

When to see a vet: any squinting, tearing or red eye that lasts more than a few hours needs a same-day appointment - go immediately if the eye is cloudy, there is a visible dent, the pupil looks different from the other eye, or your dog is pawing at the face.

The bottom line

Treat any squinting, watery, red eye as an ulcer until a vet proves otherwise, and get a fluorescein stain the same day - put a cone on, never use steroid or human eye drops, and expect a recheck stain before you stop treatment.

FAQs

How do I know if my dog has an eye ulcer or just a red eye?

You cannot tell reliably at home, and that is the point. An ulcer usually causes squinting, heavy tearing and light sensitivity on top of redness, but conjunctivitis and glaucoma can look similar. Only a fluorescein stain shows whether the corneal surface is damaged, so a red eye with squinting should be stained the same day.

Can a dog eye ulcer heal on its own?

Very small superficial ulcers sometimes heal, but the risk of infection, melting or perforation makes waiting a poor bet. Untreated ulcers can lose corneal tissue within days and may leave a scar that affects vision. Treatment with antibiotic drops and a cone shortens healing and protects the eye while it closes.

How long does a corneal ulcer take to heal in dogs?

A simple superficial ulcer typically heals in five to seven days with treatment, and your vet will usually re-stain it to confirm. Deep, infected or non-healing ulcers take longer, sometimes weeks, and may need surgery such as a conjunctival graft. Healing time depends far more on depth and infection than on the size you can see.

Why does my dog need a cone for an eye ulcer?

Rubbing is the fastest way to turn a small ulcer into a ruptured eye, and it takes seconds. A hard Elizabethan collar prevents pawing and furniture rubbing while the surface heals. Soft fabric collars are not reliable for eye problems because a dog can still reach the face with a paw.
