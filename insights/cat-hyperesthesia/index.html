Cat Hyperesthesia: Why Your Cat's Back Ripples and Twitches

Calm & Behavior · 2026-10-19

Bottom line: Film a 30-second clip of an episode, book a vet visit to rule out fleas, anal sac pain, dental pain and spinal disease first, then reduce handling near the tail base and keep the daily routine predictable - most cats improve on that plan alone, and the ones who do not usually need medication rather than a stricter owner.

Feline hyperesthesia is a real condition, not a quirk, and the rippling skin along the spine, tail chasing and sudden biting are its signature signs. Start with a vet visit, because the lookalikes - fleas, anal sac pain, dental pain, spinal disease - are far more common and far easier to fix.

The word means heightened sensation. That is exactly what owners describe: a cat who suddenly cannot tolerate being touched near the tail, whose back rolls in visible waves, who bolts across the room mid-groom, or who turns and sinks teeth into the hand that was stroking her a second earlier.

It is often brushed off as a cat being odd. It is not. It is a recognised syndrome, it has a name in the veterinary literature, and it responds to treatment.

What an episode actually looks like

The classic picture is a cluster of signs, not one:

  • Rippling or rolling skin along the lower back, like something moving under the coat.
  • Tail chasing and biting at the tail base or flanks, sometimes hard enough to break the skin.
  • Dilated pupils and a fixed stare just before it starts.
  • Sudden sprinting, often with loud vocalising, as if something has bitten her.
  • Intolerance of touch on the back and hindquarters, which is the sign owners notice most.
  • Over-grooming in the same areas, leading to bald patches and sometimes raw skin.

Episodes are usually short - seconds to a couple of minutes - and the cat is normal in between. That on-off pattern is one of the reasons it gets missed in a ten-minute consultation.

What is going on under the skin

Nobody can point to a single cause, and anyone who tells you otherwise is oversimplifying. The current understanding is that it sits at the overlap of several systems:

  • Abnormal sensation processing. The skin and nerve endings along the spine seem to fire inappropriately, so ordinary touch registers as intense or unpleasant.
  • A muscle component. The rippling is visible myoclonus - brief involuntary muscle contractions.
  • A possible seizure-like element. Some cats respond to anti-seizure medication, which suggests a neurological component in at least part of the population.
  • A stress and conflict component. Episodes often cluster when the household changes: a new pet, a move, a new baby, disrupted routine.

Certain breeds - Siamese, Burmese, Abyssinian, Himalayan - appear over-represented, which hints at a genetic contribution. Age of onset is typically young adult to middle-aged.

The lookalikes that must be ruled out first

This is the part that matters most, because hyperesthesia is a diagnosis of exclusion. Several ordinary problems produce almost the same picture and are far more common.

  • Flea allergy. Intense itching over the tail base and back, plus biting at the flanks. One flea bite can drive it. Compare with cat flea allergy before anything else.
  • Anal sac disease. Scooting, licking under the tail, sudden turns to bite at the rear end.
  • Dental pain. A cat with a sore mouth often behaves oddly rather than showing an obvious mouth problem - see cat tooth resorption and cat stomatitis.
  • Spinal or disc pain. Reluctance to be picked up, a stiff walk, a flinch when the back is touched.
  • Skin infection or ringworm. Crusting, scaling and hair loss that itch - cat ringworm is the one to rule out in a multi-cat home.
  • Ear disease or mites, which can trigger head and neck twitching.
  • Hyperthyroidism and other metabolic disease in older cats, which can present with restlessness and irritability.

Most of these are cheap to check and quick to treat. Skipping them and jumping straight to a behaviour label is how cats end up living with an itch for a year.

What your vet will do

Expect a thorough physical exam first: skin, ears, teeth, anal sacs, and a careful feel along the spine. Then a flea control trial with a veterinary product, because you cannot rule out flea allergy by looking.

Depending on what turns up, the workup may include skin scrapes or cytology, blood work including thyroid and kidney values, and imaging of the spine if pain is suspected. In cats with clear neurological signs or no response to treatment, referral for advanced imaging is sometimes offered.

A short phone video of an episode is worth more than any description you can give. Owners often wait weeks for an appointment and then the cat is completely normal in the consulting room. Film it. Take it with you.

What helps at home

Home management is not about discipline. It is about lowering the sensory load.

1. Stop stroking the back and tail base. Most owners do this affectionately and unknowingly trigger episodes. Stroke the head and chin instead, and let the cat come to you.

2. Keep the routine boring. Same feeding times, same litter, same furniture layout. Predictability genuinely reduces episode frequency in stress-sensitive cats.

3. Give the cat somewhere to retreat to. A high shelf, a covered bed, a quiet room she can reach without passing the dog.

4. Enrich, do not console. Puzzle feeders, short play sessions with a wand toy, and vertical space redirect arousal into something useful.

5. Never punish the bite. The cat is reacting to a sensation, not being defiant. Punishment increases fear and makes the next episode more likely.

Do not bathe the cat to soothe the skin unless your vet suggests it. For most cats the handling involved costs more than the bath helps, and how to bathe a cat exists precisely because it is difficult.

When medication is the answer

If the lookalikes are cleared and the episodes continue, medication is reasonable and often effective. Options your vet may discuss include gabapentin for nerve-related pain, fluoxetine for anxiety and compulsive components, and in some cases anti-seizure medication. None of these are owner-selected - dosing in cats is unforgiving and several common human painkillers are fatal to them.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: book an appointment for any new rippling, tail chasing or biting episode - especially if your cat is breaking skin, has bald or raw patches, is off food, or is an older cat with a sudden change in behaviour, since pain and metabolic disease must be ruled out before this is called hyperesthesia.

The bottom line

Film a 30-second clip of an episode, book a vet visit to rule out fleas, anal sac pain, dental pain and spinal disease first, then reduce handling near the tail base and keep the daily routine predictable - most cats improve on that plan alone, and the ones who do not usually need medication rather than a stricter owner.

Sources: Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Feline Health Center; VCA Animal Hospitals - Feline Hyperesthesia Syndrome; AAHA - 2015 Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines.

FAQs

Is feline hyperesthesia the same as a seizure?

Not the same, though they overlap. Episodes involve brief involuntary muscle contractions and altered sensation rather than the full loss of consciousness typical of a generalised seizure. Some cats respond to anti-seizure medication, which is why vets treat the two as related rather than identical. Only a vet can tell them apart.

Will my cat grow out of it?

Some cats have long stretches with no episodes, particularly once triggers are removed and the household is stable. Others need ongoing management. It is not something that reliably disappears with age, and it is not caused by anything you did wrong as an owner.

Should I stop touching my cat altogether?

No. Keep normal affection but move it to the head, chin and cheeks, and let the cat initiate contact. Cats with hyperesthesia usually still want company - they just cannot tolerate the back and tail base being handled, especially without warning.

Can diet cause hyperesthesia?

There is no established dietary cause, and no diet has been shown to cure it. Food allergy can cause itching that looks similar, so a dietary trial is sometimes part of ruling things out, but it is not a treatment for hyperesthesia itself.
