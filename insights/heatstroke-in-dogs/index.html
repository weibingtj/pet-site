Heatstroke in Dogs: Signs, First Aid & When to Call a Vet

General · 2026-10-12

Bottom line: Heatstroke is a minutes-not-hours emergency: cool a hot dog with room-temperature water and airflow while you drive to a vet, and never use ice or wait to see if they improve. Dogs at highest risk are flat-faced breeds, thick-coated dogs, seniors, overweight dogs and anyone left in a car. Heatstroke is heat-driven, not a fever: a dog's body temperature climbs past roughly 104F (40C) because they cannot dump heat fast enough, and it can go from heavy panting to organ damage inside 30 minutes. If you suspect it, move your dog out of the heat, wet them with room-temperature water, and head for a vet while you keep cooling. Do not wait to see whether they perk up.

What heatstroke actually is

Dogs cool themselves mainly by panting, and that system has limits. When the air is hot and humid, panting moves far less heat, and core temperature climbs. Past about 104F the body starts losing the fight: blood is diverted to the skin, the gut lining becomes leaky, and clotting and organ function come under threat. Above roughly 106F the risk of permanent damage - kidney injury, abnormal clotting, brain injury - rises fast.

Two things separate this from a fever. A fever is the body deliberately raising its temperature to fight infection. Heatstroke is the body failing to lower it. And heatstroke kills quickly: many dogs that die do so within hours, sometimes before they reach a clinic.

Dogs most at risk

Any dog can overheat, but some are far more vulnerable:

  • Flat-faced breeds - Bulldogs, Pugs, French Bulldogs, Boston Terriers, Boxers, Shih Tzus. Their airways are built to struggle.
  • Thick double coats - Huskies, Malamutes, Chow Chows, Newfoundlands.
  • Overweight dogs, who trap more heat and work harder to move.
  • Very young and very old dogs, and any dog with heart or airway disease.
  • Dogs on certain medications, including some diuretics and heart drugs.
  • Any dog left in a parked car, even with the windows cracked and even on a mild day. A car interior can pass 100F within minutes when it is only 70F outside.

Early signs versus emergency signs

Stop and act on these:

  • Heavy, frantic panting that does not settle when the activity stops
  • Bright red gums and tongue, thick ropy drooling
  • Hunting for shade, lying flat, refusing to keep walking
  • Skin that feels hot to the touch
  • Slightly unsteady, or unusually quiet and withdrawn

Emergency - go now:

  • Wobbling, staggering or collapsing
  • Glassy eyes, confusion, not responding to their name
  • Vomiting or diarrhoea, especially bloody
  • Gums that look pale, grey or blue
  • Tremors, seizures or loss of consciousness

The first group is your window. Once a dog is ataxic or vomiting, they need a vet, not a longer rest in the shade.

First aid in the first ten minutes

1. Get out of the heat. Shade, air conditioning, or a car with the AC on full.

2. Wet the whole body with room-temperature or cool tap water. Concentrate on the neck, armpits, groin and belly, where big blood vessels sit close to the surface.

3. Get air moving. A fan, a car vent, an open window. Evaporation plus airflow is what actually pulls heat out.

4. Offer small sips of water if your dog is alert and swallowing normally. Never force water into the mouth.

5. Drive to the vet and keep cooling on the way. Wet towels plus airflow, not wet towels alone.

6. Take a temperature if you have a thermometer. Normal is 101 to 102.5F. Note the number and the time and tell the vet both.

What not to do

  • Do not use ice or ice-cold water. It constricts surface blood vessels and works against the cooling you are trying to achieve, and it adds a shock risk. Cool water is the target, not cold.
  • Do not wrap your dog in wet towels and leave them. A soaked towel with no airflow becomes an insulating blanket and traps heat in.
  • Do not pour alcohol on your dog or their pads. It is toxic and absorbed through the skin.
  • Do not give aspirin, paracetamol or any human medication. They do not help, and several are poisonous to dogs.
  • Do not "wait and see". Heat damage continues after the exposure has ended.

Preventing it in ordinary weather

  • Walk early or late, on grass and in shade, and shorten the route when humidity is high.
  • Carry water and offer it every 15 to 20 minutes on warm walks. A collapsible travel bowl from our shop makes that easy.
  • Test the pavement with the back of your hand for five seconds. If it is too hot for you, it will burn pads - our grooming, paws and ears guide covers hot-weather paw care and how to spot a burn.
  • Never push a flat-faced dog hard in the heat, even in the evening.
  • Never leave a dog in a car. Not for a two-minute shop, not with the windows down.
  • If your dog is limping or sore afterwards, our guide to the signs a dog is in pain helps you judge what needs a vet.

When to see a vet

Treat any suspected heatstroke as a same-day emergency: if your dog is panting uncontrollably, has bright red gums, is wobbly, vomiting, confused or collapsed, cool them with room-temperature water while you drive to the nearest vet - do not wait for them to improve on their own. Even a dog that seems to recover can have internal damage, abnormal clotting or kidney injury that only bloodwork will show, so a veterinary check is still needed afterwards. This article is general education and not veterinary advice, and it cannot diagnose your dog.

The bottom line

Heatstroke is a minutes-not-hours emergency: cool a hot dog with room-temperature water and moving air while you drive to a vet, and never use ice or wait to see if they improve. The dogs at highest risk are flat-faced breeds, thick-coated dogs, seniors, overweight dogs and anyone left in a car. After any episode, have bloodwork done - a dog that looks fine can still be carrying internal damage.

Sources: AVMA - Hot Weather Safety for Pets; VCA Animal Hospitals - Heatstroke in Dogs; AAHA - Canine and Feline Preventive Healthcare Guidelines.

FAQs

How hot does a dog have to get before it is heatstroke?

A normal dog runs about 101 to 102.5F. Heatstroke is generally defined as a temperature above roughly 104F (40C) caused by heat rather than infection, and the danger climbs steeply above 106F (41C). Because you cannot always tell the temperature by how the dog looks, the practical rule is simpler: heavy frantic panting plus wobbliness, red gums or confusion is an emergency regardless of the number.

Should I use ice or cold water to cool my dog down?

Use room-temperature or cool tap water, not ice. Ice and ice-cold water constrict the surface blood vessels that are trying to release heat, and they add a shock risk in a dog whose circulation is already stressed. Wet the dog thoroughly, especially the neck, armpits, groin and belly, and get air moving over them with a fan or car vent. Then drive to the vet.

My dog seemed fine after 20 minutes in the shade. Do I still need a vet?

Yes, at least a call. Heatstroke can cause delayed damage - abnormal clotting, kidney injury, gut damage - that does not show in behaviour on the day. A dog that cooled down quickly may still need bloodwork. Call your vet, describe the temperature, how long the exposure lasted and how the dog is acting, and follow their advice.

Can a dog get heatstroke just from sitting outside?

Yes. Heatstroke does not require exercise. A flat-faced dog in direct sun on a humid 85F day, a thick-coated dog on hot pavement, or any dog in a parked car can overheat with no activity at all. Humidity matters as much as temperature, because panting stops working efficiently when the air is already saturated.
