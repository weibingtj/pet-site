How to Clean Dog Paws After a Walk: A Simple Routine

Grooming, Paws & Ears · 2026-11-01

Bottom line: Rinse paws with lukewarm water, check between the toes, then dry completely after every walk, and get a vet to look at any paw your dog will not stop licking.

Rinse your dog's paws with lukewarm water after every walk, check between the toes, and dry them completely, and you prevent most of the paw problems owners end up at the clinic with. The rinse is the easy part. Getting the paw fully dry is the part people skip, and it is the part that matters.

Why the walk, not the bath, is the problem

A dog's paw is a sponge with fur on it. Every walk picks up whatever is on the ground: road salt and chemical de-icers in winter, grit and sand, pollen and cut grass in spring, pesticides on park edges, mud from the same puddle every morning, and grass seeds in late summer. None of that is a problem on the pavement. It becomes a problem when it sits between the toes for hours, warm and damp, under a layer of fur.

That trapped environment is what produces the classic cycle: irritation, then licking, then more irritation, then a red swollen web between the toes, then a vet visit for pododermatitis.

What you actually need

You do not need a grooming table. A practical kit fits in a basket by the door:

  • A towel or two, one for standing on and one for drying
  • A jug, small basin, or paw washer cup for lukewarm water
  • A mild pet shampoo for the occasional proper wash, not every walk
  • A comb or soft brush for burrs
  • Paw balm for dry, cracked pads, used sparingly
  • Treats, because a routine the dog cooperates with is a routine you will keep doing

The five minute routine

1. Catch it at the door. Teach a simple standing cue on a towel inside the entrance. Feet get wiped before they touch the carpet, every single time, so it becomes automatic rather than a decision.

2. Rinse with lukewarm water. Ten to twenty seconds per paw is plenty. Hot water stings and strips oils. Cold water in winter makes the whole exercise a wrestling match.

3. Work the gaps with your fingers. Open the paw, rinse the space between the pads and around the dewclaw. Do not scrub hard; you are flushing, not scouring.

4. Inspect while you are in there. Look for grit, seeds, burrs, splits in the pad, redness, swelling, or a nail that has caught. This ten second check is where most early problems get caught before they become limping.

5. Dry properly. Towel dry, then let the paw air for a few minutes. Get between every toe, top and underside. Damp fur between toes is the single biggest favor you can do for a yeast or bacterial infection.

6. Reward, then treat dryness if needed. Balm goes on clean, dry, cracked pads only, and gets worked in rather than left in a thick layer the dog will lick off.

Winter salt and de-icers

Rock salt burns. It also gets licked off, and some de-icers cause mouth irritation and stomach upset. On icy days, rinse within minutes of getting home rather than letting the dog lie down with salt packed between the toes. If your dog wears boots, still check the paws afterwards, because salt works its way in around the edges. For dogs with already dry or split pads, our guide to dry and cracked dog paws covers the repair side of things.

Grass seeds and the summer check

Late spring through early autumn is grass seed season. Foxtail type seeds work their way into the fur between the toes, then into the skin, and they do not dissolve or work themselves out. A dog that suddenly licks one paw intensely, holds it up, or has a small swelling between the toes after a walk in long grass needs that paw checked closely and, if you cannot find and remove the seed easily, seen by a vet. Seeds also travel up noses and into ears, so a dog sneezing in fits or shaking its head after a field walk is the same story.

Mud, ponds, and hot pavement

Mud is not dangerous, but it dries into a crust that cracks the skin when the dog flexes the paw. Rinse it off rather than brushing dried mud out. Pond water carries bacteria, so rinse and dry rather than letting the dog lick the paws clean. On hot days, the risk runs the other way: asphalt that is painful to a human hand at five seconds is painful to pads, and the routine should be a midday walk on grass instead.

Products worth using and worth skipping

  • Pet paw wipes are fine for light dust and for a quick clean when you are out. They are not enough for salt or chemical residue, which need water.
  • Human baby wipes often contain fragrance and preservatives. Occasional use is usually tolerated, daily use on a licking dog is not worth the risk.
  • Daily shampoo is too much. It strips the oils that keep pads supple. Water rinses most days, shampoo when the dog is genuinely dirty.
  • Boots are optional. They help with salt, hot pavement, and long exposure, but a dog that hates them will fight, and a fight every walk defeats the purpose.
  • Paw balm is for dryness. It is not a treatment for redness, swelling, or a dog that is licking constantly.

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit if your dog limps after a walk, licks one paw relentlessly, has swelling or a red lump between the toes, a split or bleeding pad, a torn or bleeding nail, or a paw that smells yeasty. Also seek care if you suspect a grass seed is embedded and you cannot see or remove it, since these can migrate deeper and need proper removal. Persistent paw licking is a symptom, not a habit, and it is often the first visible sign of allergies, infection, or pain elsewhere in the leg.

The bottom line

Rinse paws with lukewarm water, check between the toes, then dry completely after every walk, and get a vet to look at any paw your dog will not stop licking.

FAQs

Is it OK to use baby wipes on my dog's paws?

Occasionally, yes, and they are handy when you are out. But wipes do not remove road salt or chemical de-icers, which need a water rinse, and daily use of fragranced wipes on a dog that licks its paws adds avoidable irritation. Rinse with water and dry when you get home.

How often should I wash my dog's paws?

Rinse after every walk in winter or wet weather, and after any walk in long grass. A full shampoo wash once every three to four weeks is usually enough unless your dog is genuinely dirty or has a skin condition your vet is managing.

Do I have to dry between the toes?

Yes, and this is the step that matters most. Damp fur between the toes keeps the skin soft and warm, which is exactly what yeast and bacteria need. Towel dry, then let the paw air for a few minutes before the dog settles down.

Does my dog need boots in winter?

Not always. Boots help on salted pavements and very cold days, especially for small dogs, thin-coated breeds, and dogs with sensitive pads. If your dog will not wear them, a rinse and dry within minutes of coming inside covers most of the risk.
