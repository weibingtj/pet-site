Dog Gingivitis: Early Signs and Home Care That Works

Dental Care · 2026-10-16

Bottom line: Book a vet dental check to find out whether the problem is gingivitis or periodontitis, then brush the gum line with enzymatic paste at least three times a week and give an approved dental chew on the days you miss.

Dog gingivitis is red, swollen gum tissue at the tooth line, and it is reversible while the inflammation stays above the gum - which is why the habit you build this month matters more than the cleaning you book next year. Gums that bleed when you brush, smell stronger than a month ago or look angry red are the earliest reliable signal of dental disease in dogs. This is education, not a diagnosis, and it does not replace a vet exam.

What gingivitis actually means

Gingivitis is inflammation of the gingiva - the gum tissue that hugs each tooth. At this stage the problem lives in soft tissue, and soft tissue heals, so the redness can genuinely go back to normal.

Push the process deeper instead, below the gum line into the ligament and bone that hold the tooth, and it becomes periodontitis. That stage does not reverse. Vets can slow it and manage it, but attachment that is lost is gone. This is why the difference between a scale and polish and a mouth full of extractions usually comes down to how early somebody looked at the gum line.

Why dog gums get inflamed so fast

  • Plaque forms within hours. A soft film of bacteria builds on the teeth continuously, and left undisturbed it mineralises into tartar in roughly 24 to 72 hours. Tartar is rough, so it collects more plaque.
  • The gum line is the gap. The sulcus, the shallow groove between tooth and gum, is the one place chewing cannot clean properly.
  • Anatomy works against some dogs. Toy breeds, flat-faced breeds and dogs with crowded or rotated teeth trap debris where a toothbrush rarely reaches.
  • Diet and chewing matter less than people hope. Kibble does not scrub teeth. Only mechanical abrasion with bristles, or a tested chew, gets into the sulcus.
  • Some dogs are simply more reactive. The immune response to plaque varies between individuals, which is why two dogs with similar mouths can have very different gum health.

The early signs owners miss

Most dogs with early gingivitis eat completely normally, so owners wait for something dramatic. Look for the quiet signs instead:

  • A thin red line along the gum margin, especially on the upper canines and the back molars.
  • Bleeding on the toothbrush or on a chew toy, even a pink tinge.
  • Worse breath than last month when nothing in the diet changed.
  • Pawing at the mouth, teeth chattering, or hesitation over hard kibble.
  • Yellow-brown tartar at the gum line and teeth that look duller than they did.
  • Reluctance to let you lift the lip on one side in particular.

A little gingivitis is common. A lot of it, or a sudden change, is a different conversation.

A home routine that genuinely works

1. Start with a vet check. Before you build a habit you need to know whether you are dealing with gingivitis or periodontitis. They need different plans, and only a vet probing under the gum can tell you which.

2. Brush daily, or at least three times a week. Enzymatic dog toothpaste plus a soft brush is the only home method with solid evidence behind it. Human toothpaste is not safe for dogs.

3. Work the gum line, not just the crowns. Angle the bristles about 45 degrees into the sulcus and use small circles. That is where the plaque driving the inflammation sits.

4. Build tolerance slowly. If your dog will not tolerate a brush yet, start with a finger brush and paste offered as a treat, then a few seconds on one tooth, and grow it over two or three weeks.

5. Add an approved chew or water additive. Look for the Veterinary Oral Health Council seal on the packet, which means the product was tested rather than just marketed. Our guide to do dental chews work for dogs covers what the evidence actually supports.

6. Check the whole mouth weekly. Lift each lip, look at both sides, note any change in smell, and write the date down. Trends are far more useful than snapshots.

Consistency beats intensity. Five short sessions across the week do more than one long session on Sunday, and they are much easier to keep going.

What brushing cannot fix

Home care does not remove tartar, does not clean under the gum line and does not treat infection. Once there is significant tartar, deep pockets or bleeding that persists, you need a professional assessment with dental radiographs under anaesthetic. If you are starting from nothing, a soft dog toothbrush and enzymatic paste is the cheapest upgrade available, and the dental care guide walks through the rest of the toolkit.

Anaesthetic-free scaling deserves a straight answer: it is cosmetic. It polishes the visible crowns while leaving disease under the gum untouched, and it can make the mouth look better while the real problem quietly continues. For how often a proper clean is actually needed, see professional dental cleaning frequency.

When to see a vet

See a vet promptly if there is bleeding that does not settle after a normal brushing session, a broken or loose tooth, facial swelling, drooling, refusal to eat hard food, one-sided chewing or any sudden drop in appetite - and book a dental exam as soon as tartar is visible, because by then the disease is usually already below the gum line.

The bottom line

Book a vet dental check to find out whether the problem is gingivitis or periodontitis, then brush the gum line with enzymatic paste at least three times a week and give an approved dental chew on the days you miss.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; VCA Animal Hospitals - Gingivitis and Periodontal Disease in Dogs; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Dental Disease in Dogs.

FAQs

Can dog gingivitis go away on its own?

Early gingivitis can improve, but not by itself. It needs plaque removed regularly, and brushing daily or at least three times a week is the realistic minimum. Leave the plaque undisturbed again for a few days and the gums become inflamed again. If tartar is already visible, brushing alone will not clear it and a professional clean is needed.

How can I tell gingivitis from periodontitis at home?

You cannot tell reliably at home, which is exactly the point. Gingivitis is inflammation of the gum tissue, so you can see it. Periodontitis involves bone and ligament loss beneath the gum, which you cannot see. A vet needs a probe and dental radiographs. What you can track is the trend: worse breath, more bleeding, more tartar or reluctance to chew.

Is human toothpaste safe for dogs?

No. Human toothpaste usually contains fluoride and sometimes xylitol, both of which are toxic to dogs, and the foaming action is hard to control in a dog's mouth. Use a dog-specific enzymatic toothpaste, which is formulated to be swallowed in small amounts. Human mouthwash is also not safe.

How often do dogs need a professional dental clean?

There is no single schedule. Many dogs need one every 12 to 24 months, while small breeds with crowded teeth, or dogs that already have periodontal disease, may need an exam and assessment every six months. Your vet will set the interval based on what they find under the gum and on how good your home care is.
