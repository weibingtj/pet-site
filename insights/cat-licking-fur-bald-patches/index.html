Cat Overgrooming: Why Your Cat Licks Fur Bald and What Helps

Calm & Behavior · 2026-10-24

Bottom line: Assume fleas before stress, treat every pet for three months, and only call it psychogenic once a vet has excluded skin disease and pain - then fix the environment with play, routine and resource separation, and book a behavioural consult if the licking continues for more than six weeks.

A cat that licks its fur until bald patches appear - most often the belly, inner thighs or flanks - is overgrooming, and the first job is working out whether the skin itches or the mind is stressed, because flea allergy and anxiety can look identical from the sofa.

The good news: genuine stress-driven overgrooming (called psychogenic alopecia) is a real diagnosis, the fur almost always grows back once the cause is handled, and the path to figuring it out is orderly rather than guesswork.

What overgrooming looks like

Overgrooming leaves a signature. Because the cat is removing its own hair, the pattern is:

  • Symmetrical and tidy - both sides match, edges are neat, and the skin underneath usually looks normal: no rash, no scabs, no redness.
  • Stubble and short hairs, not open sores. Cats cannot pull hair out without cutting it, so a barbered look (like a bad haircut) is typical.
  • Predictable locations - the belly, inner thighs, front legs and flanks. A cat cannot reach the back of its neck or the top of its head, so hair loss there almost always has a different cause.

If the bald areas are inflamed, scabby or crusted, or the cat reacts to touch in those spots, skin disease is more likely than stress, and a vet should look before you blame the new sofa.

Step one: rule out the itchy causes

Here is the uncomfortable part: most overgrooming is itchy, not anxious. Surveys of overgrooming cats find far more skin causes than psychological ones, and fleas top the list - even in indoor-only cats, because fleas hitchhike on clothing, on other pets and through open windows.

Work through these in order:

1. Fleas. One flea bite is enough to make a flea-allergic cat chew its belly for weeks. Treat every dog and cat in the house with a vet-approved product, monthly, for at least three months before you judge the result. Cat flea allergy explains why one flea causes so much damage.

2. Other skin disease. Cat skin allergies to food or the environment, and fungal infection such as cat ringworm - which is contagious to humans - all cause licking and hair loss.

3. Pain. Cats sometimes overgroom the spot that hurts, for example a cat licking its belly over an inflamed bladder. If the grooming is fixed to one area and the vet cannot find skin disease, ask about pain.

Only after itchy and painful causes are excluded does the stress diagnosis earn its place.

Why stress makes cats overgroom

Grooming is how cats self-soothe. It releases endorphins, which is why a cat washes after a startle or before a nap. Push that soothing behaviour far enough and it becomes compulsive, the same way nail biting does in people. Common triggers include:

  • A new pet, baby or person in the home
  • Conflict between cats in a multi-cat household - often silent, with no fights, just staring and blockades at doorways
  • Moving house, renovation, or a change of routine
  • Boredom in indoor cats with nothing to hunt, climb or watch
  • Loss of a companion animal or family member

A telling detail: stress grooming usually happens when the cat is alone or when nothing else is happening, and the cat often stops if you interact with or startle it.

How to break the cycle

1. Get the vet exam first. Skin checks, a flea-comb examination and possibly a trial flea treatment come before any behavioural plan. Skipping this step is the most common mistake.

2. Kill the fleas for real. Every pet in the home, monthly, three months minimum, plus hot-washing the bedding.

3. Add hunting back into the day. Two or three short play sessions with a wand toy, each ending with a small food reward, and puzzle feeders in between. A tired hunting brain has less urge to self-soothe with grooming.

4. Fix multi-cat friction. Give each cat its own food bowl, water, litter box (one per cat plus one) and a vertical escape route. Separate resources by room, not just by corner.

5. Use calming support as a bridge, not the cure. Pheromone diffusers can lower background stress. They work alongside the changes above, not instead of them.

6. Never punish. Shouting or spraying teaches the cat to do the grooming where you cannot see it, which makes the problem harder to read, not better.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: before assuming stress - if bald patches appear, if the skin is red or scabby, if the cat is also scratching or shaking its head, or if there are sores or changes in appetite, thirst or litter box habits. And urgently if a cat suddenly grooms one spot and reacts when touched, which can point to pain rather than itch.

Will the fur grow back

Usually, yes. Once the trigger is removed - fleas cleared, conflict reduced, anxiety managed - regrowth typically starts within a month or two and fills in over several months. If the skin has been licked raw and infected, that needs treating before the follicles recover. Long-standing cases where the skin has thickened and darkened take longest, and some areas may keep a slightly different colour permanently.

The deeper point is that overgrooming is a signal, not the disease itself. A cat that is balding its belly is telling you something in its body or its environment is not working. Find that, and the coat usually sorts itself out. If the flicking and rippling of the skin, rather than bald patches, is the main sign you are seeing, that is a different condition - see cat hyperesthesia to compare. The bottom line

Assume fleas before stress, treat every pet for three months, and only call it psychogenic once a vet has excluded skin disease and pain - then fix the environment with play, routine and resource separation, and book a behavioural consult if the licking continues for more than six weeks.

FAQs

How can I tell if my cat is overgrooming from stress or fleas?

You often cannot tell by looking, which is why the safest route is to treat every pet in the home for fleas monthly for at least three months and see whether the pattern changes. Stress grooming tends to be symmetrical, limited to reachable areas, with normal-looking skin, and often stops when you interact with the cat. A vet visit with skin checks comes before any anxiety diagnosis.

Will the bald patches grow back?

In most cases, yes. Once the cause is controlled, regrowth usually starts within one to two months and the coat fills in over several months. Areas where skin was inflamed or infected may take longer and can keep a slightly different colour.

Should I put a cone on my cat to stop the licking?

Generally no, and not without vet advice. A cone stops the licking but not the reason for it, and it adds stress, which is itself a trigger for overgrooming. It is occasionally used for a short period to let raw skin heal while the underlying cause is treated.

Can indoor cats really get fleas?

Yes. Fleas ride in on clothing, on other pets and through open windows, and they breed in carpets and bedding. Indoor-only status is one of the most common reasons flea treatment gets skipped, and skipping it is the most common reason an overgrooming cat never improves.
