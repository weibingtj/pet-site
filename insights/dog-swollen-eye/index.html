Swollen Eye in Dogs: Causes and When It Is an Emergency

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-10-21

Bottom line: Cold-compress the eye for ten minutes at a time, fit a cone if he is rubbing it, and get him seen the same day if the eyeball itself looks larger, the eye is shut, or he is in pain - and never use human or leftover steroid drops, because an ulcer under a steroid can perforate.

A swollen eye in a dog is usually an allergy, a sting or an infection, but glaucoma and an abscess behind the eye look almost identical from the outside - so a dog whose eye is swollen shut, whose eyeball itself looks bigger, or who is plainly in pain needs to be seen the same day. A cold compress and a cone are safe while you wait. Human drops and old prescriptions are not.

Owners usually describe it as "his eye just puffed up overnight". That timing is a real clue. Sudden swelling with itching points one way, sudden swelling with pain and a bulging eye points somewhere much more serious.

Puffy lid, or swollen eyeball?

This is the first split a vet makes, and you can make a rough version of it at home by looking at the eye from the front and from the side.

  • Lid and skin puffy, eye still visible and normal size. That is periorbital swelling - the tissue around the eye. Allergy, sting, bite, infected eyelid gland, or conjunctivitis.
  • The eyeball itself looks larger, or sits further forward than usual. The globe is involved. Glaucoma, an abscess behind the eye, or trauma. This is urgent.
  • A red fleshy lump in the inner corner. That is a prolapsed tear gland, known as cherry eye, and it needs surgery rather than drops.
  • Swollen and squinting, with discharge. Infection or a corneal ulcer. Never put a steroid drop into a squinting eye.

One eye or two also matters. Both eyes puffy at once is almost always allergic or systemic. One eye is usually local - a sting, a scratch, a tooth root, a foreign body.

The causes, most common first

1. Insect sting or spider bite. A single puffy lid within an hour of a walk in long grass, sometimes with a small puncture mark on the lid edge.

2. Allergic reaction. Often both eyes, often with hives on the muzzle or ear flaps. Swelling that also brings vomiting, wobbliness, or noisy breathing is an emergency, not a wait-and-see.

3. Bacterial conjunctivitis. Thick yellow-green discharge, a red eye, and a dog rubbing his face on the carpet. Highly contagious between dogs in the same house.

4. Blepharitis. Inflamed lids with crusting at the lash line. Allergies, bacteria and Demodex mites can all cause it.

5. Tooth root abscess. The upper fourth premolar sits directly under the eye. A swollen cheek with a puffy lower lid, dribbling, or a dog who drops hard food is the classic picture.

6. Glaucoma. A hard, painful, enlarged eye with a wide pupil and a hazy cornea. Vision can be lost in days, which is why this one is treated as an emergency.

7. Retrobulbar abscess. Infection behind the eye pushes it forward. The dog may run a fever, resent having his mouth opened, and look genuinely unwell.

8. Trauma. A cat scratch, a stick, a car door, or a groomer's clipper nick.

Allergy swelling: safe first aid

If both eyes are puffy, the dog is otherwise bright, and there is no eye discharge, this is usually an allergic reaction to a sting, a bite, or something he pushed his face into.

  • Cold compress for ten minutes. A clean cloth wrung out in cold water, applied gently, reduces swelling and takes the itch down. Repeat every couple of hours. Never press on the eyeball itself.
  • Fit a cone if he is rubbing. A dog can turn a mild swelling into an ulcer in twenty minutes of face-rubbing on carpet.
  • Do not use human antihistamines unless a vet has given you a dose for this dog. Several common ones are unsafe in dogs, and the combination products contain decongestants that are worse.
  • Watch the airway. Hives spreading to the throat, noisy breathing, vomiting or collapse means an emergency clinic now, not in the morning.
  • Photograph it. A phone photo from the same angle every hour tells the vet far more than your memory of how it looked at 7am.

The two that get missed

Tooth root disease is the one owners never suspect. The eye looks puffy, the dog seems fine in himself, and the actual problem is an infected molar root a centimetre below the skin. It recurs until the tooth is treated, and antibiotics alone only buy time.

Glaucoma is the one that costs an eye. Signs worth acting on immediately: the eye feels harder than the other one when you compare them with the lids closed, the pupil is wider on the affected side, the surface looks steamy, and the dog is pressing his head into furniture or avoiding bright light. A swollen eye plus any of those is not a tomorrow problem.

Breeds that swell more easily

  • Flat-faced breeds - Pug, French Bulldog, Bulldog, Shih Tzu - have shallow eye sockets and prominent globes, so a minor knock produces dramatic swelling and a higher ulcer risk.
  • Cocker Spaniels and Springer Spaniels are over-represented in eyelid and tear gland problems.
  • Dogs with known allergies tend to swell fast and often, and a written plan from your vet beats improvising each time.

What not to do

  • No steroid drops before the eye is stained. If there is an ulcer under that lid, steroids can let the cornea melt.
  • No leftover drops from another pet or an old bottle. The drug may be wrong, expired, or both.
  • No salt water flushes with a syringe on a struggling dog. You can convert a scratch into a deep injury.
  • No warm compresses on a hot, bulging eye. Heat increases blood flow and pressure.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: same-day for any swollen eye, and immediately if the eyeball looks larger or harder than the other one, the eye is completely shut, the pupil is wide or unequal, the cornea looks cloudy, the dog is vomiting or breathing noisily, or he is pressing his head against walls - glaucoma and retrobulbar abscess can blind or kill within days, and a swollen cheek with a puffy lower lid usually means an infected tooth root that needs more than antibiotics.

The bottom line

Cold-compress the eye for ten minutes at a time, fit a cone if he is rubbing it, and get him seen the same day if the eyeball itself looks larger, the eye is shut, or he is in pain - and never use human or leftover steroid drops, because an ulcer under a steroid can perforate.

FAQs

Why did my dog's eye swell up suddenly?

The two fastest causes are an insect sting or bite and an allergic reaction. A sting usually affects one lid within an hour of being outdoors. An allergy often affects both eyes and may come with hives on the muzzle or ears. A tooth root abscess and glaucoma can also appear overnight, which is why a swollen eye is worth a vet visit rather than a wait-and-see.

Can I give my dog human antihistamines for a swollen eye?

Not without a vet's dose for your dog's weight. Some human antihistamines are unsafe in dogs, and combination cold products contain decongestants that cause serious problems. A cold compress, a cone and a same-day examination are the safe actions. If the swelling is spreading to the throat or the dog is vomiting, go straight to an emergency clinic.

How can I tell glaucoma from an allergy?

An allergy makes the lids puffy and itchy while the eye itself stays normal in size, and the dog rubs at it. Glaucoma makes the eyeball feel harder than the other one, the pupil wider, the cornea hazy, and the dog is in pain - often pressing his head into furniture or avoiding light. You cannot confirm it at home, but hardness, a wide pupil and pain mean emergency.

Will a swollen eye go away on its own?

Mild sting swelling often settles within 24 to 48 hours with a cold compress. Swelling that is getting worse after a few hours, that comes with discharge, squinting, a cloudy eye, or a dog who is quiet and off food, does not settle on its own and needs treatment. Never assume it will resolve if the eyeball looks abnormal.
