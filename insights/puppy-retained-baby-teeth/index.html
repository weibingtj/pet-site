Retained Baby Teeth in Puppies: When They Need Pulling

Dental Care · 2026-11-11

Bottom line: Have the mouth checked at the last puppy visit and book extraction for any retained tooth at six to seven months, combined with the neuter so there is one anaesthetic, then start brushing three times a week once the gum has healed.

Retained baby teeth are the most common dental finding in puppies under a year old, and one of the cheapest problems to fix. If a baby tooth is still in place once the adult tooth has pushed through beside it, it should come out, ideally at the same appointment as the spay or neuter.

What a retained deciduous tooth actually is

Puppies start with 28 baby teeth. By roughly six months, all 42 adult teeth should have replaced them. A retained tooth is simply a baby tooth that did not fall out on schedule. The adult tooth erupts beside it and you end up with two rows in the same socket, which owners usually notice as a double canine.

The most common offenders are the upper canines, the upper incisors and the premolars. Lower canines are less often retained, but they cause more bite damage when they are.

Why it matters more than it looks

A retained tooth is not just extra hardware. It creates a food and plaque trap between the two teeth, so tartar builds months earlier than it should. That means early gum inflammation in a dog that is still a puppy. It also nudges the adult tooth into the wrong position, and once the bite sets it tends to stay set.

In flat-faced breeds the crowding is worse because there is less jaw length to work with, so double canines can rub the roof of the mouth or the lip and cause sores. Left long enough, that becomes a painful, infected problem rather than a cosmetic one. More on the downstream damage is in our dental care guide.

The timeline that matters

Small breeds run late, large breeds run early. Toy and small breeds often hold baby teeth until six or seven months. Large and giant breeds are usually done by four to five months. That difference is why a Yorkie at seven months with double canines is unremarkable, while a Labrador at the same age with the same finding deserves a closer look.

The practical checkpoint is simple. At the last puppy vaccination visit, have the vet open the mouth and count. If a baby tooth is still solid and the adult tooth is already visible, book the extraction instead of waiting to see what happens.

What you should not do at home

  • Do not wiggle it. Loose baby teeth come out on their own; firm ones do not loosen from handling.
  • Do not tie floss or thread around it. This is an old trick that breaks roots.
  • Do not pull it. Retained teeth often have long, hooked or resorbed roots that you cannot see.
  • Do not rely on chews to do the job. Chewing helps shed a tooth that is already mobile. It will not remove one that is firmly attached.

Pulling a rooted tooth at home risks a broken root, damage to the adult tooth underneath, or a fragment left behind that turns into an abscess weeks later.

What removal actually involves

Extraction is a short procedure under general anaesthesia. The vet will usually take dental X-rays first, because retained teeth frequently have roots that do not match what you see above the gum. Most clinics will combine it with the neuter or spay so the puppy goes under once.

Recovery is quicker than owners expect. Mild soreness for 24 to 72 hours, soft food for a few days, no hard chews until the gum has closed, and most puppies are eating normally the next day. Soft food, a soft brush set and a little patience cover the whole recovery window.

Which breeds top the list

Toy breeds dominate: Yorkshire Terriers, Chihuahuas, Pomeranians, Maltese, Shih Tzu, Pugs, French Bulldogs, Boston Terriers and Poodles. Brachycephalic dogs are over-represented because their jaw-to-tooth ratio is tight. Cats get retained teeth too, usually the canines, and the rule is identical.

The cost of waiting

Waiting rarely saves money. A retained tooth left for a year usually means the adult tooth is now misaligned, the gum between the two teeth is chronically inflamed, and the extraction is harder because the roots have thickened or fused. The cheapest version of this procedure is the one done at six months.

When to see a vet

Book an exam as soon as you spot two teeth in one socket, a baby tooth that is still solid at six months, or bleeding from the gum beside a new adult tooth. Call sooner if you see swelling under the eye, a puppy dropping food while eating, or bad breath in a dog under a year old, since those point to infection or a fractured tooth rather than a simple retained tooth. Facial swelling is an urgent sign, not a wait-and-see sign.

The bottom line

Have the mouth checked at the last puppy visit and book extraction for any retained tooth at six to seven months, combined with the neuter so there is one anaesthetic, then start brushing three times a week once the gum has healed.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; AVMA - Dental Care for Pets; VCA Animal Hospitals - Retained Deciduous Teeth in Dogs.

FAQs

Do retained baby teeth always need to be pulled?

If the adult tooth has already erupted beside the baby tooth, yes. The pair traps plaque and misaligns the adult tooth. A baby tooth alone in its socket with no adult tooth behind it can sometimes be monitored, but that call needs an exam and usually an X-ray.

At what age should a puppy have lost all its baby teeth?

Most puppies have all 42 adult teeth by six to seven months. Toy breeds are often a few weeks behind large breeds, so a small breed at seven months can still be finishing the swap. Any baby tooth still firm at that point should be assessed.

Can I just wait and see if it falls out?

For a tooth that is already loose, waiting a couple of weeks is reasonable. For a tooth that is firm while the adult tooth is through, waiting usually makes the extraction harder and the bite worse. The window where this is a simple procedure is around six months.

Is extraction painful for the puppy?

The procedure is done under anaesthesia, so there is no pain during it. Afterwards expect mild soreness for one to three days, managed with the pain relief your vet prescribes. Most puppies eat soft food the same evening and behave normally by the next day.
