Pyoderma in Dogs: Signs of a Bacterial Skin Infection

Skin & Coat · 2026-10-29

Bottom line: Pyoderma is a bacterial skin infection that flares when something else - usually allergies, fleas or skin folds - has already broken the skin barrier, so treat the infection and find the trigger together; most cases clear with medicated washes and vet-prescribed therapy within weeks.

Pyoderma is a bacterial skin infection in dogs - literally "pus in the skin" - that appears as itchy red patches studded with small pustules, crusts and circular scabs. It is one of the most common skin reasons dogs end up at the clinic, and it almost always flares because something else damaged the skin barrier first, most often allergies, fleas or trapped moisture in skin folds.

How deep the infection goes matters

Vets grade pyoderma by depth, and it changes the treatment:

  • Surface pyoderma sits on the top layer - think interdigital moisture, fold dermatitis under the muzzle, or a moist hot-spot-like patch.
  • Superficial pyoderma lives in the hair follicles - this is the classic pustules-and-crusts picture on the belly, armpits and groin.
  • Deep pyoderma invades below the follicle: nodules, oozing tracts, real pain and sometimes fever. This one needs prompt veterinary care and often weeks of treatment.

Signs you can spot at home

  • Small red bumps or pimple-like pustules that rupture into yellow-brown crusts
  • Circular patches of scaling with a ring of crust (called an epidermal collarette)
  • Sudden intense scratching, licking or chewing at one region
  • Patchy hair loss where the fur has been chewed or rubbed away
  • An unpleasant smell from the skin, worse in folds or between the toes
  • Moist, weepy patches that spread over days rather than shrinking

The belly, armpits, groin and between the toes are the usual first sites, because that is where skin is thinnest and most exposed to irritants. Short-coated breeds sometimes show it more starkly than fluffy ones, but under a dense coat the crusts and smell are exactly the same - part the fur and check the skin itself rather than trusting the silhouette.

Why it keeps coming back

This is the part most owners miss: bacteria such as *Staphylococcus pseudintermedius* normally live on canine skin without trouble. Pyoderma happens when a trigger lets them overgrow. The usual suspects:

1. Allergies - environmental (pollen, dust mites) or food - causing chronic scratching and a compromised barrier

2. Fleas - even a few bites in a sensitive dog restart the whole cycle

3. Skin folds in bulldogs, pugs and shar-peis, where friction and moisture accumulate

4. Hormonal disease such as hypothyroidism or Cushing's, which thins the skin

5. Mites or other parasites damaging the follicle

6. Poor grooming or persistent dampness after swimming or bathing

Treat the infection without finding the trigger and it usually returns within weeks.

What actually helps

Do not pop pustules or pick crusts - you push bacteria deeper. The reliable sequence is:

  • Vet diagnosis first. Tape preps, cytology and sometimes culture confirm bacteria and rule out mites or fungal disease, which look similar.
  • Medicated bathing. Chlorhexidine-based shampoos, used two to three times weekly as directed, are the workhorse for superficial cases and reduce the need for long antibiotic courses.
  • Targeted antibiotics only if prescribed. Superficial pyoderma may not need oral antibiotics at all; deep infections do, and culture-guided choices matter because resistant bacteria are increasingly common.
  • Break the itch. Anti-inflammatory medication prescribed by your vet stops the scratch cycle that keeps re-infecting the skin.
  • Fix the environment. Dry skin folds thoroughly, keep nails short so scratching does less damage, and stay on flea prevention year-round. If you are unsure whether dryness or allergy is driving the scratching, our skin and coat guide walks through the common causes side by side.
  • Recheck the skin after it heals. A short follow-up lets your vet confirm the infection is fully gone and spot the first signs of relapse while it is still easy to reverse.

A moist, rapidly spreading patch is the one presentation that behaves like a hot spot and can worsen in hours, so do not wait those out.

When to see a vet

See a vet promptly for pustules, spreading crusts or any sore that oozes, smells or keeps enlarging. Go urgently if the skin is hot and painful, the dog is lethargic or off food, the infection keeps returning despite treatment, or your dog is on long-term medication for something else. Recurrent pyoderma deserves a workup for allergies or hormonal disease, not just another round of antibiotics.

Pyoderma is treatable, but the underlying trigger is what decides whether it stays away. Nothing here replaces an examination, and persistent or deep infections always need professional care - our notes on dog dry skin causes help you tell a simple dry-skin problem from an infection before the appointment.

The bottom line

Pyoderma is a bacterial skin infection that flares when something else - usually allergies, fleas or skin folds - has already broken the skin barrier, so treat the infection and find the trigger together; most cases clear with medicated washes and vet-prescribed therapy within weeks.

FAQs

Is pyoderma in dogs contagious to humans or other dogs?

Generally no. The bacteria involved normally live on dogs anyway, and pyoderma reflects a weakened skin barrier rather than a catchable infection. It is not considered contagious to people or to other pets, though basic hygiene - wash hands after treating sores - is always sensible.

How long does pyoderma take to clear up?

Superficial cases often improve visibly within one to two weeks of medicated bathing and prescribed treatment. Deep pyoderma can take six to eight weeks or longer. Finish the full course your vet prescribes even when the skin looks healed early, or relapse is very likely.

Can I treat my dog's pyoderma at home without a vet?

Medicated chlorhexidine shampoos can help mild, very localised irritation, but pustules, crusting or spreading patches need a diagnosis first - mites, fungal infections and autoimmune disease mimic pyoderma closely and need different treatment. Home care works best as the follow-through after a vet visit, not the first step.

Why does my dog keep getting pyoderma in the same spots?

Recurring infection in the same area usually points to a persistent local trigger: skin folds, trapped moisture between the toes, flea bites, or an allergy flaring each season. That pattern is worth a specific conversation with your vet about allergy testing or long-term skin-barrier management.
