Dog Mange: Sarcoptic vs Demodectic Mites Explained

Skin & Coat · 2026-11-14

Bottom line: Get a vet diagnosis before you buy anything: sarcoptic mange needs every dog in the house treated and the bedding washed hot, while demodectic mange in an adult dog needs a look at the immune system behind it. Prescription isoxazolines clear most cases in weeks — the failures are almost always stopped-early courses or untreated contact dogs.

Mange means a mite infestation, but the two types that matter behave nothing alike. Sarcoptic mange is contagious, ferociously itchy, and clears in weeks with the right drug. Demodectic mange is not contagious at all and usually points to something else going on in the immune system. Getting the name right decides whether you treat one dog or the entire household.

Two mites, two completely different problems

Both are tiny mites living on or in the skin, and both cause hair loss. Past that they diverge:

  • Sarcoptes scabiei burrows into the top layer of skin. Highly contagious between dogs, briefly contagious to people, and the itch is out of all proportion to what you can see.
  • Demodex species live harmlessly inside hair follicles in almost every dog. Problems start when they multiply out of control, which is an immune question rather than a hygiene one.

Confusing the two wastes weeks. A demodectic dog does not need the whole house disinfected; a sarcoptic dog absolutely does.

Sarcoptic mange: the itchy, contagious one

This is the mite owners describe as "he will not stop". Signs tend to appear on the ear margins first, then the elbows, hocks, chest and belly — places a dog can reach to scratch but that are easy to miss under fur:

  • Frantic scratching, biting and rubbing, often worse at night
  • Crusty, thickened ear tips and elbows
  • Red bumps and scabs on the belly and groin
  • Hair loss from self-trauma rather than from the mite itself
  • Restlessness, poor sleep, sometimes reduced appetite

People in the house often itch too — a red, bumpy rash on the forearms and waistband that fades on its own once the dog is treated. That family itch is a strong clue.

Here is the frustrating part: skin scrapings come back negative in a large share of real cases, because the mites are few and hard to find. Vets therefore frequently treat on suspicion, especially when the pattern and the itching fit and other dogs in contact are affected.

Demodectic mange: the immune-system one

Demodex mites are normal skin residents. Puppies pick them up from their mother in the first days of life, and in most dogs they cause nothing. Trouble shows up in two forms:

  • Localised — a few bald, scaly patches on the face, muzzle or front legs in a dog under 18 months. Often mild and sometimes settles without treatment, though vets usually still prescribe something to speed it along.
  • Generalised — widespread patches, thickening, redness and secondary infection. In an adult dog this is a signal to look for an underlying cause: thyroid disease, Cushing's, or anything suppressing immunity. Treating the skin without asking why is how cases drag on for a year.

Demodectic mange is not contagious to other dogs or to people, and it does not come from dirty bedding.

How vets confirm it

Expect a combination rather than a single test:

1. Deep skin scrapings from several sites — the classic method, squeezed until capillary bleeding.

2. Hair plucks or tape impressions, which catch mites that scraping misses.

3. Cytology to see whether bacteria or yeast have moved into the damaged skin. Secondary infection is common and needs its own treatment.

4. Bloodwork in adult-onset generalised cases, because the immune question matters more than the mites.

Treatment that actually works

Modern isoxazoline products changed this disease from a months-long ordeal into a fairly short course. Depending on your vet's choice, treatment may be an oral tablet given monthly, a topical spot-on, or a medicated bath protocol for the skin itself.

What determines success is rarely the drug and usually the routine:

  • Treat every dog in contact, not just the itchy one, for sarcoptic mange.
  • Finish the full course. The itch often stops before the mites are gone, and stopping early is the number one cause of relapse.
  • Wash bedding, blankets and car seat covers in hot water, and vacuum the dog's favourite spots.
  • Do not use a dog product on a cat. Some tick and mite ingredients are safe in dogs and dangerous in cats, so each animal needs a species-appropriate product from your vet.
  • Recheck as scheduled, because vets often re-scrape to confirm the mites have cleared.

For the wider picture on itching that is not mange, our skin and coat guide and this breakdown of why dogs get so itchy cover the other six usual suspects. Medicated shampoo, washable bedding covers and a cone for the scratching hours are in the shop.

What not to do

  • Skip the motor oil, bleach baths, kerosene and garlic. They do not kill mites, and several cause chemical burns on skin that is already damaged.
  • Do not assume a negative scraping means no mange. It happens constantly with sarcoptic cases.
  • Do not treat one dog and leave the others. That is how a household loops through the same infestation for months.
  • Do not stop when the coat looks better. Mite counts and appearance do not move at the same speed.

When to see a vet

Book a visit within a few days for any dog with spreading hair loss, crusty ear tips, or itching that keeps the household awake — and sooner if the skin is broken, weeping, smells yeasty, or your dog is losing weight. Rapidly spreading bald patches, open sores, or a dog that seems systemically unwell needs prompt care rather than another over-the-counter shampoo. Mange is treatable, but the prescription products that work are not the ones sold for general itching.

The bottom line

Get a vet diagnosis before you buy anything: sarcoptic mange needs every dog in the house treated and the bedding washed hot, while demodectic mange in an adult dog needs a look at the immune system behind it. Prescription isoxazolines clear most cases in weeks — the failures are almost always stopped-early courses or untreated contact dogs.

FAQs

Is dog mange contagious to humans?

Sarcoptic mange is, briefly. The mites prefer dogs and cannot complete their life cycle on people, so the rash usually fades on its own in a couple of weeks once the dog is treated. Demodectic mange is not contagious to people or to other dogs at all.

Can I treat mange at home without a vet?

You can manage the environment — hot-washing bedding, vacuuming, treating every contact dog — but the effective drugs are prescription. Over-the-counter shampoos and home remedies do not clear a mite infestation, and some cause chemical burns on already damaged skin.

How long does mange treatment take?

Sarcoptic cases often improve within two weeks and are usually rechecked at three to four weeks. Localised demodectic mange in a puppy can settle in four to eight weeks; generalised cases in an adult take longer because the underlying immune issue has to be addressed too.

Does my dog need to be isolated during treatment?

For sarcoptic mange, keep affected dogs away from dogs outside the household until your vet confirms clearance, and treat all in-home dogs at the same time. There is no need to isolate a dog with demodectic mange, since it does not spread.
