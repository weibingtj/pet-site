Cat Glaucoma Signs: The Quiet Eye Emergency Owners Miss

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-11-12

Bottom line: Feline glaucoma is a quiet, painful pressure problem that is usually secondary to inflammation inside the eye, so treat any cloudy, squinting or enlarged cat eye as a same-day veterinary visit, not a wait-and-see symptom.

Cat glaucoma is raised pressure inside the eye that damages the optic nerve and retina, and in cats it rarely looks like the dramatic red, tearing eye you see in dogs. The typical feline presentation is quiet: a slightly cloudy cornea, mild squinting, a pupil that responds oddly, or an eye that seems a little bigger than the other. That subtlety is the danger, because pressure damage is easy to miss and impossible to reverse once the nerve is gone.

One thing to settle up front: glaucoma is not something you manage at home, and it is not a normal aging change to monitor casually. It is urgent. A cat with a painful, cloudy or enlarged eye should be seen within a day, and a cat with a suddenly blind eye is a same-day emergency.

What is actually happening inside the eye

The eye produces a fluid called aqueous humor, which flows in at the back and drains out through a sieve-like tissue at the front. Glaucoma happens when outflow is blocked faster than production can balance, so pressure builds. Two patterns matter:

  • Primary glaucoma comes from an inherited drainage defect. It is uncommon in cats compared with dogs, and when it appears it is usually in breeds like Siamese and Burmese.
  • Secondary glaucoma is far more common in cats. Something else damages the drainage angle, and the usual culprit is chronic uveitis, which is inflammation inside the eye.

Feline glaucoma is therefore a symptom as much as a disease. The pressure problem and the underlying cause both need treatment, which is one reason home remedies have no role here.

Early signs owners actually notice

Because cats compensate so well, the signs are easy to dismiss:

  • A cloudy or blue-grey cornea, often mistaken for a cataract
  • Mild squinting or increased blinking in one eye
  • Uneven pupil size, or a pupil that stays large
  • One eye looking slightly larger than the other
  • A watery eye with no obvious injury behind it
  • Bumping into furniture, hesitating at stairs, or a sudden drop in confidence when jumping down, which suggests vision is already compromised
  • Sleeping more, hiding, or eating less, because chronic eye pain in cats usually shows up as behavior change, not pawing at the face

Some of these overlap with milder problems, such as the causes behind a squinting or watery cat eye, but cloudiness together with squinting deserves a pressure measurement, not guesswork. A useful habit: compare both eyes in a photo taken at the same moment under the same lighting. Subtle cloudiness and size differences jump out side by side in a way they never do in real time.

Why the underlying cause matters so much

In cats, the pressure problem usually rides on another disease. Chronic uveitis can come from feline herpesvirus, FIV, FIP, Toxoplasma or immune-mediated disease. Other drivers include lens luxation, untreated corneal ulcers, tumors inside the eye, and advanced untreated cataracts. This is why the vet workup is not only about pressure: your vet will want to know why, because lowering pressure while uveitis smolders protects nothing.

Older cats and cats with a history of eye inflammation deserve extra vigilance. A cat that has had one episode of uveitis should have its eyes checked at every wellness visit for life, even when both eyes currently look normal.

What the vet visit looks like

Expect three things:

1. Pressure measurement (tonometry), a quick test with numbing drops or no contact at all, performed on both eyes

2. A full eye exam with magnification: cornea, lens, retina, plus bloodwork or infectious disease testing when uveitis is suspected

3. A treatment plan built around the cause: pressure-lowering drops such as dorzolamide or latanoprost, anti-inflammatory or antiviral therapy when uveitis drives the problem, and surgery, including laser treatment or removal of the eye, for eyes that cannot be saved

That last option sounds brutal but is not. A blind, glaucomatous eye is a painful eye, and removing it is a comfort decision rather than a cosmetic failure. Most cats adjust remarkably well to one-eye or no-eye vision using hearing, whiskers and habit.

What you can do at home

There is no safe home treatment for eye pressure. What actually helps:

  • Never use leftover drops from another pet or your own medicine cabinet. The drop that lowers pressure in one condition can raise it in another, and human glaucoma drops are not dosed for cats.
  • Give prescribed medication exactly as directed, and if the eye is squinting so hard that you cannot get drops in, that itself is a call-the-vet sign. The walkthrough on how to give a cat eye drops makes the daily routine easier.
  • Photograph the eye weekly if your cat has chronic uveitis, so gradual change becomes visible instead of deniable.
  • Keep the face clean and dry around a watery eye, and build a fuller daily routine with our eye care guide, then book exams on the schedule your vet gives you rather than waiting for symptoms.

When to see a vet

Call your vet the same day if one eye is cloudy, squinting, tearing, enlarged, or the pupils suddenly look different from each other, and treat a suddenly blind or badly painful eye as an emergency. Any eye change in a cat with known FIV, a uveitis history or an active corneal ulcer also deserves a same-day look. Eye pressure and vision loss in cats move on a clock measured in hours to days, and delaying does not preserve options, it removes them.

The bottom line

Feline glaucoma is a quiet, painful pressure problem that is usually secondary to inflammation inside the eye, so treat any cloudy, squinting or enlarged cat eye as a same-day veterinary visit, not a wait-and-see symptom.

FAQs

Can a cat's vision recover from glaucoma?

Vision already lost to optic nerve damage does not come back. Treatment aims to protect the remaining sight and control pain. Eyes caught early can keep useful vision for years, which is exactly why subtle early signs matter more in cats than in dogs.

How fast does glaucoma damage a cat's eye?

An acute pressure spike can cause permanent damage within hours to a couple of days. Chronic secondary glaucoma in cats usually builds over weeks, which is why gradual cloudiness or a slowly enlarging eye still deserves a same-day appointment rather than watchful waiting.

Is glaucoma painful for cats?

Yes, even the quiet-looking version. People with glaucoma describe headache and deep eye ache, and cats express that as hiding, sleeping more, irritability or face sensitivity rather than crying. Pain relief is part of every glaucoma treatment plan.

Can a blind glaucomatous eye just be left alone?

No. A blind eye with uncontrolled glaucoma stays painful, sometimes badly so. Removal or other surgical options are comfort decisions, not cosmetic ones, and most cats recover quickly and return to normal behavior afterward.
