Cat Greasy Coat: Why the Fur Feels Oily and What Helps

Skin & Coat · 2026-11-10

Bottom line: Book a vet check for sudden greasiness or a greasy tail base, then bathe monthly with a degreasing shampoo and add an omega-3 supplement to the diet.

A coat that feels oily to the touch, clumps into spikes and leaves a waxy film on your hands is usually a sebum or grooming problem rather than a cat that needs a bath because it is dirty. Sudden greasiness, a greasy strip at the tail base, or grease paired with weight loss or increased appetite moves it from cosmetic to medical.

What a greasy coat is telling you

Cat skin produces sebum from sebaceous glands, and a small amount is normal. It waterproofs the coat and keeps the skin flexible. Too much, or sebum that is not distributed and removed by grooming, leaves the coat looking stringy, flat and slightly damp.

The distribution matters more than the amount. Grease at the tail base with hair loss, grease over the whole back, or grease concentrated on the chin and lower lip point to different causes, and that pattern is the most useful thing you can tell a vet.

The five common causes

  • Grooming failure. An overweight, arthritic or senior cat cannot reach the lower back and tail base. Sebum and dead hair accumulate exactly where the tongue cannot go.
  • Stud tail (supracaudal gland overactivity). A greasy, bald, sometimes blackhead-studded strip on the top of the tail base. Intact males are over-represented but neutered males and females get it too.
  • Primary seborrhoea. A scaling and greasiness disorder of keratinisation, often starting young and often breed-linked. It is manageable rather than curable.
  • Diet and fat quality. Rancid fats, poor-quality oils and sudden diet changes can shift coat condition, though diet is blamed far more often than it is guilty.
  • Underlying disease. Hyperthyroidism, diabetes, obesity and skin infection all change coat texture. Grease plus weight loss, huge appetite or increased drinking is a medical picture.

The tail base pattern has its own write-up in cat stud tail, and the difference between ordinary grease and that specific gland problem is worth knowing before you buy a shampoo.

Why overweight and senior cats top the list

Flexibility is the hidden variable in coat condition. A cat that cannot reach its own back within four seconds of twisting is not grooming that area, no matter how motivated it is. The fur there stays lank, sheds into the coat instead of being swallowed, and starts to mat at the skin.

That also means the greasy coat is often the first visible sign of weight gain, arthritis or dental pain. If your cat has stopped grooming the back but still washes its face, the mouth and the joints are both worth checking, because face washing uses the front legs and back grooming uses the spine.

Washing a greasy cat without making it worse

Greasy coats need degreasing, and degreasing needs surfactant, not volume. Dilute a pet shampoo formulated for oily coats, work it into the coat with a rubber curry brush rather than fingertips, and give it five minutes of contact time before rinsing. One thorough wash beats three quick ones.

  • Pre-dilute shampoo in a cup. Neat shampoo is hard to distribute and hard to rinse out.
  • Two rounds: the first lifts grease, the second actually cleans.
  • Rinse until the water runs clear, then rinse for another thirty seconds.
  • Towel dry and keep the cat warm. A damp cat that gets cold will not cooperate next time.
  • Bathe every three to four weeks at most. Weekly bathing strips the coat and triggers more sebum.

The handling sequence matters as much as the product, and the calm method in how to bathe a cat is worth reading before the first attempt. Never use dish soap as a shortcut. It strips the coat barrier, and the rebound grease is worse within a week.

What to do between baths

Daily brushing does most of the work. A rubber brush or a fine metal comb lifts loose hair and spreads sebum along the coat instead of letting it pool at the skin. Two minutes on the back and tail base, once a day, is enough.

For cats that cannot reach, a warm damp microfibre cloth wiped along the spine every second day removes surface grease without a full bath. Baby wipes are a poor substitute because the fragrance and preservatives irritate skin.

Omega-3 supplementation is the one dietary change with reasonable support behind it. Marine-source EPA and DHA at a pet-labelled dose can improve coat gloss over six to eight weeks. Flax is a weak substitute in cats because they convert plant omega-3 poorly. Do not add oil to the food of an overweight cat without adjusting calories.

The wider routine, including how often to brush by coat type, sits in the skin and coat guide.

When to see a vet

Book an exam if greasiness appeared over a few weeks rather than years, if the tail base is bald and blackhead-studded, or if grease comes with weight loss, a ravenous appetite, increased drinking, scratching or a musty smell. Those combinations point to thyroid disease, diabetes or a skin infection rather than a grooming gap. See a vet the same day if the skin under the grease is red, weeping, painful to touch or smells infected, and do not apply human medicated shampoo to a cat without veterinary direction, since some ingredients are toxic to cats. This article is general information, not a diagnosis.

The bottom line

Book a vet check for sudden greasiness or a greasy tail base, then bathe monthly with a degreasing shampoo and add an omega-3 supplement to the diet.

FAQs

Why is my cat's coat suddenly greasy?

A sudden change over weeks is more suspicious than a slow one over years. Common explanations are weight gain that stops the cat reaching its back, stud tail at the tail base, or an underlying condition such as hyperthyroidism or diabetes. Sudden grease deserves an exam rather than a new shampoo.

Can I use dish soap on a greasy cat?

No. Dish soap strips the coat barrier and the skin responds by producing more sebum, so the coat looks greasy again within a week. Use a pet shampoo formulated for oily coats, pre-diluted, with five minutes of contact time.

How often can I bathe a cat with an oily coat?

Every three to four weeks is the practical maximum for most cats. Weekly bathing makes greasiness worse by disrupting the skin barrier. Between baths, daily brushing and a damp microfibre wipe along the spine control surface grease.

Does diet cause a greasy coat in cats?

It can contribute, particularly with rancid or low-quality fats, but diet is blamed more often than it is responsible. The bigger dietary lever is omega-3 from marine sources, which improves coat condition over six to eight weeks at a pet-labelled dose.
