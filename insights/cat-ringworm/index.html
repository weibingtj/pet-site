Cat Ringworm: Signs, Treatment and How to Stop the Spread

Skin & Coat · 2026-10-17

Bottom line: Get a fungal culture before you buy any product, then treat the cat, every other pet in the house and the vacuum cleaner on the same schedule for at least four to six weeks - and do not stop when the fur grows back.

Ringworm in cats is a fungal skin infection, not a worm, and it is contagious to people and other pets - so any plan that only treats the cat will fail. Most feline cases are caused by Microsporum canis. It is very treatable, but it takes weeks rather than days, and stopping treatment early is the usual reason it comes back.

What ringworm actually is

A group of fungi called dermatophytes digest keratin, the protein in skin, hair and claw, and they live in the dead outer layer of it. Microsporum canis accounts for the majority of feline cases. Trichophyton mentagrophytes is the next most common and often arrives via rodents.

The classic ring shape is simply the active leading edge of the infection with a healing centre. It is not always present, and longhaired cats in particular often show scaling and patchiness instead of a neat circle.

Signs to look for

  • Circular patches of hair loss with a scaly or crusty border, most often on the head, ears, muzzle, forelegs and tail base.
  • Broken, stubby hairs and a coat that feels rough, tufted or patchy.
  • Reddish rings on pale skin, though in cats the scaling is usually more obvious than the redness.
  • Claw involvement - thickened, brittle claws, which is more common than owners expect.
  • No signs at all. Some cats, longhaired and Persian cats especially, are carriers with no visible lesions and still shed spores.

Hair loss has plenty of other causes, so it is worth reading cat hair loss alongside this. Over-grooming from itch usually produces symmetrical thinning along the flanks and belly; ringworm tends to be patchy, asymmetrical and scaly at the edge.

How it spreads

Spores are shed on hairs and skin flakes and land on bedding, brushes, carpets, furniture and human skin. They are tough, and they survive in a home for many months, which is why vacuuming matters as much as the medicine.

Cats pick it up from direct contact with an infected animal, from shared grooming tools, and sometimes from contaminated soil or from a carrier cat in a multi-cat household. Young, elderly and immunosuppressed cats are more susceptible, and so are cats under significant stress or on immunosuppressive drugs.

How vets confirm it

Ringworm looks like several other things, and guessing costs weeks.

  • Wood's lamp is a screening tool only. Some Microsporum canis strains fluoresce apple green, but plenty do not, so a negative lamp means very little.
  • Fungal culture is the standard. Hair plucks or a toothbrush sample are cultured, and results take one to three weeks.
  • PCR testing is faster and increasingly used, though a positive still needs clinical context because healthy carriers exist.

Treatment that actually clears it

Most cases need topical and systemic treatment together, and the combination is what shortens the course.

1. Oral antifungal prescribed by your vet, usually itraconazole or terbinafine, dosed to your cat's weight. Never use a human preparation or guess a dose; some are hard on the liver and need monitoring.

2. Topical therapy - medicated shampoo or lime sulphur dips, typically twice weekly. Lime sulphur smells awful and works well, and it stains, so plan for old towels.

3. Clip long coats if your vet advises it. It cuts spore shedding substantially and lets topical treatment reach the skin.

4. Treat every pet in the house, including the ones with no lesions, if your vet recommends it. An untreated carrier quietly reinfects the treated cat.

Treatment normally runs for at least four to six weeks and continues until repeat cultures are negative, not until the coat looks better. Stopping when the fur regrows is the most common mistake owners make.

Cleaning the environment

This is the part that owners underestimate, and it is where reinfection comes from.

  • Vacuum daily while treatment is running, and empty the canister outside.
  • Wash bedding, blankets and soft toys at 60C or hotter, or replace them.
  • Wipe hard surfaces with diluted household bleach, roughly one part bleach to ten parts water, or an accelerated hydrogen peroxide product. Bleach is not safe around cats while wet - keep them out of the room until surfaces are dry.
  • Bin anything porous you cannot clean: cheap brushes, cardboard scratchers, fabric tunnels.
  • Wash your hands after handling the cat, and keep it off your pillow until it is cleared.

A skin and coat guide covers the general grooming routine that keeps the coat and skin in better shape between episodes, and a simple grooming kit with a dedicated brush for the infected cat helps stop spores travelling between animals.

The human risk

Ringworm is zoonotic. People usually get an itchy ring-shaped patch on the forearm, neck or scalp, and it is easy to dismiss. Children, older adults, pregnant women and anyone immunosuppressed should limit close contact until the cat is cleared. Anyone who develops a new rash should see a doctor rather than reaching for a steroid cream, which makes fungal infections worse.

When to see a vet

See a vet as soon as you notice patchy hair loss with scaling, broken hairs or crusty lesions - and urgently if the skin is oozing or the cat is itchy and distressed, if lesions are spreading quickly, or if there are other cats or children in the house, because ringworm needs a confirmed diagnosis and weeks of consistent treatment rather than an over-the-counter shampoo.

The bottom line

Get a fungal culture before you buy any product, then treat the cat, every other pet in the house and the vacuum cleaner on the same schedule for at least four to six weeks - and do not stop when the fur grows back.

Sources: Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Feline Dermatophytosis; VCA Animal Hospitals - Ringworm in Cats; AAHA - Dermatology and Zoonotic Disease Guidance.

FAQs

Is cat ringworm contagious to humans?

Yes. Ringworm is zoonotic, so people in the household can catch it through direct contact or contaminated bedding and brushes. Most human cases are mild itchy ring-shaped patches, but children, older adults and immunosuppressed people are more vulnerable. Wash hands after handling the cat and keep it off beds and pillows until repeat cultures are negative.

How long does ringworm last in a cat?

Typically four to six weeks of treatment, and sometimes longer in longhaired cats or multi-cat homes. The cat is not considered clear until repeat fungal cultures come back negative, which is why vets re-test rather than judging by appearance. Stopping treatment when the fur regrows is the usual reason it returns.

Can I treat cat ringworm with a human antifungal cream?

No. Human creams are not formulated for a cat's skin or for licking, and topical treatment alone rarely clears a cat because the infection sits in the hair follicles. Cats usually need prescription oral medication plus medicated bathing or dipping, both dosed by your vet. Some human products are also toxic if ingested during grooming.

Do I need to treat other pets with no symptoms?

Often yes. Healthy-looking cats, especially longhaired ones, can be carriers that shed spores without lesions, and they will reinfect a treated cat. Your vet may recommend culturing or simply treating in-contact pets. This is one of the few situations where treating an apparently well animal is the right call.
