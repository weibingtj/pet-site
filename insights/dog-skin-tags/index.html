Skin Tags on Dogs: What They Are and When to Worry

Skin & Coat · 2026-11-13

Bottom line: Leave a classic soft, stalked skin tag alone and photograph it monthly; have a vet remove it only if it snags, bleeds or irritates, and treat any firm, fast-growing or ulcerated lump as a same-week vet visit, not a tag.

A skin tag on a dog is a small, soft, floppy flap of skin that usually shows up in middle-aged and senior dogs, and it is almost always harmless. The real job for an owner is telling a true skin tag apart from the lookalikes that are not harmless - warts, embedded ticks and the occasional skin tumor that dresses up like a tag.

What a skin tag actually is

A skin tag (vets call them acrochordons or fibrovascular papillomas) is an overgrowth of fibrous tissue hanging off the skin surface, usually on a narrow stalk. They are benign by nature, and they tend to appear where skin rubs skin or rubs against a collar or harness: the armpits, chest, groin, neck and sometimes the eyelids. Some dogs grow one or two; others sprout them steadily with age, especially breeds prone to loose or folded skin.

Nothing you did caused them. Skin tags are mostly an age-and-genetics story, not a hygiene failure, so switching shampoos or diets will not make existing ones disappear.

Skin tag, wart, tick or something else

Use these differences as your checklist:

  • Skin tag: soft, flesh-colored, floppy, attached by a narrow stalk, same shade as the surrounding skin or slightly darker, surface stays intact.
  • Wart (viral papilloma): rough, bumpy, cauliflower-like. In young dogs they cluster around the lips and mouth; in older dogs they are usually benign keratin growths on the body.
  • Tick: has legs. Look closely with a magnifier - a tick is attached and may wiggle, and it swells as it feeds. Ticks get mistaken for tags most often in floppy-eared breeds.
  • Tumor: firm, fixed to deeper tissue, growing, changing color, ulcerating or bleeding. Skin tags stay soft, small and boring.

If you are not sure which one you are looking at, do not act - photograph it and let a vet look.

Why removal should be a vet's job

It is tempting to snip a dangly tag at home, and you will find plenty of advice about floss-tying or home removal kits. Skip all of it. Three things go wrong:

1. Misdiagnosis. If that lump was actually a mast cell tumor or an infected tick site, home surgery turns a small problem into a big one.

2. Bleeding and infection. Tags have a blood supply in their stalk; uncontrolled cutting means pain, bleeding and a skin infection that takes weeks to clear.

3. No biopsy. Anything a vet removes can be sent to the lab; anything you remove goes in the bin.

Vet-side removal is quick and simple when it is needed: a snip under local anesthetic, cryotherapy (freezing), or surgical ligation for larger tags. Many vets remove them opportunistically during a routine visit or dental.

A monitoring routine that actually works

Most tags need no treatment at all, just watching. Once a month:

  • Photograph each lump next to a coin for scale, and keep the photos in one folder.
  • Note any that your dog scratches, chews or that catch on brushes and collars.
  • Check for the red flags: rapid growth, color change, bleeding, crusting, a base that suddenly goes wide and fixed instead of narrow and floppy.

A tag that snags on everything and keeps bleeding is a candidate for removal; a tag that sits quietly for years is not. The rest of the skin routine - brushing, bathing, watching for irritation - fits around those monthly checks, and our skin and coat guide covers it, while the shop carries soft brushes that make hands-on checks easier.

Can you prevent skin tags

Not really, and you mostly should not try. They come from age, genetics and ordinary skin friction, so there is no proven supplement, shampoo or diet that stops them. What you can influence is the friction part: a properly fitted harness instead of a tight collar, weight management so loose skin folds rub less, and quick treatment of any skin infection that inflames an area. Those steps reduce irritation around existing tags, which is what turns a quiet tag into a bleeding nuisance. The goal is not a tag-free dog - it is a dog whose tags never become a problem.,

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit if any lump is firm or fixed in place, grows quickly, bleeds, ulcerates, changes color, or sits somewhere your dog keeps scratching. A sudden crop of many new growths also deserves one look, and anything on an eyelid, lip or in a collar rub-zone should be checked rather than watched. This article is general guidance, not a diagnosis - only a vet can tell a tag from a tumor with confidence.

The bottom line

Leave a classic soft, stalked skin tag alone and photograph it monthly; have a vet remove it only if it snags, bleeds or irritates, and treat any firm, fast-growing or ulcerated lump as a same-week vet visit, not a tag.

Sources: VCA Animal Hospitals - Benign Skin Growths and Masses in Dogs; Merck Veterinary Manual - Tumors of the Skin in Dogs; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine.

FAQs

Do skin tags hurt dogs?

True skin tags are painless. A lump that hurts when touched, or one a dog keeps chewing at, is either irritated from snagging or is not a skin tag at all - both are reasons for a vet look.

What does a cancerous skin tag look like on a dog?

Cancer rarely imitates a classic tag. Warning differences are a firm or fixed base, fast growth, ulceration, bleeding that will not stop, or black or uneven color. Any of those means a vet visit this week, not watchful waiting.

Can I tie off my dog's skin tag with floss at home?

Do not. Without a diagnosis you may be ligating something worse than a tag, and even a true tag brings bleeding and infection risk. Vet removal under local anesthetic is quick, clean and lets the tissue be checked.

Why does my senior dog keep getting new skin tags?

Age, genetics and years of skin friction in areas like the armpits and groin. New ones are usually benign, but a sudden burst of many growths, or any that change, is worth showing to a vet once.
