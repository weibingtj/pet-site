How to Remove Tartar From a Dog's Teeth (What Works)

Dental Care · 2026-10-19

Bottom line: Once tartar is hard and mineralised, only a professional scaling under anaesthetic removes it properly - book that first, then brush daily with an enzyme paste and add a VOHC-accepted chew, because the brushing is what decides how long the clean mouth lasts.

Established tartar cannot be dissolved, brushed off or safely scraped away at home - it needs a professional scaling and polish under anaesthetic. What you can do at home is stop it forming and slow it coming back.

That answer disappoints people, so it is worth understanding why it is true. The distinction between plaque and tartar is the whole story.

Plaque and tartar are not the same thing

Plaque is a soft, sticky biofilm of bacteria that forms on teeth within hours of a meal. It is invisible, it wipes off, and it is the thing that causes gum disease.

Tartar (calculus) is plaque that has mineralised. Saliva minerals - calcium and phosphate - crystallise into the biofilm and within roughly 24 to 72 hours it becomes a hard, rough, yellow-brown deposit bonded to the enamel.

Once that has happened, the deposit is no longer a film sitting on the tooth. It is a mineral structure fused to it. You cannot brush it off, because brushing works on soft plaque. You cannot rinse it off. And no gel, spray or water additive sold to consumers dissolves it, regardless of what the packaging implies.

There is also a second problem: tartar is rough. Rough surfaces collect more plaque, so the deposit grows faster the longer it is left.

What home tartar removal kits actually do

Search for tartar removal and you will find stainless steel dental scalers sold for home use, and videos of people chipping deposits off a dog's teeth. A few things are worth knowing.

  • Scaling above the gum line is the easy half. Most of the disease sits below it, in the periodontal pocket, where no home tool can reach. The visible tartar is the part that looks bad; the part that hurts is hidden.
  • Improper scraping damages enamel. Amateur scaling leaves scratches, and scratched enamel collects plaque faster than smooth enamel. You can genuinely make the problem worse.
  • The tooth surface needs polishing afterwards. A professional clean ends with polishing precisely because scaling roughens the surface. Skip that and the tartar returns within weeks.
  • Dogs do not hold still for it. A wriggling dog and a sharp steel hook near the gum line is a good way to cause an injury.
  • It is painful if the gums are inflamed. Scraping inflamed gums without pain relief is not a kindness.

If a dog already has visible tartar, heavy deposits, or inflamed gums, home scaling is not a shortcut. It is a delay.

What actually removes tartar

A professional dental cleaning under general anaesthetic. That is the only reliable method, and the anaesthetic is not optional padding. It allows:

  • Full scaling of every tooth, including below the gum line.
  • Polishing to smooth the enamel again.
  • Probing of each periodontal pocket to measure attachment loss.
  • Dental X-rays, which reveal root disease, retained roots and bone loss that are invisible from above.
  • Extraction of teeth that cannot be saved, without the dog feeling it.
  • A proper examination of the whole mouth, including under the tongue and at the back.

The anaesthetic worry, answered

This is the objection that stops most owners, and it is a fair one. Ask these questions before booking:

1. Will there be pre-anaesthetic blood work? It screens liver and kidney function and is standard at good practices.

2. Is an intravenous catheter and fluids used? It supports blood pressure and speeds recovery.

3. Who monitors the patient, and with what? Expect dedicated monitoring of heart rate, breathing, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and temperature.

4. Is pain relief given before and after? Dental work is painful, and pre-emptive analgesia is good practice.

5. Will dental X-rays be taken? Without them, a large proportion of disease is missed.

6. What is the plan if a tooth needs to come out? Agree the approach in advance, including the cost range.

Modern anaesthesia in a healthy dog carries a low risk, and the risk of untreated periodontal disease is not zero either - chronic gum infection is associated with changes in the heart, kidneys and other organs. That trade-off is worth discussing openly with your vet rather than avoiding.

What genuinely slows tartar down

This is where home care earns its place, and it works best on a mouth that has just been cleaned.

  • Brush daily. Brushing is the single most effective home measure, because it removes plaque before it mineralises. The frequency matters more than the duration: daily beats a thorough weekly session. See how often to brush a dog's teeth and, for cats, how to brush cats' teeth.
  • Use an enzyme toothpaste made for pets. It helps break down plaque and, just as importantly, dogs accept the flavour. Never use human toothpaste - fluoride and xylitol are both a problem for dogs.
  • Choose chews with evidence behind them. Look for the VOHC (Veterinary Oral Health Council) accepted seal rather than a shape that looks abrasive. Our piece on whether dental chews work covers what the testing actually shows.
  • Consider a dental diet if your dog tolerates it. Some prescription diets are formulated and tested to reduce plaque and tartar.
  • Use a water additive only as a supplement, never as the main plan.
  • Start early with a young dog. A dog who has had his teeth touched daily since puppyhood will accept brushing for life.

If the gums are already red and bleeding, start with dog gingivitis home care and get a vet check rather than brushing harder.

The signs that mean it is already past home care

  • Visible yellow-brown deposits, especially along the upper back teeth.
  • Bad breath that returns quickly after brushing.
  • Red, swollen or receding gums, or a thin dark line where gum meets tooth.
  • Bleeding when you brush or when the dog chews.
  • Chewing on one side, dropping food, or reluctance to pick up hard toys.
  • Loose teeth or a tooth that looks rotated.

Any of these means the disease is active and tartar removal alone will not fix it. Dental care tools, brushes and VOHC chews are in our shop, and the dental care guide covers the full routine.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: book a dental examination as soon as you can see hard tartar, red or bleeding gums, loose teeth or persistent bad breath - and urgently if your dog is drooling, pawing at the mouth, refusing food, or has facial swelling, since those signs point to pain or infection that needs treatment now rather than a better brush.

The bottom line

Once tartar is hard and mineralised, only a professional scaling under anaesthetic removes it properly - book that first, then brush daily with an enzyme paste and add a VOHC-accepted chew, because the brushing is what decides how long the clean mouth lasts.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; AVMA - Dental Care for Pets; VCA Animal Hospitals - Periodontal Disease in Dogs.

FAQs

Can I remove tartar from my dog's teeth at home?

You can remove soft plaque with daily brushing, but not hardened tartar. Once the deposit has mineralised it is bonded to the enamel and will not dissolve or brush off. Home scalers scratch the enamel and cannot reach below the gum line, where the disease actually is. Professional scaling under anaesthetic is the only complete removal.

Do tartar-removal gels and sprays really work?

Some products help slow new plaque formation, and a few carry VOHC acceptance for that specific claim. None of them dissolve existing tartar. If a product claims to melt away established deposits, treat that claim with scepticism - no published evidence supports it.

How often does my dog need a professional dental clean?

It depends on the mouth, not the calendar. Small breeds and dogs with crowded teeth often need annual cleaning; some large-breed dogs go two to three years. Your vet should grade the mouth at every check-up. Daily brushing at home is what stretches the interval.

Is anaesthetic-free dental cleaning a good alternative?

No. It cleans only the visible crown, leaves the subgingival deposits that drive periodontal disease, cannot be polished properly, and often stresses the dog more than a sedated procedure. It is cosmetic. It is not a substitute for a proper clean with X-rays.
