How to Give Your Dog or Cat Eye Drops Without a Fight

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-10-15

Bottom line: Ask your vet to show you the technique once and write down the exact product name, then make every dose a two-person, treat-heavy, no-negotiation routine: warm the bottle, aim for the lower eyelid pocket rather than the eyeball, and never touch the tube tip to the eye. If your pet fights hard enough that you cannot land a full dose, say so at the next visit - a technique switch beats a wrestling match.

Eye drops only work if they actually land in the eye, and most owners get a fraction of the dose in - so the technique matters as much as the prescription. Warm the bottle, aim for the pocket of the lower lid instead of the eyeball, give a treat straight after, and never let the tube tip touch the eye. This guide covers the safe method for dogs and cats, how to handle the ones who hate it, and the mistakes that make drops stop working. It is educational only and does not replace your vet's instructions.

Why technique decides whether treatment works

An eye drop has to sit on the surface long enough to act. Miss the eye and the drug runs down the face; blink it straight out and the dose is gone in seconds. Studies of human eye drops suggest that a large share of patients never get a full dose in, and pets are harder still because they move.

Three things quietly wreck a dose:

  • A cold bottle. Cold drops sting, and a stinging drop triggers instant blinking and head-shaking.
  • Aiming at the centre of the eye. Your pet sees the tip coming and the reflex is to shut the eye.
  • A contaminated tip. Touching the tube to the eyelid or lashes transfers bacteria into the bottle, and the next dose puts it back in the eye.

Step-by-step for dogs and cats

1. Wash your hands and have the bottle, a treat and a tissue within reach before you touch your pet.

2. Warm the bottle in your hand or pocket for a minute. Body temperature drops are far better tolerated than fridge-cold ones.

3. Choose the position that suits your pet. Small dogs and most cats do well on a table at chest height with their body tucked against you. Large dogs are often easiest standing, with you behind or beside the head.

4. Steady the head with your non-dominant hand resting gently under the jaw. Do not grip the muzzle - that is what most pets resist.

5. Rest your dosing hand on your pet's head so your hand moves with them, not against them. This one habit removes most of the aiming problems.

6. Pull the lower eyelid down to make a small pocket, and squeeze the drop into that pocket. When your pet blinks, the drop spreads across the eye naturally.

7. Never touch the tip to the eye, lashes or skin.

8. Release, treat, praise. Immediately. The treat has to come within a second or two of the drop, every single time.

9. Wipe the bottle tip after use and close it. Store as directed - some drops must be refrigerated, and anything past its discard date should be replaced.

Two drops at once is a common instruction: wait 30 to 60 seconds between them so the first drop is not simply flushed out by the second.

If your pet fights it

Resistance is normal and it is not a sign you are doing it wrong. What helps:

  • Wrap a cat loosely in a towel with the front paws tucked in, legs free. Keep the session under ten seconds.
  • Get a second pair of hands for the first few days. One person holds and treats, one doses.
  • Practise without the bottle. For a week beforehand, handle the head, lift the lower lid and treat. The handling stops predicting a fight.
  • Do not chase. If your pet bolts, stop and try again two minutes later. A chase teaches them that the bottle means being hunted.
  • Ask about a different formulation. Gels, ointments and long-acting preparations exist, and they often suit a difficult pet better than a twice-daily drop.

Ointments blur vision for a few minutes, which is normal and not a reason to stop - just give the treat after, not before.

Common mistakes that make drops fail

  • Stopping as soon as it looks better. Eye surface problems often look resolved before they are healed, and stopping early is a common cause of relapse. Our guide to cherry eye in dogs shows how one structural problem behaves over time, and the same principle applies to surface disease - finish the course.
  • Sharing a bottle between pets or using a leftover bottle from a previous episode. Doses and diagnoses differ, and an old bottle may be past its discard date.
  • Storing it wrong. Some drops must stay in the fridge; others must not. Check the label rather than guessing.
  • Touching the tip to the eye, then continuing to use it.
  • Missing the second drug when two are prescribed, usually an antibiotic plus something to control inflammation. Both matter.

How to tell it is working

Owners often ask how fast they should see a change. A few broad patterns, with the caveat that they vary with the diagnosis:

  • Squinting and light sensitivity should improve within the first day or two. If it is not better at all after 24 hours, ring your vet.
  • Discharge should reduce over a few days. Our guide to dog eye discharge colours explains what the colour is telling you.
  • Redness fades over several days.
  • The surface heals on its own schedule, often longer than the visible signs suggest. That is exactly why you finish the course.

Keep every recheck appointment. Eye surface damage can progress silently under a closed lid, and a simple stain test at the vet can catch a scratch that looks like nothing at home.

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit the same day if your pet is squinting and cannot open the eye, has a cloudy or bluish eye, a pupil that looks different from the other side, sudden pain or pawing at the face, or any eye problem that is not clearly improving after 24 hours on drops - and treat a bulging eye, a deep corneal wound or an eye that has suddenly gone blind as an emergency.

The bottom line

Ask your vet to show you the technique once and write down the exact product name, then make every dose a two-person, treat-heavy, no-negotiation routine: warm the bottle, aim for the lower eyelid pocket rather than the eyeball, and never touch the tube tip to the eye. If your pet fights hard enough that you cannot land a full dose, say so at the next visit - a technique switch beats a wrestling match.

FAQs

How do I put eye drops in my dog's eyes without a struggle?

Rest your dosing hand on your dog's head so your hand moves with them, pull the lower eyelid down to create a small pocket, and squeeze the drop into the pocket rather than at the eyeball. Warm the bottle in your hand first, keep the session under ten seconds, and give a treat within a second of the drop. Steady the head under the jaw - gripping the muzzle is what most dogs resist.

Can I use human eye drops on my dog or cat?

No. Many human eye preparations contain ingredients that are unsuitable or unsafe for pets, and some - including preparations with certain preservatives or steroids - can worsen an eye problem that needs a different treatment. Only use drops prescribed for your pet, at the dose and frequency your vet specified.

What if I miss a dose of my pet's eye drops?

Give it as soon as you remember, unless it is nearly time for the next dose, in which case skip the missed one and carry on with the normal schedule. Never double up to catch up. If you are missing doses often because your pet resists, tell your vet - a gel, an ointment or a long-acting preparation may suit you better than a twice-daily drop.

How long until eye drops start working?

Squinting and light sensitivity usually improve within a day or two, discharge over a few days, and redness over several. The surface itself often takes longer to heal than the visible signs suggest, which is why you finish the course the vet prescribed. If there is no improvement at all after 24 hours, ring your vet rather than waiting.
