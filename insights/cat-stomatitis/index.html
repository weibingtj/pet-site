Cat Stomatitis: Why Your Cat's Mouth Hurts and What Helps

Dental Care · 2026-10-14

Bottom line: Book a vet exam as soon as you notice drooling, dropping food, refusing hard food or a scruffy coat, because feline stomatitis is a painful immune reaction to plaque rather than a cosmetic problem. The treatment ladder starts with proper pain relief and a full dental assessment under anaesthesia, and partial or full extraction genuinely transforms quality of life for many cats.

Feline stomatitis is a painful, immune-driven inflammation of the mouth that makes eating and grooming hurt, and it does not resolve with a soft diet alone - it needs a vet diagnosing and treating it early. Cats are experts at hiding mouth pain, so the first clues are usually subtle: dropping food, a ragged coat, much stronger breath or a sudden preference for wet food. This guide covers the signs, the causes vets consider and the treatment ladder that actually works. It is educational only and cannot diagnose your cat.

What stomatitis means

The full name vets often use is chronic gingivostomatitis. It means the gums, the lining of the cheeks and the tissue behind the last molars are persistently inflamed. It is not simple tartar build-up and it is not "a bit of gingivitis". The tissue is sore, swollen, bleeds easily and stays that way.

There are two broad patterns:

  • Alveolar stomatitis - concentrated where the tooth meets the gum, often in cats with significant dental disease.
  • Caudal stomatitis - fiery redness at the back of the mouth, sometimes with raised, almost cauliflower-like tissue.

Both are painful, and both usually get worse without treatment.

Why it happens

The exact trigger is still being studied, but the accepted picture is an over-reactive immune response to the bacterial plaque sitting on the teeth. A few factors make it far more likely:

  • Plaque and periodontal disease. Even a small amount of plaque can drive the reaction in a susceptible cat.
  • Viral status. Cats carrying feline calicivirus, FIV or FeLV have a much higher risk.
  • Genetics and breed. It is seen more often in some purebred lines.
  • Stress and immune state. Poorly controlled chronic disease or a heavy stress load can tip a cat over.

One thing is worth being clear about: this is not caused by a bad owner. Cats can simply react very severely to a modest amount of plaque.

Signs to watch for

Mouth pain in cats rarely looks like yelping. It looks like this:

  • Eating changes. Dropping food, chewing on one side, tilting the head while eating, a sudden preference for wet or softened food, or refusing hard treats.
  • Drooling. Often thick, and sometimes blood-tinged.
  • Bad breath that is noticeably stronger than usual.
  • Grooming changes. A scruffy, greasy or unkempt coat because grooming hurts.
  • Behaviour changes. Hissing when the face is touched, hiding, irritability, reduced play or weight loss.
  • Pawing at the mouth or teeth chattering, especially at mealtimes.

Any combination of these deserves an appointment. Cats rarely show how much mouth pain they are in until it is severe.

How vets diagnose it

  • A full oral examination, often under a brief anaesthetic, because the back of the mouth cannot be assessed properly on a conscious cat.
  • Dental X-rays, to find root disease, resorptive lesions and bone loss that are invisible above the gum.
  • A biopsy in some cases, to confirm the diagnosis and rule out tumours or other immune disease.
  • Bloodwork and viral testing, to check FIV, FeLV, kidney disease and diabetes, all of which change the plan.

The treatment ladder

Treatment is staged, starting with the least invasive option that actually controls pain:

1. Pain relief and anti-inflammatory medication prescribed by your vet - never human painkillers, which are toxic to cats.

2. A full dental clean and X-rays under anaesthesia, plus extraction of any diseased teeth.

3. Home dental care if your cat will tolerate it - see our dental care guide and how to brush your cat's teeth for a realistic weekly routine.

4. Extraction of most or all teeth in stubborn cases. This sounds drastic, but many cats with severe stomatitis are dramatically more comfortable afterwards and eat normally without teeth.

5. Immune-modulating therapy in selected cases, decided by your vet.

The key point is that pulling only the obviously rotten teeth often is not enough, because the immune reaction can persist around teeth that look healthy. That is why vets assess the whole mouth rather than one bad tooth.

Home care that helps

  • Soften food with warm water, or choose a pate texture while your cat is sore.
  • Warm the food slightly - cold food can be more uncomfortable on inflamed gums.
  • Use a shallow, wide bowl so the whiskers are not pressed.
  • Offer small, frequent meals rather than one large portion.
  • Pick gentle dental products from veterinary ranges such as those in our shop.
  • Never start human pain relief. Paracetamol, ibuprofen and aspirin are dangerous for cats.

When to see a vet

Drooling, dropping food, refusing hard food, much stronger breath, weight loss or any cat that flinches when you touch its face needs a vet appointment promptly - and a cat that stops eating entirely, cries while eating or has blood in the saliva should be seen the same day, because severe mouth pain and dehydration in cats can become an emergency quickly.

The bottom line

Book a vet exam as soon as you notice drooling, dropping food, refusing hard food or a scruffy coat, because feline stomatitis is a painful immune reaction to plaque rather than a cosmetic problem. The treatment ladder starts with proper pain relief and a full dental assessment under anaesthesia, and partial or full extraction genuinely transforms quality of life for many cats.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Feline Stomatitis; VCA Animal Hospitals - Gingivostomatitis in Cats.

FAQs

Does cat stomatitis go away on its own?

Usually no. Chronic gingivostomatitis is driven by an over-reactive immune response to plaque, so it tends to persist or worsen without treatment. Some cats get partial control from regular dental cleaning and home care, but many need extraction of affected teeth or immune-modulating therapy prescribed by a vet.

What are the first signs of stomatitis in cats?

The early signs are subtle: drooling, dropping food while eating, chewing on one side, refusing hard treats, much stronger breath, and a scruffy or greasy coat because grooming hurts. Some cats flinch or hiss when the face is touched. Cats hide mouth pain well, so any of these is worth a vet appointment.

Can I treat cat stomatitis at home?

You can make your cat more comfortable by softening food with warm water, warming it slightly, using a shallow wide bowl and offering small frequent meals. You cannot treat the disease at home. Human painkillers including paracetamol, ibuprofen and aspirin are toxic to cats, so pain relief has to come from your vet.

Is it cruel to remove all of a cat's teeth?

No - for cats with severe stomatitis it is often the kindest option. The pain comes from the inflamed tissue around the teeth, not the teeth themselves, and most cats eat wet and even dry food comfortably once their gums are no longer inflamed. Many owners report a dramatic improvement in mood and appetite afterwards.
