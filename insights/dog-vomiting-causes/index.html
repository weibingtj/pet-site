Dog Vomiting: Common Causes & When to Call the Vet

General · 2026-10-09

Most dog vomiting is a single episode triggered by something your dog ate or a sudden diet change, and it settles within 12 to 24 hours with rest, water and a bland meal. What matters is not the one pile on the carpet - it is the pattern around it: how often, what came up, and how your dog is behaving in between.

Vomiting versus regurgitation

Owners use the same word for two very different events, and the distinction changes the likely cause.

  • Vomiting is active. Your dog retches, heaves from the abdomen, drools, licks its lips, and looks nauseous before and after. The contents are usually digested food, yellow bile, or froth.
  • Regurgitation is passive. Food or water slides back out with no heaving, often minutes after eating, and looks almost unchanged - tube-shaped, undigested kibble.

Vomiting points at the stomach or the system behind it. Regurgitation points at the food pipe - often a dog who wolfs food down, or a problem in the oesophagus.

The most common causes

1. Dietary indiscretion. Bin raiding, fatty table scraps, a dead bird in the garden. This is the single most common cause in an otherwise healthy adult dog.

2. A sudden diet change. Swapping food overnight is a classic trigger. Transition over 7 to 10 days instead.

3. Eating too fast. Big meals swallowed whole come straight back up. Large and deep-chested breeds also need slow feeding to lower bloat risk.

4. Parasites. Roundworms and giardia are common in puppies and in dogs around other dogs.

5. Infection. Parvovirus in an unvaccinated puppy, or a bacterial gut infection, often with diarrhoea and lethargy.

6. A foreign body or obstruction. A sock, toy, bone fragment or corn cob can partially block the gut and cause repeated vomiting that does not stop.

7. Toxins. Chocolate, grapes and raisins, xylitol, antifreeze, human medication, and many houseplants.

8. Motion sickness. Very common in young dogs and usually predictable: it happens in the car, and only in the car.

9. Chronic disease. Pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease, kidney or liver disease, diabetes, gastric ulcer and some cancers all present with recurring vomiting.

  1. Bloat (GDV). An emergency. A distended, drum-like belly with unproductive retching - your dog tries to vomit and nothing comes up - needs an emergency vet immediately.

What to do in the first 12 hours

1. Stop the food, keep the water. Remove the bowl for 6 to 12 hours in a healthy adult dog, but never restrict water. Offer small amounts frequently so a thirsty dog does not gulp and re-trigger vomiting.

2. Keep things quiet. Skip the walk and the dog park. Rest and a calm room settle a nauseous stomach - if your dog is anxious, our calm and behavior guide has settling techniques that help.

3. Write down the pattern. Time, how much came up, what it looked like (food, yellow bile, foam, blood), and whether diarrhoea followed. A phone photo is genuinely useful at the appointment.

4. Offer water, then a small bland meal. Once 12 hours have passed with no vomiting, plain boiled chicken with white rice or a prescription bland diet, in small portions, four times a day.

5. Watch hydration. Gums should be pink and moist, and the skin on the back of the neck should spring back when gently lifted. See our guide to a dog not drinking water if your dog refuses fluids.

6. Note the pain. A hunched back, a tucked belly, or a dog who will not lie down often means abdominal pain - our 8 pain signs guide covers what to look for.

What never to do

  • Do not give human anti-nausea drugs or painkillers. Ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin and acetaminophen are toxic to dogs; some human anti-nausea medications are unsafe or mask a worsening problem.
  • Do not induce vomiting. If your dog swallowed something sharp, caustic or unknown, forcing it back up can do more damage than leaving it. Call a vet or a pet poison line.
  • Do not withhold water for hours. Dehydration turns a mild problem into a serious one, especially in small dogs and puppies.
  • Do not push more food in to "settle the stomach". Give the gut a rest.
  • Do not assume grass eating is a cure. It is common and usually harmless, but it is not a treatment.

When to see a vet

Call your vet the same day if your dog vomits more than three or four times in a day, if vomiting continues past 24 hours, if you see blood or something that looks like coffee grounds, if the belly is hard or swollen, if your dog retches without producing anything, or if there is vomiting plus diarrhoea, lethargy, or refusal of water. Puppies, seniors, pregnant dogs and dogs on medication move to the front of the queue - they dehydrate fast and a mild cause becomes serious quickly. Any suspected toxin, swallowed object, or known access to human medication is an emergency call, not a wait-and-see. This article is general education and not veterinary advice.

The bottom line

Most dog vomiting is a single episode caused by something your dog ate or a sudden diet change, and it settles within 12 to 24 hours with rest, water and a bland meal. Repeated vomiting, blood, a hard bloated belly, or a puppy who cannot keep water down is a same-day emergency - vomiting is a symptom with a cause, never a diagnosis on its own.

FAQs

How long should I wait before calling the vet about vomiting?

One isolated vomit in a bright, drinking, otherwise normal adult dog can be watched for a few hours. Three or more vomits in a day, anything lasting past 24 hours, or vomiting with lethargy, diarrhoea or refusal of water means calling your vet the same day rather than waiting.

My dog vomited once and then ate normally. Is that fine?

Usually yes. A single episode followed by normal appetite, energy and drinking is typically dietary indiscretion and settles without treatment. Watch for repeats over the next 12 hours and keep the meal small and bland for a day.

Should I feed my dog after it vomits?

Give the stomach a rest first - 6 to 12 hours without food in a healthy adult dog, with water always available. Then offer small, frequent bland meals. Puppies, small breeds and dogs on medication should be guided by your vet instead of fasted at home.

Is yellow foam in dog vomit serious?

Yellow or white foam is usually bile from an empty stomach, common in dogs who have gone a long time between meals. It is not automatically serious, but repeated bile vomiting, or bile with blood, pain or lethargy, needs a vet visit.
