Mouth Ulcers in Dogs: Causes, Care and When to Worry

Dental Care · 2026-10-29

Bottom line: A mouth ulcer in your dog is a symptom worth acting on quickly: book a vet exam within days, soften the food and never treat it with human gels, since the underlying cause decides whether this is routine dental care or something more serious.

A mouth ulcer in dogs is an open sore on the gums, tongue, cheek lining or palate that stays raw and painful instead of healing within a few days. The most common trigger is advanced periodontal disease, but burns, foreign bodies, kidney disease and immune conditions can all be behind one, so the ulcer itself is a symptom, not the whole diagnosis.

What a mouth ulcer actually looks like

You will rarely get a good look at first, because ulcerated tissue hurts. Watch for these clues instead:

  • A yellow-grey crater with a bright red rim on the gums, tongue or inner cheek
  • Heavy drooling, sometimes tinged pink with blood
  • Dropping kibble, chewing on one side, or eating more slowly than usual
  • Pawing at the mouth or flinching when touched near the muzzle
  • Breath that has turned noticeably worse over days
  • Reluctance to have the face handled, or hiding away more than normal
  • A sudden preference for soft food, or walking away mid-meal

If you can, lift the lip gently once a day to track whether a sore is shrinking or spreading - photos help your vet enormously, because ulcer appearance can change quickly between appointments.

The usual causes, roughly in order

1. Periodontal disease. Infected gums and bone erode, and the inside of the lip or gum ulcerates where it rubs against tartar-covered teeth. This is by far the most common culprit and it is very treatable.

2. Burns. A dog that chewed an electrical cord, licked a chemical cleaner or ate scalding food can ulcerate the tongue and palate. Cord burns can be far more dangerous internally than they look in the mouth.

3. Foreign bodies and trauma. A splintered bone fragment, a grass awn wedged in the gum, or a repeated self-bite during play can break the lining open.

4. Immune-mediated disease. Conditions such as pemphigus or eosinophilic ulceration are uncommon, but they keep recurring and typically need biopsy and long-term medication.

5. Systemic illness. Advanced kidney disease can produce uremic ulcers along the gum line and the side of the tongue, often with weight loss, increased thirst and vomiting. This combination is an emergency, not a dental issue.

6. Oral tumours. In dogs over roughly ten, any sore that refuses to heal deserves an early check for cancer - early biopsy matters.

Safe care while you arrange a vet visit

Keep things boring and soft for a few days:

  • Swap to wet food or softened kibble so chewing does not reopen the sore
  • Skip chews, dental sticks and any hard toys until the mouth has been examined
  • Rinse the water bowl twice daily; a clean bowl plus fresh water is genuinely helpful
  • Never use human mouthwash, teething gels or painkillers - products containing benzocaine or xylitol are dangerous for dogs, and human NSAIDs are toxic at any dose

Do not buy time with endless soft food, though. An ulcer that is quietly caused by kidney disease or an immune condition will keep worsening while it looks like a simple sore.

What treatment usually involves

Your vet will sedate or anaesthetise the dog for a proper look, because most of a painful mouth is invisible on a conscious exam. From there it depends on the cause: a professional dental cleaning with extractions of dead teeth for periodontal disease, removal of a foreign body, or a small biopsy if the sore has not healed after two weeks of appropriate care. Antibiotics are only prescribed when infection is confirmed, not as a default cover - our dental care guide explains how regular home brushing and professional cleaning reduce the odds of ever reaching this stage.

Most dogs eat comfortably again within days of the cause being treated, which is one more reason not to stretch a diagnosis out for weeks while the sore quietly persists.

When to see a vet

Book an appointment within a couple of days for any mouth sore that is visibly raw, spreading, or paired with drooling, blood or dropping food. Go the same day if your dog cannot close its mouth, is pawing continuously, has eaten something hot or caustic, is vomiting alongside the sore, or is a senior dog with a sore that will not heal. Mouth pain escalates fast and dogs hide it well.

Mouth ulcers are one of those problems where home care supports treatment but never replaces diagnosis. Nothing in this article is a substitute for an examination, especially when eating or breathing is affected - see our notes on dental disease warning signs to check the rest of the mouth while you wait.

The bottom line

A mouth ulcer in your dog is a symptom worth acting on quickly: book a vet exam within days, soften the food and never treat it with human gels, since the underlying cause decides whether this is routine dental care or something more serious.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; VCA Animal Hospitals - Oral Ulceration in Dogs; Merck Veterinary Manual - Disorders of the Mouth in Dogs.

FAQs

Can a dog's mouth ulcer heal on its own?

Minor irritation can, but a true ulcer that lasts more than two or three days usually has an underlying cause - dental disease, a burn, or something systemic - that will not resolve by itself. If the sore is still visible after a few days, or your dog is eating differently, get it examined rather than waiting.

Are mouth ulcers in dogs contagious to other dogs or humans?

The ulcers themselves are not contagious. The rare exceptions are infections that can produce oral lesions, so if several pets in the household develop sores at once, mention it to your vet - that pattern points to an infectious or shared-environment cause rather than routine dental disease.

What can I feed a dog with a mouth ulcer?

Soft, room-temperature food: wet food, kibble soaked in warm water, or a vet-approved bland option. Avoid crunchy treats, chews and anything acidic or salty. Appetite normally bounces back quickly once the underlying problem is treated, so persistent food refusal is a reason to go back to the clinic.

How are mouth ulcers in dogs treated?

Treatment targets the cause: professional dental cleaning and extraction of diseased teeth for periodontal disease, removal of foreign bodies, biopsy and immune medication for recurring sores, and bloodwork when kidney or systemic disease is suspected. Vets may also use a protective oral gel short-term to reduce pain while the mouth heals.
