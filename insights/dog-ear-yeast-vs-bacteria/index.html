Dog Ear Infection: Yeast vs Bacteria and How Vets Tell

Grooming, Paws & Ears · 2026-11-16

Bottom line: You cannot tell yeast from bacteria by smell alone, so treat recurring head shaking as a reason for a cytology swab rather than another round of leftover drops. Clean gently with a vet-matched product, finish the full course, and look for underlying allergies if the ears keep flaring.

Both yeast and bacteria make a dog's ear red, sore, itchy, and smelly, and you cannot reliably tell them apart from the outside. The difference matters because the medications are different, and using the wrong one for two weeks is how a mild ear problem turns into a chronic one.

Why the two look so alike

A dog's ear canal is a warm, dark, narrow tube, which makes it an excellent place for organisms to grow. When the environment inside changes, whether from allergies, moisture, or a wax plug, several organisms can take advantage at the same time.

  • Yeast, usually Malassezia, tends to produce a musty, sweet, or bread-like smell with thick brown or black discharge.
  • Bacteria, often Staphylococcus or Pseudomonas, more often produce a sharper, foul or rotten smell, with yellow or green pus and more obvious pain.
  • Mixed infections are common, which is one reason guessing at home fails so often.

Those are tendencies, not rules. A long-standing yeast ear can smell genuinely awful, and a low-grade bacterial ear can smell like almost nothing at all.

The clues worth paying attention to

1. Which ear is affected. Yeast overgrowth often shows up in both ears at once, while one hot, painful ear points more toward bacteria, a foreign body, or a problem in the middle ear.

2. The discharge color. Brown and crumbly leans yeast. Yellow or green and wet leans bacteria.

3. The smell. Musty and bready versus sharp and rotten.

4. The reaction to touch. A dog that suddenly holds the head sideways or yelps when the ear is lifted has pain deeper in the canal, not just surface itch.

Even with all four clues, the only way to know is cytology. Your vet swabs the ear, stains the sample, and looks at it under a microscope. That takes a couple of minutes and it changes the treatment plan.

Why guessing with leftover drops backfires

Many owners keep an old bottle of ear drops and reach for it when the head shaking returns. There are three problems with that.

1. The old infection may have been yeast while the new one is bacterial, so the drug does not match the target.

2. Some antibiotics and antifungals can damage the eardrum if it is already torn, and you cannot see the eardrum from the outside.

3. Partially treated infections come back stronger and can become resistant, which is especially true of Pseudomonas.

If the ear has been treated more than twice in six months, ask your vet about underlying allergies rather than reaching for another bottle. In dogs, recurring ear infections are usually a symptom of skin disease, not an ear problem in isolation.

Cleaning without making it worse

Cleaning has a role, but it is supportive, not curative. It removes debris, cuts down the food supply for yeast, and lets the medication reach the canal wall, but it will not clear an infection that needs a prescription.

  • Use a vet-recommended ear cleaner matched to what is actually growing in the ear. You can compare options in our shop.
  • Never use cotton swabs down the canal. They push debris and wax deeper toward the eardrum.
  • Fill the canal, massage the base of the ear for a few seconds, let your dog shake, then wipe the visible folds with a cotton pad.
  • Do not deep-clean a very painful ear at home. If the ear is swollen shut or your dog resists being touched, let the vet handle it.

If your dog is being treated for an infection, finish the full course even after the ear looks normal, and keep the follow-up appointment so your vet can confirm the infection has cleared. What this is not

This is general information, not a diagnosis for your dog. Ear disease is one of the most common reasons dogs are brought to the vet, and it is also one of the easiest to mismanage at home. Only a veterinarian can look at the eardrum, take a sample, and prescribe the right medication.

When to see a vet

See a vet promptly for any ear that is painful to touch, swollen, filled with pus, or producing a foul smell. Book the same day if your dog holds the head tilted, walks in circles, loses balance, vomits, or shows facial drooping, since those signs suggest the infection has reached the middle or inner ear.

The bottom line

You cannot tell yeast from bacteria by smell alone, so treat recurring head shaking as a reason for a cytology swab rather than another round of leftover drops. Clean gently with a vet-matched product, finish the full course, and look for underlying allergies if the ears keep flaring.

FAQs

Can I use human ear drops on my dog?

No. Human ear preparations are not formulated for a dog's L-shaped ear canal, and some ingredients are toxic or damaging if the eardrum is torn. Use only products your vet recommends.

How long does a dog ear infection take to clear?

Most uncomplicated infections improve within three to seven days of the correct treatment, but the full course usually runs one to two weeks. Stopping early is the most common reason ears flare again.

Why does my dog keep getting ear infections?

Recurring ear infections almost always have an underlying cause, most often allergies, plus anatomy such as narrow canals or heavy ear flaps in breeds like Cocker Spaniels and Basset Hounds.

Is it safe to clean my dog's ears every day?

Daily cleaning is not routine care and can irritate a healthy canal. Clean when your vet recommends it, usually during an active infection, and otherwise keep the outer folds dry and check them weekly.
