Cat Scratching Furniture: How to Redirect Without Punishment

Calm & Behavior · 2026-10-13

Bottom line: Scratching is normal cat behaviour, not revenge: it sheds the dead outer layer of the claw, marks territory with scent glands in the paws, and stretches the spine, so the goal is redirection rather than punishment. Scratching is not misbehaviour. It sheds the dead outer husk of the claw, marks territory with scent glands in the paw pads, and stretches the muscles along the spine - which makes it a need, not a habit, so the goal is redirection rather than punishment. Put a tall, stable scratcher directly next to the piece of furniture being damaged, make that surface more appealing than the sofa, and trim the claw tips every two to three weeks. Declawing is never the answer.

Why cats scratch

Understanding the three functions explained above makes the whole problem easier to solve.

  • Claw maintenance. The outer shell of each claw sheds in layers, and dragging it through a rough surface pulls it off. Indoor cats have no tree bark, so furniture becomes the substitute.
  • Territory marking. Scent glands sit between the paw pads, and a scratched surface carries both a visual mark and a smell that says "this is mine". This is why cats scratch the corner of the sofa facing the room, not the back of a closet.
  • Stretching and exercise. A good stretch works the shoulders and back and is part of a cat's daily routine.

Once you accept all three, the plan becomes obvious: give your cat somewhere better to do it.

What makes a scratcher actually get used

Most bought scratchers fail for the same three reasons: they are too short, they wobble, and they are in the wrong place.

  • Height. It has to let your cat stretch fully, with the front paws above the head. Look for at least 30 inches (75 cm) of usable surface, and taller for a large cat.
  • Stability. If it tips or slides, the cat will not trust it. Fix it to the wall, weight the base, or choose a floor-to-ceiling post.
  • Material. Sisal rope, corrugated cardboard and untreated wood all work. Carpet is a poor choice because it teaches the cat that carpet is good to scratch.
  • Orientation. Some cats want vertical, some want horizontal, and many want one of each. Offer both before deciding which your cat prefers.
  • Placement. Right next to the damaged furniture, and near a sleeping spot or a doorway, because scratching happens where the cat already spends time.

A two-week redirection plan

1. Protect, do not punish. Cover the damaged corner with a throw, or press double-sided tape over the spot for a fortnight. The goal is to break the habit loop, not to scare the cat.

2. Move a tall scratcher in front of it. Literally place it against the sofa arm. The cat will scratch where it already wanted to; now the correct surface is the nearest option.

3. Make it irresistible. Rub the new post with a little catnip or silvervine, and play a wand toy up and down the surface so your cat grabs it with both front paws.

4. Reward the moment it happens. A treat or a calm "good" within two seconds is worth more than any correction later.

5. Trim the claw tips. Short tips do far less damage, and trimming removes the sharpest hook. Every two to three weeks is plenty; a groomer or vet can show you the technique on the first visit.

6. Never use a water spray, a shout or a tap on the nose. Punishment teaches a cat to scratch when you are not looking, and it damages the relationship you need for the rest of the training to work.

When scratching suddenly increases

A new burst of scratching, especially on vertical surfaces at cat height or on door frames, is often a stress signal rather than a furniture preference. Think about what changed in the last month:

  • A new cat, dog, baby or housemate, or a change in the household routine.
  • A move, new flooring, or furniture rearranged so the old territory marks are gone.
  • Too little stimulation. Boredom shows up as scratching, over-grooming and night activity. Two short play sessions a day plus a window perch often settle it.
  • Not enough vertical space. Cats that cannot get up high feel exposed and mark more. Cat trees and wall shelves give a cat somewhere to own.

Feline pheromone diffusers can take the edge off a stressful period, and they are worth a try alongside the behaviour changes. If the scratching is happening on the cat's own body rather than on furniture, that is a medical question, not a behaviour one - see below.

Scratching posts, nail caps and the declaw question

Soft nail caps glued over the claws are a reasonable short-term option for a specific problem, such as a cat scratching a person with thin skin, but they need replacing every four to six weeks and do not address the underlying need. Frequent trimming plus good scratching surfaces usually does more. Declawing - an onychectomy - removes the last bone of each toe along with the tendon and claw. It is a partial amputation, it can cause long-term pain and lame behaviour, and it is banned or restricted in much of Europe, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Israel and several Canadian provinces. The American Veterinary Medical Association opposes it unless it is a last resort for a medical problem.

When to see a vet

See your vet if the scratching is aimed at your cat's own skin, ears or face rather than furniture, since that usually points to fleas, ear mites, a skin allergy or a painful claw rather than a behaviour problem. Book an appointment as well for a sudden change in behaviour in an older cat, for over-grooming that leaves bald patches or sore skin, for a claw that is split, ingrown or bleeding, or for any cat that stops using the litter box or hides constantly. A cat that is hurting or itchy cannot be trained out of it, and this article is general education rather than veterinary advice.

The bottom line

Stop trying to stop the scratching and give your cat a better target: a tall, stable post placed right beside the damaged furniture, rewarded and trimmed every two to three weeks, will solve most cases inside a fortnight - and never declaw. FAQs

Can I train my cat to stop scratching the sofa completely?

Not completely, because scratching is a core cat behaviour rather than a bad habit. What you can do is redirect it almost entirely in two to three weeks by placing an attractive, tall and stable scratcher beside the damaged furniture, protecting that furniture temporarily, and trimming the claws regularly. Most cats will keep one preferred scratching station, and the fight ends once that station is not your sofa.

Is declawing ever acceptable?

It is a partial amputation: the last bone of each toe is removed along with the claw and tendon, and it can cause lasting pain, altered gait and behaviour problems. It is banned or restricted in many countries, and the AVMA advises it only as a last resort for a medical problem. Trimming, nail caps and proper scratching surfaces solve the same problem without surgery.

Why does my cat only scratch when I am in the room?

Scratching is partly social marking, and some cats do it where the household gathers, often right after waking or when you come home. It is not spite. Give the cat a tall scratcher in that same room, ideally near where you sit, and reward the moment they use it so the marking happens on a surface you like.

Should I worry if my cat scratches itself instead of furniture?

Yes. Scratching and chewing at its own skin, ears or face is a medical sign rather than a behaviour one. The usual causes are fleas, ear mites, a food or environmental allergy, or a painful claw or nail bed. Book a vet visit; no amount of behaviour work will fix itching, and this article cannot diagnose what is going on.
