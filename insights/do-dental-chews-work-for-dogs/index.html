Do Dental Chews Actually Clean Dogs' Teeth? (The Truth)

Dental Care · 2026-09-27

Bottom line: Chews are a genuine help, not a gimmick — but only the right ones, used alongside brushing. If you do one thing, brush the back teeth daily. If you do two, add a VOHC-accepted chew on the off days. This is general guidance, not a diagnosis; your vet knows your dog's mouth best and can flag problems you can't see.

Dental chews help, but they don't replace brushing. Treat them as a genuinely useful supplement that slows tartar between brushings — not a stand-in for the toothbrush.

What the research actually shows

Several veterinary dental studies find that a properly designed chew reduces plaque and tartar by a meaningful margin compared with no dental care at all. The benefit comes from mechanical abrasion as your dog gnaws, and some chews add enzymes or antibacterial coatings that keep working while the dog chews. The American Animal Hospital Association notes that home care — brushing, rinses, and approved chews — is the foundation of preventing periodontal disease, which affects the large majority of dogs by middle age.

The catch is coverage. A chew only touches the teeth your dog actually bites down on — usually the big chewing teeth and the outer surfaces. The upper molars and the inside of the teeth, where brushing does the most good, often get skipped entirely. So chews reduce the problem; they don't solve it.

The honest limits of a chew

  • Coverage gaps. The surfaces a chew misses are exactly the ones where gum disease starts. That's why no veterinary body recommends chews as a replacement for brushing.
  • Not all chews are equal. A soft, gummy treat that vanishes in two bites does almost nothing. The product has to be chewed, not gulped, to earn its benefit.
  • Calories count. Many chews are surprisingly caloric. On a small dog, one daily chew can be a real chunk of the day's intake, so factor it into meals or pick a lower-calorie option.
  • Hardness risks. Very hard items can fracture teeth — more on that below.

How to pick one that does something

1. Look for the VOHC Seal of Acceptance. The Veterinary Oral Health Council independently verifies that a product reduces plaque or tartar. No seal means no proof it works — skip it.

2. Size it to your dog. A chew should be large enough that your dog chews rather than swallowing it whole. Too small is a choking and blockage risk.

3. Match texture to the risk. Very hard items — antlers, raw bones, hard nylon — can crack teeth. A chewy, flexible texture is the safe middle ground that still scrubs.

4. Read the ingredient list. Avoid anything with xylitol (toxic to dogs) and be wary of raw-hide products sourced or processed in ways you can't verify. A simple, digestible formula is safest.

A daily routine that actually works

The most effective home plan layers the tools so each covers the other's gaps:

  • Daily brush reaches the gumline where disease starts — the part chews miss.
  • A VOHC-accepted chew on non-brush days adds abrasion and keeps your dog engaged with the process.
  • Water additive or spray covers the gaps when life gets busy and you skip a session.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: a chew should never cause pain. Broken teeth, bleeding from the mouth, dropping food, pawing at the face, or a sudden foul odor after a chew can mean a fractured tooth or something wedged — call your vet. Persistent bad breath despite regular chews is also a reason to book a professional cleaning, because it often signals underlying dental disease that home care can't reverse.

The bottom line

Chews are a genuine help, not a gimmick — but only the right ones, used alongside brushing. If you do one thing, brush the back teeth daily. If you do two, add a VOHC-accepted chew on the off days. This is general guidance, not a diagnosis; your vet knows your dog's mouth best and can flag problems you can't see.

FAQs

Can dental chews replace brushing my dog's teeth?

No. Chews reduce plaque and tartar on the surfaces a dog bites, but they miss the gumline and inner tooth surfaces where disease starts. Veterinary guidelines treat brushing as the foundation and chews as a supplement, not a substitute.

What does the VOHC seal mean on a dental chew?

The Veterinary Oral Health Council awards its Seal of Acceptance only after a product is independently shown to reduce plaque or tartar. It's the closest thing to proof a chew works — no seal means no verified benefit.

Are bully sticks or antlers safe for dog teeth?

They're popular but risky. Very hard chews like antlers and raw bones can fracture teeth, which is painful and needs a vet. Softer, flexible chews that still require real gnawing are the safer choice for daily use.

How many dental chews can I give my dog per day?

Follow the label, but treat most chews as a calorie source — one a day is typical for many products. On a small dog, factor the chew into the daily meal allowance, or choose a lower-calorie option to avoid weight gain.
