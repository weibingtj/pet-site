Cat Hairballs: What's Normal and When Should You Worry?

General · 2026-11-09

Bottom line: Brush a long-haired cat two or three times a week and treat weekly hairballs as a symptom rather than a chore. Most frequent hairballs trace back to overgrooming, pain, obesity or a motility problem, so get the cat examined before you buy another paste.

An occasional hairball is normal. A cat bringing one up every week, retching without producing anything, or going off food after a hairball is not, and it should be checked rather than managed with paste. Most frequent hairball cases turn out to be grooming, pain or gut problems wearing a fur coat.

What a hairball actually is

A hairball is a trichobezoar: a felted plug of swallowed hair, food and mucus that forms in the stomach and is vomited up, usually as a tubular shape. Cats spend a third of their waking hours grooming, and the barbs on the tongue send loose hair down the throat by design. Most of it passes through the gut. A small amount collects in the stomach and comes back up.

Two useful corrections. First, cats do not cough up hairballs, they vomit them, so the gagging noise is stomach content moving up. Second, a cat that coughs and produces nothing at all is more likely to have asthma than a hairball problem, and feline asthma is a genuine disease that needs treatment.

How often is actually normal

Most healthy short-haired cats produce a hairball a few times a year, and plenty produce none at all. Escalating frequency is the signal:

  • A few times a year: normal, especially in spring and autumn shedding
  • Monthly: worth noting, usually a coat or grooming issue
  • Weekly or more: treat as a symptom, get the cat examined
  • Daily retching with nothing coming up: urgent, this is not a hairball pattern

The pattern matters as much as the count. Hairballs in a cat that is otherwise bright, eating well and passing normal stool are low risk. Hairballs in a cat that is losing weight, vomiting food, or hiding are a different problem entirely.

Which cats are most likely to have them

  • Long-haired breeds: Persians, Maine Coons, Ragdolls and any double coat, where loose undercoat is abundant
  • Senior cats with dental pain or arthritis: grooming hurts, so hair is swallowed in clumps rather than being removed
  • Overweight cats, who cannot reach their back and hindquarters to groom properly
  • Cats with itchy skin: parasites, allergies or ringworm drive extra licking, and the hair goes down with it
  • Anxious or stressed cats, where overgrooming is a coping behaviour rather than hygiene

That list explains why brushing more is not always the fix. If a cat is overgrooming because its skin itches or its mouth hurts, the hairballs will continue until the cause is treated.

The myth that hairballs are just part of having a cat

They are common, and that has been translated into normal. It is a costly assumption in older cats. Frequent vomiting that owners attribute to hair can be chronic enteropathy or, in cats over eight, intestinal lymphoma, with hair simply riding along in the vomit. A cat that vomits hairballs weekly for months deserves bloodwork, a weight trend and often imaging, not another tube of gel.

Persistent hairballs also cause real harm on their own: esophagitis from repeated acid exposure, appetite loss, and in rare cases an obstruction that needs surgery.

What actually helps

1. Brush the coat two or three times a week. This is the highest-yield step and it removes hair before it is swallowed. Our grooming, paws and ears guide covers tools by coat type, and a deshedding comb beats a soft brush on a double coat.

2. Raise water and moisture intake. Wet food, a fountain, or water added to meals keeps ingesta moving through the gut. Dehydrated cats move hair through more slowly.

3. Add fibre with a plan. A teaspoon of plain canned pumpkin, a vet-recommended psyllium product, or a therapeutic high-fibre diet can help motility. Introduce slowly over a week to avoid gas and loose stool.

4. Use hairball paste sparingly. Mineral oil based products lubricate and can help short term, but they do not solve the cause, and long-term petroleum-based products can interfere with fat-soluble vitamin absorption. Never give paste to a cat that is vomiting or off food, because it can worsen a blockage.

5. Treat the overgrooming cause. If the coat is thin, patchy or barbered, the licking is the problem. This article on cats overgrooming bald patches walks through the itch, pain and stress drivers worth ruling out first.

6. Keep the environment interesting. Boredom and stress both drive licking, so scratching posts, vertical space and daily play are not soft extras. Grooming tools and fibre supplements live in the shop if you are starting from nothing.

One caution worth stating plainly: never give a laxative, petroleum jelly or an enema to a cat at home. Enemas in cats can cause serious electrolyte and heart problems, and they are a clinic procedure for a reason.

When to see a vet

Call your vet if hairballs come up weekly or more, if the cat retches or coughs without producing anything, if it goes off food or seems lethargic after a hairball, or if there is no stool for 24 to 48 hours. Weight loss, vomiting of food rather than hair, diarrhoea or hiding all point to something beyond hair and need an exam with bloodwork. A cat straining to vomit with a hard or painful belly is an emergency, since a hairball obstruction or a foreign body can both present that way.

The bottom line

Brush a long-haired cat two or three times a week and treat weekly hairballs as a symptom rather than a chore. Most frequent hairballs trace back to overgrooming, pain, obesity or a motility problem, so get the cat examined before you buy another paste.

FAQs

How many hairballs a year is normal for a cat?

A few, and often none. Most short-haired cats produce hairballs only occasionally, usually during heavy shedding. Monthly hairballs suggest a coat or grooming issue, and weekly hairballs should be investigated as a symptom rather than managed with paste.

Do hairball remedies actually work?

Mineral oil based pastes can lubricate the gut and help in the short term, but they do not address the cause and are not a long-term solution. Fibre, moisture and regular brushing do more over time. Never give paste or petroleum jelly to a cat that is vomiting or not eating.

Can hairballs kill a cat?

Rarely, but obstruction is possible, and repeated vomiting causes real harm such as esophagitis and appetite loss. A cat that retches unproductively, has a painful belly, stops passing stool or refuses food needs urgent veterinary care rather than home remedies.

My cat coughs like it is bringing up a hairball but nothing comes up. What is that?

Cats vomit hairballs, they do not cough them up. Repeated coughing with no vomit is more consistent with feline asthma, which is a treatable but progressive disease, or with an airway irritant. Get it examined rather than assuming hair.
