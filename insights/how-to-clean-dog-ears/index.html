How to Clean Your Dog's Ears at Home (Safely, Step by Step)

Grooming, Paws & Ears · 2026-09-26

Clean ears prevent problems — but only if you do it right

Most dogs don't need their ears cleaned often, and over-cleaning does more harm than good. The goal is to remove visible wax and debris before it builds into an infection — not to scrub the ear canal sterile. Done gently every few weeks (or as your vet advises), ear cleaning is one of the easiest ways to head off the most common dog ear problem: otitis, or inflammation of the ear canal.

This is general guidance, not a diagnosis. If your dog's ear looks red, smells, or your dog is in pain, stop and call your vet — cleaning an infected ear can make it worse.

How often is "often enough"?

  • Floppy-eared breeds (Cocker Spaniels, Basset Hounds, Golden Retrievers): every 1–2 weeks, because trapped moisture and poor airflow invite yeast.
  • Swimmers: after every swim or bath, dry the outer ear — water left in the canal is a yeast buffet.
  • Upright, airy ears (most terriers, German Shepherds): often every 2–4 weeks, or only when you see wax.

When in doubt, your grooming, paws & ears guide has a breed-by-breed starting point, and the shop carries vet-style ear rinses and drying solutions.

What you need

1. A dog-formulated ear cleaner (never hydrogen peroxide, alcohol, or witch hazel — they sting and dry the skin).

2. Cotton balls or gauze — for the visible part of the ear only.

3. Treats, because cooperation is a learned skill.

Step-by-step

1. Lift the ear flap and squeeze a small amount of cleaner into the canal — just enough that you see it at the opening. Don't poke the bottle tip inside.

2. Massage the base of the ear for 20–30 seconds. You'll hear a squelch; that's the cleaner loosening debris. Your dog will probably shake — that's fine and expected.

3. Wipe the outer ear and the visible canal with a cotton ball. Go only as deep as your finger comfortably reaches.

4. Reward, then repeat on the other side.

5. Never use cotton swabs (Q-tips) inside the canal — they push wax deeper and can perforate the eardrum.

The mistakes that cause infections

  • Swabbing the canal. It packs wax against the eardrum and scratches delicate skin.
  • Using human products. Alcohol and peroxide disrupt the ear's natural defenses.
  • Cleaning too often. Stripping healthy wax irritates the canal and paradoxically causes more discharge.
  • Ignoring the shake. If your dog won't tolerate it, forcing the process turns a calm routine into a fight — and a stressed dog's ears get cleaned poorly.
When to see a vet: skip home cleaning and call your vet if you see any of these — a strong or yeasty smell, red or swollen ear canal, discharge that's brown, yellow or bloody, your dog pawing at the ear or crying when it's touched, head tilt, or loss of balance. These point to infection or a ruptured eardrum, where cleaning at home can cause real damage.

Dirty ear vs. infected ear

A dirty ear has light brown wax, no smell, and your dog doesn't react when you touch it. An infected ear is red, smelly, produces dark or pus-like discharge, and is painful. When you can't tell, assume infection and let the vet decide — it's the safer call.

Signs your dog is due for a clean

You don't need a calendar if you watch the ear. Book a cleaning (or a vet check, if anything looks off) when you notice:

  • A visible buildup of waxy brown debris in the outer ear.
  • A mild, waxy odor that wasn't there last week.
  • Your dog shaking their head or pawing at the ear more than usual — early irritation often shows here first.
  • After any swim or bath, as a routine preventive step.

If the ear is already red, smelly, or sore, that's not a cleaning cue — that's a vet cue (see the callout above).

Puppies vs. seniors

Puppies should meet ear handling early: lift the flap, peek inside, treat, repeat — so the adult dog never learns to guard their ears. Seniors often produce more wax and may have thicker, less flexible canals, so a slightly more frequent, gentler schedule helps. Older dogs also tolerate change poorly, so keep the routine calm and predictable.

Breed anatomy matters

Dogs with narrow, hairy canals (Poodles, Schnauzers, Bichons) trap wax and hair that you may need a groomer or vet to pluck or clear — don't dig for it yourself. Brachycephalic breeds (Bulldogs, Frenchies) have compressed anatomy and higher infection rates, so watch them closely. When a breed is prone, prevention is cheaper than the infection.

The bottom line

Ear cleaning is preventive maintenance, not a deep clean. Gentle, infrequent, and never past your first knuckle keeps most dogs infection-free. Save the aggressive scrubbing for the kitchen floor.

FAQs

How often should I clean my dog's ears?

It depends on breed and lifestyle. Floppy-eared or swimming dogs often need every 1–2 weeks; upright-eared dogs may only need it every 2–4 weeks or when wax is visible. Over-cleaning causes irritation, so don't do it daily.

Can I use cotton swabs (Q-tips) in the ear?

No. Swabs push wax deeper and can scratch the canal or perforate the eardrum. Use cotton balls or gauze on the visible outer ear only, and never past where your finger comfortably reaches.

What if my dog hates ear cleaning?

Go slow: let them lick peanut butter while you lift the flap, then reward after each step. If they're genuinely distressed or in pain, stop — that resistance can itself signal infection, so check with your vet.

How do I tell a dirty ear from an infected one?

A dirty ear has light wax and no smell, and your dog doesn't mind being touched there. An infected ear is red, smelly, may have dark or pus-like discharge, and is painful. When unsure, treat it as infected and call the vet.
