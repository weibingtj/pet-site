How to Check Your Dog's Weight and Body Condition at Home

General · 2026-10-15

Bottom line: Learn the body condition score once and use it every month: feel the ribs without pressing, run your hands along the waist and check for a tucked belly from the side, then weigh your dog on the same home scale so you have a trend rather than a single number. Log it, and bring the log to your next vet visit - creeping weight change is far easier to act on when you catch it a kilo at a time instead of five.

Your dog's body condition score tells you far more than the scale does, and you can check it at home in under a minute: the ribs should be easy to feel but not visible, there should be a waist behind the ribs from above and a tucked belly from the side. Weight alone does not distinguish muscle from fat, and the number on the scale means nothing without a reference point. This guide shows you how to score body condition, track weight properly at home, and know what to bring to a vet visit. It is educational only and does not replace your vet's assessment.

Why the scale alone is not enough

Two dogs can both weigh 25 kg and be in completely different shape. One is lean and muscular; the other carries fat that will load its joints, its heart and its airway. That is why veterinary nutritionists use a body condition score (BCS) alongside weight - usually on a 1 to 9 scale, where 4 to 5 out of 9 is ideal for most dogs.

Weight matters most as a trend. A steady 22 kg that has been stable for two years tells a very different story from a rise from 18 to 22 kg over one winter. Trends need two things: a consistent method and a written record.

How to score body condition in under a minute

Use your hands, not just your eyes - long coats hide a great deal.

1. Run your hands along the ribs. Gentle pressure only. You want to feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat, like the back of your fingers under light skin. Ribs you can see clearly means underweight. Ribs you have to push to find means overweight.

2. Look from above. Stand over your dog while they stand square. There should be a visible waist - a slight inward curve behind the ribcage before the hips. No waist at all, or an outward bulge, means too much body fat.

3. Look from the side. The belly should tuck up from the chest towards the hind legs. A flat or downward-sloping belly line is a strong sign of excess weight.

4. Feel the hip bones and spine. They should be easy to find but not prominent or sharp. Very visible bones suggest your dog is too thin and needs a vet check.

5. Check the tail base. Fat pads either side of the tail base is one of the earliest places extra weight shows up, and one of the last places it leaves.

Compare what you find against a BCS chart. A score of 4 or 5 out of 9 is the usual target for most adult dogs, though some breeds run leaner or heavier by design and sighthounds genuinely show more rib.

How to weigh a dog at home

  • Small dogs fit on a kitchen scale, or stand on a bathroom scale while holding them and subtract your own weight.
  • Medium and large dogs work fine on a human bathroom scale. Stand on it yourself first, then step on holding your dog, or get them to stand still on it alone with a treat and a hand under the chest.
  • Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before breakfast, on the same scale. Consistency beats absolute accuracy.
  • Weigh every two to four weeks for an adult dog in stable condition, and weekly while you are actively managing weight.
  • Track it. A note on the fridge, a phone note with the date, or a simple spreadsheet. Two numbers with dates tell you more than a perfect single measurement.

For puppies, expect growth, not stability - weigh weekly and compare against your vet's growth chart rather than a target number.

What a change in weight is telling you

  • Gradual gain over months. Usually calorie intake creeping above need, often from treats, table scraps or a change of food. It is the most common and most fixable pattern.
  • Sudden weight loss with a good appetite. Needs a vet visit. A dog eating well and losing condition is a red flag.
  • Weight loss without eating enough, often with a reduced appetite, mouth pain or nausea. See our guide to why your dog might not be eating for the patterns worth reporting.
  • Weight gain with lethargy and a poor coat. Worth a check for thyroid and other endocrine problems.
  • Weight change plus increased drinking, which is covered in our daily hydration guide and is often the clue that ties the two together.
  • Loss of muscle along the spine and hindquarters in an older dog. This can happen without much weight change at all, and it matters for mobility. Muscle loss after age seven is common and worth a discussion - our senior dog care guide covers what to watch.

What to do with the number

  • If your dog is over the ideal score, cut treats first. Measure food with a proper measuring cup or a kitchen scale rather than a scoop, and cut the daily ration by around 10 per cent to start. Then recheck the score in a month. Crash dieting is not safe for dogs and should be vet-supervised, particularly in small breeds.
  • If your dog is under the ideal score, increase food gradually and get a vet check. Unexplained thinness is a symptom, not a cosmetic issue.
  • If your dog is stable and lean, keep doing what you are doing. Prevention here is genuinely easier than reversal, particularly for joints.
  • Senior dogs benefit from more frequent scoring, because both fat gain and muscle loss happen more easily. Weigh monthly once your dog passes seven.

A cheap set of kitchen scales for the food bowl, a bathroom scale and a five-minute monthly habit cover most of what matters.

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit for any unexplained weight loss, weight change of more than about 10 per cent, weight gain alongside lethargy, increased drinking or a poor coat, a body condition score at either extreme of the scale, or sudden loss of muscle along the back and hindquarters - and mention weight change at your next routine appointment rather than waiting for it to become obvious.

The bottom line

Learn the body condition score once and use it every month: feel the ribs without pressing, run your hands along the waist and check for a tucked belly from the side, then weigh your dog on the same home scale so you have a trend rather than a single number. Log it, and bring the log to your next vet visit - creeping weight change is far easier to act on when you catch it a kilo at a time instead of five.

Sources: VCA Animal Hospitals - Body Condition Scores for Dogs; AAHA - Weight Management and Nutritional Assessment Guidelines; AVMA - Pet Obesity and Healthy Weight Resources.

FAQs

How do I check my dog's body condition score at home?

Use your hands as well as your eyes. Feel the ribs - they should be easy to find under a thin layer of fat but not visible. Look from above for a visible waist behind the ribcage, and from the side for a tucked-up belly line. Bones you have to search for or a waist that has vanished both mean excess fat. Most adult dogs sit at 4 or 5 out of 9.

How can I weigh my dog without a vet scale?

A standard bathroom scale works. Step on alone first and note your weight, then step on holding your dog and subtract. Small dogs can be weighed on kitchen scales, or held on a bathroom scale the same way. Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before breakfast, on the same scale, and repeat every two to four weeks so you have a trend rather than a single number.

Is it normal for an older dog to lose weight?

No - unexplained weight loss in an older dog always deserves a vet visit. It can point to dental pain, kidney or liver disease, an endocrine problem, inflammatory bowel disease or cancer. Loss of muscle along the spine and hindquarters without much change on the scale is also common after age seven and worth raising, because it affects mobility and is easier to slow if you catch it early.

How much weight should my dog lose if it is overweight?

Aim for a slow, steady loss with a vet guiding the target, rather than a crash diet. Start by cutting treats and table scraps, measure food with a kitchen scale or a proper measuring cup instead of guessing, and reduce the daily ration by around 10 per cent. Recheck the body condition score after a month. Rapid weight loss is not safe, particularly in small breeds, so get the rate set by your vet.
