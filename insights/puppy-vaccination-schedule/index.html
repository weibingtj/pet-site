Puppy Vaccination Schedule: Which Shots and When to Give

General · 2026-11-10

Bottom line: Follow the core series to a final dose at 16 weeks or older, keep the puppy away from unvaccinated dogs until a week after that dose, and socialise through safe indoor and carrier-based exposures in the meantime.

Core puppy vaccines start at six to eight weeks and repeat every three to four weeks until the final dose is given at 16 weeks or older, because the antibodies a puppy inherits from its mother fade on a different timetable in every pup. That last dose is the one that counts, and a series that stops at 12 weeks leaves a real immunity gap.

Why the series matters more than the date

A newborn puppy absorbs antibodies through colostrum in the first day of life. Those maternal antibodies protect the pup while they last, but they also neutralise the vaccine, which is why a single jab at eight weeks does nothing for a puppy whose antibody levels are still high.

Antibody levels fall somewhere between six and sixteen weeks, and nobody can tell you the exact week for your puppy without a titre test. So the series is designed to catch the pup after maternal antibodies drop but before exposure happens. Every booster in the series is a second attempt at hitting that window.

This is also why an older puppy adopted at 14 weeks with no records needs the full series, not a single shot. Age is a proxy, not proof.

The core vaccines

Core vaccines are the ones every dog should receive because the diseases are serious, widespread and easily transmitted.

1. DA2PP or DHPP covers distemper, adenovirus (infectious hepatitis), parvovirus and parainfluenza. Given at 6 to 8 weeks, then every 3 to 4 weeks until 16 weeks or older.

2. Rabies is given once, usually at 12 to 16 weeks depending on local law, then boosted at one year and every one to three years after that.

Parvovirus is the reason timing matters so much. It survives in soil for months, it kills young puppies quickly, and the window between maternal antibody fade and full vaccination is when most cases occur. Distemper is less common but has a worse prognosis once neurological signs appear.

Non-core and lifestyle vaccines

Non-core vaccines are chosen for the individual dog based on exposure risk, and the decision should be made with your vet rather than by default.

  • Bordetella for dogs going to daycare, training classes, groomers or boarding
  • Leptospirosis for dogs with access to ponds, standing water, livestock or rodents, and increasingly recommended in urban areas
  • Lyme disease for dogs in tick-heavy regions
  • Canine influenza where outbreaks are active or where the dog mixes with many others

A dog that lives alone in an apartment and never leaves the block has a genuinely different risk profile from a dog that goes to a dog park three times a week. Say both facts out loud at the appointment.

A typical schedule

Exact timing varies by country, by vaccine brand and by your vet's protocol, so treat this as the shape of the schedule rather than a prescription.

  • 6 to 8 weeks: first DA2PP, faecal check, general health check
  • 10 to 12 weeks: second DA2PP, first leptospirosis if indicated, bordetella if indicated
  • 14 to 16 weeks: third DA2PP, rabies, second lepto
  • 16 to 20 weeks: final DA2PP if the series started late or timing slipped
  • 12 to 16 months: first adult booster for all core vaccines

Deworming runs alongside this, usually every two weeks until 12 weeks and then monthly to six months, following your vet's plan and your local parasite risk.

The 16 week rule and what it protects

The single most common vaccination mistake is finishing the series at 12 weeks because the puppy is going out and the owner wants it done. A puppy whose last core dose landed at 12 weeks may still have had maternal antibodies at that point, which means that dose was wasted. The puppy looks vaccinated and is not.

One dose given at 16 weeks or later, after the maternal antibodies are gone, is the dose that reliably produces immunity. If your puppy's series ended early, one extra dose after 16 weeks closes the gap.

Socialising before the series is finished

The old advice was total isolation until the series ended, and that produced well-vaccinated puppies with behaviour problems. The current balance is to socialise deliberately while avoiding high-risk ground.

  • Carry the puppy in your arms or a sling in public, and let it meet people safely
  • Invite vaccinated, healthy adult dogs to your home for calm introductions
  • Attend a puppy class that requires proof of vaccination and disinfects the floor
  • Avoid parks, pet shop floors, communal grass and anywhere unknown dogs toilet
  • Do not let the puppy sniff or greet unknown dogs on the street

The week-by-week structure for this is in how to socialise a puppy, and the home base setup that makes it manageable is in crate training a puppy. Basic supplies for a new pup are listed in the shop.

Records, reactions and boosters

Keep a single card or app record with product names, batch numbers and dates. If your puppy changes vet, or you travel, that record decides whether a booster is needed. Rabies documentation in particular must be official.

Mild sleepiness and a tender spot at the injection site for 24 hours are common. A small lump that persists beyond a few weeks should be checked. Facial swelling, hives, vomiting, repeated diarrhoea or difficulty breathing after a vaccine needs immediate veterinary attention, and if it happens once it should be recorded so the next appointment can plan around it.

When to see a vet

Call your vet the same day if your puppy has vomiting, diarrhoea, refusal to eat, lethargy or fever in the two weeks after a vaccine, and seek emergency care immediately for facial swelling, hives, repeated vomiting or laboured breathing, which can be a vaccine reaction. Any puppy under six months with bloody diarrhoea, repeated vomiting or sudden collapse needs urgent assessment for parvovirus regardless of vaccination status. This article explains the standard schedule and is not a substitute for a veterinary consultation, since local law and individual risk change the plan.

The bottom line

Follow the core series to a final dose at 16 weeks or older, keep the puppy away from unvaccinated dogs until a week after that dose, and socialise through safe indoor and carrier-based exposures in the meantime.

Sources: AAHA 2022 Canine Vaccination Guidelines; AVMA: Vaccinations and Your Dog; VCA Animal Hospitals: Puppy Vaccination Schedule.

FAQs

Can my puppy go outside before all its shots?

Yes, with care. Carry the puppy in public, use your own garden or a clean indoor space, and meet only healthy vaccinated adult dogs. Avoid parks, communal grass and pet shop floors until a week after the final core dose at 16 weeks or older.

What happens if I miss a booster date?

A few days late is not a disaster, but a long gap can mean starting the series again. If the gap between doses runs past about four weeks in a young puppy, your vet will usually restart the sequence rather than count the earlier doses.

Why does my puppy need three or four shots of the same vaccine?

Because maternal antibodies fade on an unpredictable timetable. Each dose is an attempt to vaccinate after those antibodies are gone. Only the dose given at 16 weeks or later is reliably effective, which is why the series is timed to reach that point.

How long after the last shot is my puppy fully protected?

Allow about a week after the final core dose for immunity to build. Full protection also depends on the series being completed at 16 weeks or older, so a puppy that finished at 12 weeks may need one extra dose before it is genuinely covered.
