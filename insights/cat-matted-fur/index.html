Matted Cat Fur: How to Untangle It Without a Fight

Grooming, Paws & Ears · 2026-10-19

Bottom line: Work out small loose mats in five-minute sessions with your fingers and a wide-tooth comb, never with scissors, and book a groomer or vet for anything tight against the skin - then treat the matting as a symptom and get the arthritis, dental pain or weight problem checked, because a cat that stops grooming is usually hurting somewhere.

Small loose mats can be worked out at home with your fingers and a wide-tooth comb in short sessions; tight mats that sit against the skin should be clipped off by a groomer or vet, never cut with scissors. And a cat who suddenly starts matting needs a medical check, because the coat is usually reporting on something else.

Matting is common, it is uncomfortable, and it is one of the few cat problems where the fix is genuinely straightforward once you know which kind of mat you are dealing with.

Why cats mat

A healthy cat grooms for hours a day. That constant combing with a barbed tongue keeps the coat separated and the oils distributed. Matting happens when that process breaks down, and there are only a handful of reasons it does:

  • Pain or stiffness. Arthritis in the spine and hips makes it painful to turn and reach the lower back, so that area mats first. This is the single most common reason an older cat stops grooming.
  • Dental disease. A sore mouth means less licking. If the coat has declined and the breath is bad, look in the mouth.
  • Obesity. A cat who cannot reach her own rear end cannot groom it. The matting follows the shape of what she cannot reach.
  • Nausea or lethargy. A cat who feels unwell stops grooming long before she stops eating.
  • Coat type. Longhaired and double-coated breeds - Persians, Maine Coons, Ragdolls, British Longhairs - mat far more readily and often need help even when healthy.
  • Something rubbing or trapping the coat. A collar, a harness left on, a cat who sleeps in one tight spot.
  • Bathing gone wrong. A cat bathed without proper rinsing and drying mats badly, because the undercoat clumps as it dries.

Notice how many of those are medical. That is the point.

Loose mat or tight mat

This distinction decides everything.

A loose mat sits on top of the coat with air under it. You can get two fingers underneath, the skin moves freely, and the clump has some give when you squeeze it.

A tight mat is felted against the body like a scab. You cannot get fingers under it, the skin is pulled up with it, and it may already be red or moist underneath. Tight mats cut off air to the skin and can hide wounds, sores or infection.

Loose mats are a grooming job. Tight mats are a clipping job, and if the skin underneath looks angry, they are a vet job.

What you need

  • A wide-tooth comb or a mat splitter - not a fine flea comb, which will snag and hurt.
  • Your fingers, which are the most useful tool here.
  • Treats within reach and a cat who is not cornered.
  • Good light, so you can see the skin.
  • Patience for several short sessions rather than one long one.

Step by step for a loose mat

1. Pick your moment. Mid-afternoon on a warm windowsill beats a cold kitchen floor. Never start when the cat is wound up or hungry.

2. Do not restrain. Let her lie where she chooses. If she gets up, the session is over. Forcing it teaches her that grooming hurts and next time will be harder.

3. Break the mat apart with your fingers first. Work from the outer edge inwards, pulling gently in small increments. Think of separating felt, not pulling it off.

4. Then comb from the tip inwards. Hold the mat between your fingers so you are not pulling the skin, and comb only the last centimetre of hair. When that section is free, move inwards.

5. Stop at five minutes. One mat per session is fine. Progress over a week beats a wrestling match.

6. Reward, then walk away. Put the treat down and leave. Do not make a fuss.

Repeat daily. Most loose mats will separate within three to five short sessions.

Why scissors are a bad idea

Cats have extremely thin, loose skin that tents up away from the body, and mats pull that skin upwards into the clump. It is almost impossible to judge where the skin ends and the mat begins.

Every year, vets see cats with scissors wounds - some of them full-thickness cuts needing surgery. The risk is not worth it. If a mat is tight enough that you feel you need scissors, it is tight enough to need clippers, and clippers have a guard that keeps the blade off the skin.

Electric clippers used at home are also risky on cats, because the blade heats and the skin is thin. For a cat with several tight mats, a professional groomer or your vet is faster, safer, and less stressful for everyone.

The mat as a medical clue

If a cat who has always been well groomed starts matting, treat it as a symptom.

  • Mats over the lower back and hips in an older cat point towards arthritis. That is treatable, and treating it usually restores grooming.
  • Mats under the chin and on the chest often accompany dental pain or drooling.
  • Mats on the rear and hind legs in an overweight cat are a body-condition problem. Our guide to checking weight at home describes the same body-condition scoring method vets use on cats.
  • Generalised matting with a dull coat and weight loss in a cat over eight warrants blood work, including thyroid and kidney values.

Sudden coat decline belongs in the same bucket as sudden cat hair loss - a change in the coat is a change in the cat.

Preventing the next lot

  • Brush two to three times a week, daily for longhaired cats. Short sessions, always.
  • Use the right tool - a slicker brush or metal comb for longhairs, a rubber curry for shorthairs.
  • Never let a harness or collar stay on in the house; friction points become mats.
  • Keep the weight down. Reach is the whole game.
  • Ask about pain relief if your cat is older and stiff. This is the highest-yield prevention step there is.
  • Skip the bath unless it is genuinely needed, and if it is needed, dry thoroughly.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: book an appointment if a mat is tight against the skin, the skin underneath is red, moist, bleeding or smelly, your cat flinches or cries when the area is touched, or the matting came on suddenly in a cat that used to groom well - hidden wounds, skin infection, arthritis and dental disease all need diagnosis before clipping.

The bottom line

Work out small loose mats in five-minute sessions with your fingers and a wide-tooth comb, never with scissors, and book a groomer or vet for anything tight against the skin - then treat the matting as a symptom and get the arthritis, dental pain or weight problem checked, because a cat that stops grooming is usually hurting somewhere.

FAQs

Can I just cut the mat out with scissors?

No. Cat skin is thin and loose and gets pulled up into the mat, so it is very easy to cut the skin rather than the hair. Vets regularly treat scissors wounds on cats, some needing surgery. Use fingers and a comb for loose mats, and clippers with a guard - ideally by a groomer or vet - for tight ones.

Will my cat's fur grow back after clipping?

Yes, though it takes time. Most cats regrow a normal coat over two to four months. A clipped cat can feel the cold more, so keep the house warm and provide somewhere soft to sleep. Regrowth can look patchy at first, which is normal.

Why is my old cat suddenly getting matted?

In older cats, the usual cause is arthritis - turning to groom the lower back becomes painful, so that area mats first. Dental disease, weight gain, thyroid disease and kidney disease also reduce grooming. A sudden change in coat condition is a reason for a vet visit, not just a better brush.

Is it cruel to shave a matted cat?

No - leaving tight mats in place is the crueller option, because they pull on the skin, trap moisture and can hide infection. A lion clip done by a groomer or vet is quick, safe and immediately more comfortable. It is not a punishment and the cat does not feel embarrassed.
