Cat Hair Loss: Common Causes and When to Worry About It

Skin & Coat · 2026-10-14

Bottom line: Book a vet appointment for any hair loss that is spreading, itchy or crusty, and note two things before you go: whether the thinning is symmetrical, which points to an internal or hormonal cause rather than a skin one, and whether the skin itself is changed or only the fur. That single distinction sends diagnosis down two very different paths.

Cat hair loss is most often caused by over-grooming, fleas, ringworm or allergies, and symmetrical bald patches on the flanks or belly usually point to an internal trigger rather than a skin problem - so any patch that is spreading, itchy or crusty needs a vet to look properly. Because cats groom constantly, hair loss appears slowly and is easy to dismiss. This guide covers what healthy shedding looks like, the causes vets check first, and how to tell an ordinary moult from a warning sign.

Normal shedding versus real hair loss

Cats shed year-round, with heavier moults in spring and autumn. Normal shedding:

  • Spreads evenly over the body.
  • Leaves no bald spots, sores or crusts.
  • Does not come with intense scratching, licking or biting.
  • Does not change the skin itself - no redness, scaling or bumps.

Real hair loss, which vets call alopecia, is patchy or localised, or leaves the skin visibly changed. If you can see skin where there should be fur, that is worth investigating.

The causes vets check first

1. Over-grooming. Stress, a new pet, a moved litter tray or a change in routine can push a cat into compulsive licking. The result is often a bald, smooth patch on the belly, inner thighs or flanks, with short broken hairs rather than completely bare skin.

2. Fleas and flea allergy dermatitis. Even one or two fleas can trigger intense itching in an allergic cat, classically over the lower back, tail base and hind legs.

3. Ringworm. A fungal infection that causes circular, scaly patches with broken hairs. It is contagious to other pets and to people, so identifying it quickly matters.

4. Allergies. Environmental allergens, foods, dust and pollen all cause itching that leads to hair loss. Our guide to why dogs get so itchy walks through the same itch-lick-lose-hair cycle in dogs.

5. Other parasites. Mites such as Demodex and Notoedres burrow into the skin and cause crusting and hair loss, often starting on the head and ears.

6. Endocrine and internal disease. Thyroid problems, Cushing's disease and diabetes can thin the coat. This is where symmetrical hair loss becomes a clue: fur thins evenly on both sides rather than in one spot.

7. Pain or arthritis. A cat with sore hips may over-groom the area, or stop grooming it entirely, leaving a dull, thinning coat over the back.

8. Nutritional problems. A poor-quality or unbalanced diet, or a cat that cannot absorb nutrients properly, produces a dry, sparse coat.

How to narrow it down at home

A structured approach tells your vet a great deal before you arrive:

  • Check for fleas properly. Comb the tail base with a fine flea comb and look for live fleas or dark grit that turns red on damp tissue.
  • Look at the skin, not just the fur. Note redness, scaling, crusts, bumps or broken hairs.
  • Map the pattern. Take dated photos of each patch so you can show whether it is spreading.
  • Watch the grooming. Is your cat licking one area, or all over? Is it grooming more at night?
  • Note what changed. New food, new pet, new cleaner, new furniture, a moved litter tray or a recent move.

Bring the photos and the notes to the appointment. Skin cases are largely a history-taking exercise, and owners who document patterns spare their cat unnecessary tests.

What treatment looks like

Treatment follows the diagnosis, not the other way round:

  • Flea control with a veterinary product that covers every pet in the household, continued for at least three months.
  • Ringworm treatment with oral medication plus environmental decontamination, because spores survive in the home for a long time.
  • Allergy management through diet trials, environmental control or medication prescribed by your vet.
  • Stress reduction and behaviour support for over-grooming, including more resources, more play and pheromone support.
  • Treating the underlying disease when bloodwork shows a thyroid, metabolic or other internal cause.

Avoid starting a random shampoo or supplement before a diagnosis, because it hides the pattern your vet needs to see. If you want supportive coat products, browse veterinary-range options in our shop, and start with the coat-type basics in our skin and coat guide.

When to see a vet

Any patch of hair loss that is spreading, itchy, crusty, red or oozing needs a vet visit - and book promptly if your cat is also losing weight, eating or drinking more than usual or vomiting, because hair loss on both sides of the body often signals an internal or hormonal cause rather than a skin problem, and this article cannot assess your cat.

The bottom line

Book a vet appointment for any hair loss that is spreading, itchy or crusty, and note two things before you go: whether the thinning is symmetrical, which points to an internal or hormonal cause rather than a skin one, and whether the skin itself is changed or only the fur. That single distinction sends diagnosis down two very different paths.

FAQs

Why is my cat losing hair in patches?

Patchy loss is most often over-grooming from stress, fleas and flea allergy, ringworm or another parasite. Look closely at the skin: if it is red, scaly, crusty or broken, or the patch is spreading or itchy, that needs a vet visit rather than a change of shampoo. Take dated photos so you can show whether it is spreading.

Is symmetrical hair loss in cats serious?

It deserves prompt attention. Hair that thins evenly on both sides of the body - flanks, belly or back - is more typical of an internal or hormonal cause such as a thyroid problem or Cushing's disease than of a skin infection. Bloodwork usually sorts it out, so book an appointment rather than waiting for it to grow back.

Can stress really make a cat lose its fur?

Yes. Compulsive over-grooming is a common response to stress, a new pet, a moved litter tray or a change in routine. It typically produces a bald, smooth patch on the belly, inner thighs or flanks with short broken hairs. Reducing stress with more resources, more play and pheromone support helps, but check for medical causes first.

How do I check my cat for fleas properly?

Use a fine flea comb over the lower back and tail base, then tip the debris onto a damp white tissue. Dark grit that turns reddish-brown is flea dirt, which means your cat has been bitten even if you never see a live flea. Treat every pet in the house with a veterinary product and continue for at least three months.
