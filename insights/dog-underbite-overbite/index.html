Dog Underbite vs Overbite: When Jaw Alignment Needs a Vet

Dental Care · 2026-11-17

Bottom line: A mild underbite or overbite is usually just how your dog is built; leave it alone if eating and gums are normal. Pain, dropped food, bleeding gums, or a bite that changed overnight means book a veterinary dental exam.

Most dogs have a slight overbite, where the upper teeth sit just ahead of the lower ones, and that is simply normal anatomy for the species. An underbite is also common and perfectly functional in many breeds, so the real question is not how the teeth line up on paper but whether the bite causes pain, traps food, or stops the dog from eating comfortably.

What a normal bite looks like

Vets describe the ideal dog bite as a scissors bite: the upper front teeth overlap the lower ones slightly, like the blades of scissors meeting. A small deviation from that is called an overbite or an underbite depending on which jaw sits further forward.

  • Overbite: the upper jaw extends noticeably beyond the lower jaw. Extreme cases are sometimes called parrot mouth.
  • Underbite: the lower jaw extends past the upper jaw. This is the expected, healthy bite for bulldogs, boxers, and several other broad-headed breeds.
  • Level bite: the front teeth meet edge to edge, which is acceptable in some dogs and can wear the teeth down over time in others.

None of these labels is a diagnosis on its own. Plenty of dogs live their whole lives with a pronounced underbite and never have a dental problem. The dental care guide explains what a healthy mouth should look and smell like day to day.

When alignment actually causes problems

The bite becomes a welfare issue when teeth meet the wrong tissue or the dog cannot use the mouth properly:

1. A tooth digging into the roof of the mouth or the lip. When lower canine teeth point inward and pierce the palate, the result is pain, ulceration, and sometimes a stubborn nose discharge. This is one of the clearest reasons a malocclusion needs treatment.

2. Crowding and rotated teeth. When the jaws are the wrong length for the number of teeth, teeth overlap or sit at odd angles. Those pockets trap food and plaque, which accelerates tartar and gum disease.

3. Trouble gripping or chewing. A dog that repeatedly drops food, chews on one side only, or gulps without chewing may have a bite that no longer works. Weight loss and a dull coat can follow.

4. Retained baby teeth. Puppies are the classic case. If a deciduous tooth stays in place while the permanent tooth erupts, the adult tooth is guided into the wrong position. A prompt vet check around six months can prevent a lifetime of crowding, and the retained baby teeth guide explains the timing.

5. A bite that changed. An adult dog whose bite was once even and suddenly is not may have a jaw injury, a growth, or a tooth problem rather than a congenital alignment issue. That is a different and more urgent conversation.

Breed notes without the folklore

Brachycephalic breeds such as bulldogs, pugs, and Boston terriers are born with a shortened upper jaw, so an underbite is normal for them. What is not normal is a lower canine that cuts into the soft palate, or gum lines that are inflamed because crowded teeth cannot be cleaned. Breed-typical does not mean problem-free, and it is worth having the mouth checked rather than assuming the look explains everything.

At the other end, long-nosed breeds can develop a pronounced overbite that looks dramatic but functions well. Judge the mouth by how the dog eats and what the gums look like, not by how unusual the profile appears in a photo.

What you can check at home

You cannot diagnose a malocclusion, but you can notice the signs that make a vet visit worthwhile:

  • Lift the lips and look for a lower tooth leaving a mark, a raw spot, or a small bleeding patch on the roof of the mouth.
  • Watch the dog eat. Dropping kibble, chewing on one side, or stopping mid-chew are all worth noting.
  • Notice the breath. Persistent bad breath is more often gum disease than the shape of the bite, but the two often travel together.
  • Run your fingers gently along the gum line for swelling or a tooth that feels loose.

Never force a painful mouth open. A dog that resists, winces, or snaps near the face should be examined by a professional, not tested further at home. Daily brushing with a pet-safe enzyme paste and the right soft toothbrush or dental wipe helps keep a crowded mouth cleaner between vet visits, but it cannot correct the alignment itself.

When to see a vet

Arrange a veterinary dental exam if your dog drops food, eats on one side, loses weight, has a raw spot or indentation on the palate, has bleeding or swollen gums, or suddenly stops letting you touch the face. A puppy with a retained baby tooth should be seen before the permanent tooth is fully seated. Pain, facial swelling, or an injury that changes the bite needs prompt attention. These signs can indicate a problem that a home routine cannot fix, and only a vet can judge whether the bite needs treatment.

The bottom line

A mild underbite or overbite is usually just how your dog is built; leave it alone if eating and gums are normal. Pain, dropped food, bleeding gums, or a bite that changed overnight means book a veterinary dental exam.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; VCA Animal Hospitals - Malocclusion and Dental Occlusion in Dogs; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Canine Dentistry.

FAQs

Is an underbite bad for a dog?

Not by itself. An underbite is the normal bite for breeds like bulldogs and boxers, and many dogs with one eat and live comfortably. It only needs attention when a tooth digs into the palate, gums become inflamed, or the dog has trouble chewing.

Can a dog's bite be corrected at home?

No. Tooth position and jaw alignment cannot be changed with chews, brushing, or training aids. Home care keeps the mouth cleaner and slows tartar, but a vet has to judge whether a malocclusion needs extraction, crown reduction, or a referral.

What is a retained baby tooth and why does it matter?

It is a puppy tooth that stays in place after the permanent tooth erupts, which can push the adult tooth into the wrong position and crowd the mouth. A vet check around six months can catch it early, with extraction timed to the neuter.

How do I know if my dog's bite is painful?

Watch for dropping food, chewing on one side only, pawing at the mouth, a raw spot on the palate, bleeding gums, or new reluctance to have the face touched. Any of those is a reason to book a veterinary dental exam rather than wait.
