Dog Tooth Abscess: Facial Swelling and What to Do Next

Dental Care · 2026-11-03

Bottom line: Facial swelling means a dental exam and x-rays, not another course of antibiotics: plan for extraction or a root canal, keep the mouth soft-fed for two weeks, then start brushing three times a week so the next tooth does not follow.

A tooth abscess in a dog is a pocket of pus at the root, and the swelling under the eye or along the jaw is pressure looking for somewhere to go. It is painful, it does not drain and heal on its own, and antibiotics alone will not fix it - the infected tooth, or at least its root, has to be treated, usually by extraction. Book a dental exam within a day or two, and the same day if the eye is involved.

What is happening under the gum

Bacteria get into the pulp through a fracture, a deep cavity or advanced periodontal disease. The pulp dies, infection tracks down the root canal and pus collects at the root tip. Bone thins, the swelling appears on the face, and eventually it either drains into the mouth through a small hole or bursts through the skin. Where it shows up tells you roughly which tooth is involved:

  • Under the eye - almost always an upper fourth premolar or molar, the big chewing teeth.
  • On the cheek or muzzle - upper canine or premolars.
  • Along the lower jaw - lower carnassial or canine teeth.
  • A small draining sore that keeps scabbing - a chronic abscess that has found an outlet.

Signs owners notice

  • A firm or squishy swelling under the eye, on the cheek or along the jawline
  • Breath that has suddenly become much worse
  • Chewing on one side, dropping food, or yelping at hard kibble
  • Drooling, pawing at the mouth, reluctance to have the muzzle touched
  • Eye discharge or puffiness on the same side as a bad upper tooth
  • In advanced cases, fever, low appetite and a dog that just seems flat

Some dogs hide it well for weeks and the first sign is a swelling that appeared overnight. The abscess was building long before the face changed shape.

Why it cannot wait

An untreated abscess keeps eating bone. In the upper jaw it can break into the nasal cavity and leave a permanent hole between mouth and nose, or spread toward the eye socket. In severe cases infection reaches the bloodstream and the dog becomes genuinely unwell. A swollen face is never a wait-for-Monday problem, and a draining sore that looks healed is not evidence the source is gone.

What the vet will do

1. Examine the mouth under sedation - most painful dental mouths cannot be assessed properly on a conscious dog.

2. Take dental x-rays - you cannot judge root health by looking at the crown, and the wrong tooth extracted means a second anaesthetic later.

3. Treat the tooth - extraction is the usual route and is often cheaper and more reliable than saving a badly damaged tooth. A root canal by a dental specialist is an option for a strategic tooth in a dog whose owner wants to preserve it.

4. Prescribe pain relief and, sometimes, antibiotics - antibiotics reduce the spread of infection before surgery, but they are a bridge, not a cure.

5. Drain and flush any pocket of pus in the soft tissue.

Radiographs are the part owners most often try to skip. They are also the part that prevents a repeat procedure.

Recovery after extraction

The first 24 hours are about rest, soft food and the prescribed pain relief. For seven to fourteen days keep the diet soft, remove hard chews and toys, and check the extraction site daily for bleeding or a returning swelling. Most dogs eat better within three days than they did for weeks before, which tells you how long they had been uncomfortable. A small amount of blood-tinged saliva on day one is normal; active bleeding, swelling that grows, or a dog that stops eating is not.

Preventing the next one

Abscesses are the end stage of periodontal disease, so the fix is unglamorous: brush the teeth several times a week, book a professional cleaning under anaesthetic when tartar builds, and stop treating dental chews as a substitute for brushing. When to see a vet

Treat facial swelling in a dog as urgent. Call for an appointment today if there is swelling under the eye or on the jaw, a foul smell from the mouth, drooling, or a dog that is reluctant to eat. Go to an emergency clinic if the swelling is rapidly enlarging, the eye is bulging or the dog cannot close it, there is fever with lethargy, or breathing becomes noisy or laboured.

The bottom line

Facial swelling means a dental exam and x-rays, not another course of antibiotics: plan for extraction or a root canal, keep the mouth soft-fed for two weeks, then start brushing three times a week so the next tooth does not follow.

FAQs

Will antibiotics cure a dog tooth abscess?

No. Antibiotics reduce the spread of infection and make the dog more comfortable before treatment, but the dead tooth and the pus pocket remain. Without extraction, a root canal or drainage, the abscess returns as soon as the course finishes.

How urgent is facial swelling in a dog?

Urgent enough to call the same day. Dental abscesses destroy bone, can break into the nasal cavity or spread toward the eye, and in severe cases cause systemic infection. Rapidly growing swelling, a bulging eye or fever means an emergency visit.

Can my dog keep the tooth instead of having it out?

Sometimes. A root canal performed by a veterinary dental specialist can save a strategic tooth, such as a canine, if the tooth is otherwise sound. For most abscessed cheek teeth, extraction is simpler, cheaper and has an excellent success rate.

How long does recovery take after extraction?

Most dogs are eating soft food comfortably within two to three days and back to normal activity in about a week. Keep hard chews and toys away for two weeks, check the site daily, and expect any leaking or swelling to settle within 48 hours of treatment.
