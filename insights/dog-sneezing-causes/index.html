Dog Sneezing: Normal, Allergic, or Something Stuck?

General · 2026-11-01

Bottom line: Judge sneezing by pattern, not volume: a few isolated sneezes are usually dust or excitement, while fits, blood, one-sided discharge or a week of sneezing need a vet exam rather than another home remedy.

An occasional sneeze in a dog is normal and needs no treatment, but fits that last minutes, a bloody or foul discharge from one nostril, or sneezing that continues for days almost always means something is stuck, inflamed, or infected. The pattern tells you more than the sound does, so watch how often it happens and whether both nostrils are involved before you decide how worried to be.

Normal sneezing versus a problem

A normal sneeze is a reflex clearing dust, hair, or a bit of water from the nose. It is usually one to three sneezes, followed by nothing for the rest of the day. Dogs also sneeze when excited, during play, or when they sniff something strong, which is harmless.

The pattern that deserves attention:

  • Long fits of sneezing, ten or twenty in a row, several times a day
  • Sneezing with blood, or a discharge that is thick, yellow-green, or smelly
  • Discharge from one nostril only, especially if it is blood-tinged
  • Pawing at the nose or face, or rubbing the muzzle on furniture
  • Sneezing accompanied by noisy breathing, snoring sounds, or a swollen muzzle
  • Any sneezing that continues for more than a week

One-sided signs are the loudest signal in that list, because infections and irritants usually affect both nostrils while a stuck object, a damaged tooth root, or a growth affects one.

The common causes, roughly in order

Irritants. Dust, smoke, aerosol sprays, scented candles, strong cleaning products, and freshly cut grass. A dog sneezing only in one room or only after cleaning is telling you something specific.

Allergies. Seasonal pollen allergy usually comes with itchy skin, licking paws, and red eyes rather than sneezing alone. If the dog is only sneezing, allergy is a less likely answer.

Foreign material up the nose. Grass seeds, foxtail awns, bits of twig, sand, and plant material. This is the classic cause of sudden violent fits of sneezing in summer, often with the dog pawing at its face and a small amount of blood.

Infections. Canine infectious respiratory disease, including kennel cough, usually brings a honking cough and a runny nose. Fungal infection of the nose is less common but causes a chronic thick discharge that does not respond to antibiotics. For the cough side of that story, see dog coughing causes.

Nasal mites. Uncommon and easily missed, and a cause of persistent sneezing in young dogs that have contact with other dogs.

Dental disease. A tooth root abscess, especially on an upper fourth premolar, drains into the nasal cavity. The result is a one-sided, foul-smelling discharge and sneezing in a dog whose teeth are overdue for attention. This is one reason dental care is part of nose health, not a separate topic.

Growths in the nose. In older dogs, a chronic one-sided bloody discharge that does not clear should be investigated rather than watched, because benign and malignant nasal tumours both start this way.

Reverse sneezing, explained

Reverse sneezing is not a sneeze at all. It is a sudden spasm of the soft palate that makes the dog stand with elbows out, neck extended, and make a loud snorting, honking inhale for a few seconds to a minute. Small breeds and brachycephalic dogs do it more. It looks alarming and is almost always harmless. Most episodes stop on their own; gently stroking the throat, briefly covering the nostrils to make the dog swallow, or offering a small treat can shorten one. Frequent reverse sneezing, or one that leaves the dog distressed, is worth mentioning to your vet, but it does not usually need an emergency visit.

What you can safely do at home

1. Remove the obvious irritants: scented candles, plug-in air fresheners, spray cleaners used near the dog, and dusty bedding.

2. Run a humidifier or take the dog into a steamy bathroom if the discharge is thick, since moisture loosens it.

3. Wipe the nose with a soft damp cloth rather than tissues, which crumble and leave bits behind.

4. Keep the dog away from long grass and seed heads until the sneezing settles, in case something is lodged.

5. Note what time of day it happens and whether it is one nostril. Those two details alone change what your vet will do next.

Do not put anything up the nose to flush it, do not use human decongestants or antihistamines without veterinary advice, and do not assume an antihistamine will help. In dogs, most sneezing is not histamine-driven, and dosing a dog from a human packet is a genuine risk.

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit for sneezing fits that keep repeating, any blood from the nose, discharge from one nostril only, a foul smell, noisy or laboured breathing, facial swelling, or sneezing that has gone on for more than a week. Go the same day if your dog is pawing frantically at its face after a walk in long grass, since that is the classic picture of a grass seed up the nose, and the sooner it comes out the simpler the procedure. This guidance is general information, not a diagnosis, and a dog that cannot breathe quietly through the nose needs to be seen rather than monitored.

The bottom line

Judge sneezing by pattern, not volume: a few isolated sneezes are usually dust or excitement, while fits, blood, one-sided discharge or a week of sneezing need a vet exam rather than another home remedy.

Sources: VCA Animal Hospitals: Sneezing and Nasal Discharge in Dogs; AAHA 2021 Canine Life Stage Guidelines; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine: Canine Infectious Respiratory Disease.

FAQs

Why does my dog sneeze when excited or playing?

Excitement changes the dog's breathing pattern and can trigger a few sneezes, and play often stirs up dust from carpet or bedding. A handful of sneezes at the start of play, with none the rest of the day, is not a concern.

Can a dog get kennel cough without boarding?

Yes. Canine infectious respiratory disease spreads wherever dogs meet, including parks, training classes, groomers, and the vet waiting room. A dog with a honking cough, runny nose, and sneezing after any of those contacts is a reasonable suspect.

Is a bloody sneeze always serious?

Not always. A single streak of blood after a violent sneezing fit, or in a dog that has been in dry air, can be minor. Blood that keeps appearing, comes from one nostril, or comes with a smelly discharge needs a vet exam, particularly in an older dog.

Do antihistamines help a sneezing dog?

Only if the cause is genuinely allergic, and even then they are usually less effective in dogs than people expect. Never dose from a human packet without veterinary guidance, because several common human products are unsafe for dogs at the wrong dose.
