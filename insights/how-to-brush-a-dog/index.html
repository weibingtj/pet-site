How to Brush a Dog's Coat: A Routine That Actually Works

Grooming, Paws & Ears · 2026-10-23

Bottom line: Match the tool to the coat, brush with the direction of hair growth in narrow sections down to the skin, and check behind the ears, the armpits, the groin, the collar line and the tail base every session - two focused minutes most days prevents the mats that a groomer otherwise has to shave out.

Brushing a dog is not one technique: the right brush and the right stroke order depend on the coat in front of you, and using the wrong tool is exactly how people create brush burn, mats, and a dog who hides when the brush comes out. Two focused minutes most days beats one long session a month.

Most dogs do not need a professional groom to stay comfortable. They need the correct tool for their coat type, a routine that reaches the places mats actually start, and a light hand.

What brushing is really doing

Four jobs, in order of how much they matter:

1. Removing dead hair before it mats or lands on your sofa. Loose undercoat that stays in the coat tangles against the skin.

2. Spreading sebum through the coat, which keeps skin supple and the coat weather-resistant.

3. A monthly body check. You find ticks, lumps, cuts, hot spots and ear trouble while they are still small.

4. Airflow and temperature control. A coat packed with dead undercoat traps heat; a brushed one insulates properly.

Match the tool to the coat

This is where home grooming usually goes wrong, because one brush gets bought for one dog and then used on the next four.

  • Short, smooth coats - Beagle, Boxer, Staffy, Pug: a rubber curry brush or hound glove, then a soft bristle brush. A slicker is overkill and irritates this skin.
  • Double coats - Husky, German Shepherd, Corgi, Labrador, Akita: an undercoat rake or wide-toothed comb for the undercoat, plus a slicker for the top coat. Never shave these dogs.
  • Curly and wool coats - Poodle, Bichon, most Doodles: a slicker plus a metal comb, and the comb is the honest test. If it will not pass through, there is a mat.
  • Wire coats - Schnauzer, most terriers: a slicker for daily tidying, and hand-stripping or a stripping knife if you want to keep the coat texture.
  • Long silky coats - Yorkshire Terrier, Shih Tzu, Maltese: a pin brush, a comb and a detangling spray, worked in sections.
  • Very short single coats - Greyhound, Whippet: a rubber mitt is enough. Anything with teeth scratches the skin.

Line brushing, step by step

For curly, wool and long coats this is the only technique that actually reaches the skin.

1. Start at the back leg or flank, not the head. Dogs settle faster once the rear quarters are done.

2. Part the coat with one hand and lay the hair forward, so you are working on a strip about two fingers wide.

3. Brush that strip from the skin outwards, in the direction the hair grows.

4. Move over one strip and repeat, working in a grid across the body. Slow and thorough beats fast and rough.

5. Finish each area with a metal comb. If the comb snags, go back to the slicker on that strip.

Stroke order, and the spots people miss

  • With the coat first, always. Brushing against the grain on a matted or sensitive dog hurts and teaches him to avoid you.
  • Against the coat only in shedding season, on double-coated dogs, and gently - that is how you lift loosened undercoat.
  • Behind the ears, the armpits, the groin, the collar line and the base of the tail are where mats begin. Check those five places with a comb every session.
  • Legs and feet last. Most dogs are touchiest there, so leave them until the dog is relaxed.

How often, realistically

  • Short coats: once a week, five minutes.
  • Double coats: ten minutes twice a week, daily during the two annual coat blows.
  • Curly and wool: line brush every other day, plus a professional clip every six to eight weeks.
  • Long silky: daily, in sections, plus a trim every four to six weeks.
  • Wire: two or three times a week, plus stripping two to four times a year.

The five mistakes that cause problems

  • Brushing a dry mat. It tightens the mat and hurts. Use a detangling spray, work it in with your fingers, and take genuinely tight mats to a groomer rather than cutting them out with scissors.
  • Pressing hard. Slicker bristles dragged with force cause brush burn, a raw red strip along the flank that can turn into a hot spot.
  • Shaving a double coat. The undercoat grows back patchy and the dog loses insulation. Dog shedding versus hair loss explains what is normal shedding and what is not.
  • Brushing only the top layer. The coat looks fine while the skin underneath is matted, which is how skin infections start under a coat nobody lifted.
  • Doing the whole dog in one sitting with a dog who dislikes it. Short, frequent sessions ending with a treat work far better.

If your dog hates being brushed

  • Put a non-slip mat down. A lot of handling problems are really sliding-on-a-hard-floor problems.
  • Rub him down with a towel first. The motion mimics grooming and settles many dogs before the brush appears.
  • Brush after a walk, when he is tired, rather than before one.
  • Two minutes, then stop - even mid-leg. Ending on a good note is worth more than finishing the job.
  • Never restrain or scruff. A dog who is held down learns that grooming is a fight he needs to avoid.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: see a vet if brushing uncovers a lump that is growing or changing, a wound, a tick you cannot remove cleanly, a raw red patch or an open sore, or if the coat is thinning in patches, the skin is greasy, smelly or scabby, or your dog suddenly will not let you touch an area he used to accept - new handling sensitivity usually means pain, and mats tight against the skin should be clipped by a groomer or vet rather than cut at home, because loose dog skin folds easily into scissor blades.

The bottom line

Match the tool to the coat, brush with the direction of hair growth in narrow sections down to the skin, and check behind the ears, the armpits, the groin, the collar line and the tail base every session - two focused minutes most days prevents the mats that a groomer otherwise has to shave out.

FAQs

How often should I brush my dog?

It depends on the coat. Short smooth coats need about once a week for five minutes. Double coats need ten minutes twice a week, and daily during the two annual shedding periods. Curly and wool coats need line brushing every other day to stop mats forming. Long silky coats need daily brushing in sections. Frequency matters more than duration.

What is the best brush for a dog with a double coat?

An undercoat rake or a wide-toothed metal comb to reach the undercoat, with a slicker for the top coat. Work in sections, with the hair, and go gently against the grain only during shedding season. Never shave a double-coated dog - the undercoat regrows patchy and the dog loses its insulation against heat and cold.

Can brushing too much hurt a dog?

Yes. Pressing hard with a slicker causes brush burn, a raw strip of skin along the flank that can become infected or turn into a hot spot. Over-brushing a short-coated dog also irritates the skin. Use light pressure, let the tool do the work, and stop if the skin looks pink or the dog flinches.

Should I cut mats out with scissors?

Not at home. Dog skin is loose and folds into the blades very easily, and matted skin underneath is often already thin and inflamed. Use a detangling spray and your fingers for small tangles, and have tight mats clipped by a groomer or vet. A mat held against the skin also traps moisture and can hide an infection.
