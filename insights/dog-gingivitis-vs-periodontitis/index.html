Gingivitis vs Periodontitis in Dogs: Key Differences

Dental Care · 2026-10-26

Bottom line: Red gums are reversible and bone loss is not, so book a dental exam while the problem is still gingivitis - then protect the investment by brushing daily with an enzymatic paste and checking the gum line monthly.

Gingivitis is inflamed, red gums caused by plaque, and it is fully reversible with a proper clean; periodontitis is the stage after that, where infection has started destroying the bone and ligament that hold each tooth in place - damage no toothbrush can undo. Telling the two apart matters because the window for a full recovery sits between them.

Most dogs over the age of three have some degree of periodontal disease, and it almost always starts the same way: plaque that nobody removed. The good news is that the early stage responds beautifully to action, and even the later stage can be managed so a dog keeps a comfortable mouth for years.

Why the difference matters

The two conditions sit on the same timeline, but they are not the same diagnosis:

  • Gingivitis - plaque bacteria irritate the gum margin. Gums look red, puffy or bleed when touched. The tooth's supporting bone is untouched, so a professional scale and polish plus home care can return the mouth to healthy.
  • Periodontitis - the infection moves below the gumline. Gum pockets deepen, attachment and bone are destroyed, roots become exposed and teeth loosen. This damage is permanent; treatment aims to stop it getting worse, not to reverse it.

That is why a dog with mild red gums is a genuinely urgent dental case - not because it is dangerous today, but because it is the last cheap exit on the road.

What gingivitis looks like

  • A thin red line along the gum edge, especially over the large cheek teeth.
  • Gums that bleed lightly when chewing or during brushing.
  • Bad breath that is noticeable but not overwhelming.
  • Plaque - a soft yellowish film - or early tartar on the teeth.
  • Eating habits unchanged. A dog with sore gums usually still eats happily.

What changes when it becomes periodontitis

  • Receding gums and visible tooth roots, most often on the upper canines and cheek teeth.
  • Deep pockets - spaces between gum and tooth where food and bacteria collect. Only a vet probing under anaesthesia can find these; you cannot see them from the sofa.
  • Loose or missing teeth, or a dog that drops kibble, chews on one side or refuses hard treats.
  • Sneezing or a nasal discharge in advanced cases, when infection eats into the bone between mouth and nose.
  • Breath that clears the room rather than merely offending it.

Small breeds - Chihuahuas, Yorkies, dachshunds and their relatives - tend to progress faster because their teeth are crowded into small jaws. Greyhounds are another famously affected breed.

How a vet tells them apart

An awake look through the lips gives a strong hint, but the real answer needs a proper examination under anaesthesia: probing each tooth for pocket depth, dental X-rays to assess bone levels, and charting every tooth. Vets follow the AAHA 2019 dental care guidelines precisely because a quick glance during a consult routinely misses what is happening below the gumline.

In practical terms: red gums plus a cleanable tooth surface points to gingivitis; pocketing, root exposure or bone loss on X-ray means periodontitis.

What actually reverses gingivitis

1. A professional scale and polish under anaesthesia - the only way to remove plaque and tartar below the gumline. Cleaning an awake mouth is cosmetic, not therapeutic.

2. Daily brushing from then on - plaque starts re-forming within hours, so the professional clean buys the window; brushing holds it. Use a dog-specific enzymatic paste, never human toothpaste.

3. Backing it up - VOHC-accepted dental chews, plaque powders or water additives help between brushings, but they are the supporting cast, not the lead.

Done properly, gums that were red and bleeding settle within weeks. Owners who keep it up often go years between dentals; owners who stop usually see the red line return within months.

Living with periodontitis

If bone loss has already begun, the plan changes shape. Treatment may involve cleaning and polishing, local antibiotic gel placed in pockets, or extraction of teeth that are beyond saving - and extraction sounds worse than it is. Dogs eat better after diseased teeth are removed, not worse, because the constant pain goes with them.

After that, the routine is the same as for gingivitis - brushing, checks and honest conversations with the vet at each annual visit. When to see a vet

Book a dental examination if gums are red or bleeding, breath is foul, teeth are loose or discoloured, your dog drops food or chews on one side, or there is any swelling on the muzzle or under the eye - facial swelling with dental disease can indicate an abscess, which needs prompt treatment, not waiting.

The bottom line

Red gums are reversible and bone loss is not, so book a dental exam while the problem is still gingivitis - then protect the investment by brushing daily with an enzymatic paste and checking the gum line monthly.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; AVMA - Pet Dental Care Resources; VCA Animal Hospitals - Periodontal Disease in Dogs.

FAQs

Can gingivitis in dogs go away on its own?

No. Plaque never stops forming, so untreated gingivitis either grumbles along or progresses to periodontitis. It reverses only with a professional cleaning plus ongoing daily brushing at home.

How fast does gingivitis turn into periodontitis?

It varies. Small breeds with crowded teeth can go from red gums to bone loss within a year or two, while larger dogs with brushed teeth may stay at the gingivitis stage indefinitely. That uncertainty is exactly why the early stage is the time to act.

Is anaesthesia really necessary for a dog dental clean?

Yes, for a genuine clean. The diseased plaque that matters sits below the gumline, and no dog tolerates instruments there while awake. Modern anaesthesia with pre-anaesthetic bloods and monitoring is routine and usually lower risk than living with untreated dental disease.

Can I see bone loss in my dog's mouth without an X-ray?

Not reliably. Receding gums and exposed roots are worrying signs you can spot, but the definitive answer comes from dental X-rays taken during a professional examination under anaesthesia.
