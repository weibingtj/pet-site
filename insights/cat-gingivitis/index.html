Cat Gingivitis: Why Gums Bleed and How to Stop It Early

Dental Care · 2026-10-23

Bottom line: Get a professional dental check first, then brush the gum line daily with enzymatic cat toothpaste and settle for three times a week - because gingivitis is the only reversible stage, and a red gum margin left alone becomes periodontitis that no amount of brushing can undo.

Cat gingivitis is inflammation of the gum line and the only stage of dental disease that can genuinely be reversed - daily brushing plus a professional scale and polish stops it, while leaving it alone lets it become periodontitis, which cannot be undone. A thin red rim where the gum meets the tooth, bad breath and a cat who drops hard food are the signs to act on.

Most owners never see early gingivitis, because cats hide mouth pain better than almost any other species. The first thing people actually notice is the smell, usually while the cat is sitting on their chest.

What is inflamed, exactly

Gingivitis means inflammation confined to the gingiva - the gum tissue right at the tooth margin. Plaque, a sticky film of bacteria, re-forms within 24 to 48 hours of being cleaned, and the gums react to it by turning red, swelling and bleeding easily.

The important detail is what has not happened yet: the fibres that attach gum to tooth are still intact. Nothing has been lost. That is why gingivitis is reversible and periodontitis is not, and why the distinction is worth the trouble of learning.

Signs your cat's gums are inflamed

  • A red line along the gum margin. Lift the lip at the side of the mouth in good light. Healthy cat gums are pale pink; inflamed ones are a deeper red, slightly swollen, and shine.
  • Bad breath that has crept up over months. Our guide to cat bad breath causes separates mouth problems from the ones starting elsewhere in the body.
  • Tartar - brown or yellow deposits sitting at the gum line, most obvious on the upper back premolars.
  • Eating changes. Chewing on one side, dropping hard kibble, preferring soft food, or a cat who comes to the bowl, looks at it, and walks away.
  • Pawing at the mouth, lip smacking or head shaking after meals.
  • Drooling, particularly drool tinged with blood.
  • Bleeding when you brush the teeth or when the cat chews a toy.

Gingivitis, periodontitis, stomatitis or resorption?

Owners, and a fair number of web pages, muddle four different diagnoses that need different treatment:

  • Gingivitis - red gum margin, no attachment loss. Reversible with plaque control and a professional clean.
  • Periodontitis - the attachment has broken down, so there is pocketing, gum recession, loose teeth and sometimes pus. Irreversible. Needs cleaning under anaesthetic, often extractions, and lifelong home care.
  • Stomatitis - severe inflammation spreading beyond the gum margin to the back of the mouth and throat. The pain is out of proportion to what you can see, and many of these cats eventually need most teeth removed. Cat stomatitis covers that picture in full.
  • Tooth resorption - not gum disease at all. Holes form in the tooth itself, often below the gum line, and the tooth becomes painful. Cat tooth resorption explains why extraction rather than filling is usually the answer.

A vet works out which one is in front of them by looking, probing, and usually taking dental x-rays, because much of the disease in cats sits under the gum where you cannot see it.

The home routine that reverses early gingivitis

Plaque is the target, and plaque is mechanical. No spray, additive or chew removes it as reliably as a brush.

1. Get a professional assessment first. If there is heavy tartar, pocketing or a resorptive lesion, brushing over it will not fix anything and may hurt. A scale and polish under anaesthetic gives you a clean start, and our guide to professional dental cleaning frequency covers how often that is realistic.

2. Use a cat toothbrush and enzymatic pet toothpaste. Never human toothpaste - fluoride and xylitol are both toxic to cats, and the foaming makes them hate the whole process. Enzymatic paste can be swallowed, which matters because you will not be rinsing.

3. Aim for daily, settle for three times a week. Daily brushing is what actually reverses gingivitis. Three times a week keeps it from returning. Once a week is decorative.

4. Brush the gum line, not the flat of the tooth. Angle the bristles at roughly 45 degrees so they sweep the sulcus, which is where the plaque does damage. Ten small circles per quadrant is enough.

5. Build tolerance over two weeks. Rub the muzzle with a finger, let the cat lick paste off your finger, brush one tooth, then a quadrant. Stop while it is still going well. How to brush your cat's teeth has the full sequence.

6. Keep chlorhexidine gel or a rinse as a backup for the days you cannot brush. It lowers the bacterial load; it does not remove plaque.

What does not work

Being blunt here saves money and disappointment:

  • Water additives on their own. Cats drink too little for the dilution to reach a useful level, and nothing in a bowl removes plaque mechanically.
  • Anaesthesia-free dental cleaning. Scaling above the gum line while the cat is awake leaves everything below it untouched, and it is stressful enough that many cats become harder to handle afterwards.
  • Antibiotics alone. They calm the inflammation for a week or two, then the gingivitis returns as soon as the course ends, because the plaque is still there.
  • Raw bones. They fracture teeth, and raw-fed cats shed more of the bacteria responsible for human infections.
  • Dental chews sized for dogs. Most cats chew them half-heartedly or not at all, and a cat with sore gums will not touch them.

Cats that get gingivitis more easily

  • FIV or FeLV positive cats, whose immune response to ordinary plaque is exaggerated.
  • Diabetic cats, because gum disease and poor glycaemic control make each other worse.
  • Senior cats, since years of untouched plaque accumulate, and arthritis can make holding the mouth open painful.
  • Flat-faced breeds such as Persians and Exotic Shorthairs, whose crowded, misaligned teeth trap plaque.
  • Cats fed only soft food, with no abrasive action at all.
  • Cats who have never had dental care - which is most cats over five.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: book a dental examination within days for a red gum margin, bad breath or visible tartar, and urgently if your cat stops eating, drools, has swelling on the cheek or under the eye, has a broken or discoloured tooth, or cries when the mouth is touched - and treat any cat who has eaten nothing for 24 to 48 hours as an emergency, because cats develop liver damage quickly once they stop eating.

The bottom line

Get a professional dental check first, then brush the gum line daily with enzymatic cat toothpaste and settle for three times a week - because gingivitis is the only reversible stage, and a red gum margin left alone becomes periodontitis that no amount of brushing can undo.

FAQs

Can cat gingivitis be cured without a vet visit?

Early gingivitis can be reversed with daily brushing, but only after a vet has confirmed there is no periodontitis, resorption or stomatitis underneath. If there is established tartar or pocketing, home brushing will not remove it and may be painful. A scale and polish under anaesthetic gives you a clean start, and brushing then keeps it that way.

How long does it take to reverse gingivitis in a cat?

Gums usually look noticeably better within two to three weeks of consistent daily plaque removal, and many cases resolve within a month. The inflammation returns within days if brushing stops, because plaque re-forms in 24 to 48 hours. This is a maintenance job rather than a one-off treatment.

Is human toothpaste safe for cats?

No. Human toothpaste contains fluoride and sometimes xylitol, both toxic to cats, and the foaming agents cause stomach upset. Cats also cannot rinse and swallow, so everything goes down. Use an enzymatic pet toothpaste in a cat-safe flavour such as poultry or malt, and use only a soft cat toothbrush or a finger brush.

Does dry food clean a cat's teeth?

Ordinary kibble does very little. Most cats swallow it whole, so it barely touches the tooth surface, and many kibbles are high in carbohydrate. Only diets with a specific veterinary dental claim, and ideally a larger kibble size designed to abrade, have evidence behind them - and none of them replace brushing.
