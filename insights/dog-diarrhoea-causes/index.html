Dog Diarrhoea: Common Causes and When to Call a Vet

General · 2026-10-16

Bottom line: Keep the dog hydrated, fast briefly only if it is a healthy adult, then feed small bland meals and log each stool - and call a vet today for blood, repeated vomiting, a bloated belly, a puppy or unvaccinated dog, or a suspected poison rather than waiting it out.

Most dog diarrhoea is a short, self-limiting episode caused by something the dog ate, and the two things that matter most are keeping the dog hydrated and knowing which red flags mean you stop waiting. Loose stools are a symptom, not a diagnosis, and the same presentation can mean a mild dietary upset or something that needs surgery within hours. This guide covers the common causes, a sensible 24 to 48 hour plan, and the signs that mean a vet today. It is educational only.

How to describe it usefully

When you call a vet, these details decide what happens next:

  • Consistency. Soft serve, watery, or normal form with an odd colour.
  • Frequency. Once in a day, versus six times.
  • Colour. Black and tarry, bright red streaks, or pale and greasy.
  • Content. Mucus, visible worms, undigested food, foreign material.
  • Behaviour with it. Bright and hungry, or quiet, painful and off food.
  • Exposure. Garbage, bins, dead animals, a new food or chew, boarding or the dog park.

Photograph the stool. It sounds unappealing, and it is genuinely one of the most useful things you can take to an appointment.

The common causes

Dietary indiscretion. By far the most frequent. Scavenged food, a sudden food switch, too many treats, fatty table scraps or a new chew. Expect a soft-to-watery stool within 12 to 24 hours, a dog that still wants to eat, and clear improvement within a day or two.

Stress and change. Boarding, travel, a new home, a new pet or a large routine change can all produce loose stools with no infection involved. Cats show a parallel version of this after a move - see moving with a cat - stress plan for how that picture looks.

Parasites. Roundworm, hookworm, whipworm, giardia and coccidia are common, especially in puppies and in dogs from kennels or rescues. Often intermittent rather than constant, and sometimes with mucus but a completely normal appetite.

Bacterial and viral infection. Parvovirus in an unvaccinated dog is the emergency version - sudden severe bloody diarrhoea with vomiting and a very flat dog. Campylobacter, salmonella and clostridial overgrowth are also seen, and some are a human health concern too.

Food intolerance or allergy. Usually chronic and intermittent rather than one explosive episode. Itching, ear problems and recurring loose stools together point this way. Our guide to dog food allergy signs covers how that is actually investigated.

Medication and toxins. Antibiotics, anti-inflammatories and some parasite treatments cause loose stools. So do many human foods - chocolate, grapes, raisins, xylitol, onions and garlic - and those are emergencies, not wait-and-see cases.

Underlying disease. Pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease, liver disease, kidney disease and intestinal tumours tend to show up in older dogs, or in dogs with weight loss and a chronic pattern.

A sensible 24 to 48 hour plan

1. Check hydration first. Gums should be moist and pink, pressing the gum should refill within about two seconds, and the skin on the back of the neck should spring back when lifted. If any of that fails, call your vet rather than waiting.

2. Withhold food briefly, not water. A 12 to 24 hour fast is often suggested for a healthy adult dog with a mild upset. Never withhold water. Puppies, small breeds, seniors and dogs with other health conditions should not be fasted without veterinary advice.

3. Then feed small bland meals. Plain cooked chicken or lean turkey with white rice, or a prescription gastrointestinal diet, in four small meals across the day. For puppies, use a proper commercial recovery diet rather than home cooking, which is rarely balanced enough for a growing dog.

4. Consider a vet-approved probiotic. Specific strains have evidence for shortening mild episodes. Ask which product, and follow the label rather than doubling the dose.

5. Stop the rich extras. No table scraps, no new chews, no dairy and no fatty treats while the gut settles. Keep food boring for three to five days after stools return to normal, then transition back over a week.

6. Keep a simple log. Time, consistency, colour, frequency and appetite. If you end up at the vet, this beats your memory of events every time.

Red flags that mean now, not later

  • Blood in the stool, bright red or black and tarry.
  • Repeated vomiting alongside the diarrhoea, or a dog that cannot keep water down.
  • A puppy under six months, an unvaccinated dog, or a dog on immune-suppressing treatment.
  • A very flat, weak or unresponsive dog, or pale gums.
  • A painful, bloated or hard abdomen.
  • Suspected toxin or foreign body - chocolate, xylitol, grapes, raisins, or a swallowed toy.
  • Diarrhoea lasting more than 48 hours in an adult, or more than 24 hours in a puppy.
  • Weight loss, weeks of intermittent diarrhoea, or much more drinking and urinating than normal.

For the other end of the picture, our guide to dog vomiting - common causes covers what to watch when the stomach is involved instead of the gut. A measuring jug for food portions is also a quietly useful purchase, because overfeeding is one of the most common triggers that never gets identified.

When to see a vet

Call your vet the same day if there is blood in the stool, repeated vomiting with diarrhoea, a bloated or painful belly, a puppy or unvaccinated dog affected, a very flat dog, or a suspected poison - and book an appointment if the diarrhoea lasts beyond 48 hours, keeps coming back, or comes with weight loss.

The bottom line

Keep the dog hydrated, fast briefly only if it is a healthy adult, then feed small bland meals and log each stool - and call a vet today for blood, repeated vomiting, a bloated belly, a puppy or unvaccinated dog, or a suspected poison rather than waiting it out.

Sources: VCA Animal Hospitals - Diarrhea in Dogs; AAHA - Canine Life Stage and Preventive Care Guidelines; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Gastrointestinal Disease in Dogs.

FAQs

Should I starve my dog with diarrhoea?

A short 12 to 24 hour fast is often suggested for a healthy adult dog with a mild upset, but it is not mandatory and it is not appropriate for puppies, small breeds, seniors or dogs with other health conditions. Never withhold water. Most vets now prefer small bland meals over a long fast, because the gut recovers better with something gentle in it.

What can I feed a dog with diarrhoea?

Plain cooked chicken or lean turkey with white rice, in four small meals a day, is the traditional home option. A prescription gastrointestinal diet is more complete and is the safer choice for puppies. Stop all table scraps, dairy, fatty treats and new chews while the gut settles, and keep the diet boring for three to five days after stools return to normal.

When is dog diarrhoea an emergency?

Treat it as an emergency if there is blood in the stool - bright red or black and tarry - or repeated vomiting, a bloated or painful abdomen, a puppy under six months, an unvaccinated dog, a very flat or unresponsive dog, pale gums, or a suspected poison or swallowed object. Call your vet straight away in those cases rather than waiting for 48 hours.

Can I give my dog human anti-diarrhoea medicine?

No, not without veterinary advice. Human anti-diarrhoea products contain ingredients that are not dosed for dogs and that can be harmful, and some contain opioids or salicylates. They also mask symptoms that your vet needs to see in order to work out the cause. Keep human medication away from dogs and ask your vet what is safe.
