Dog Ear Infection: 6 Signs and When to See a Vet

Grooming, Paws & Ears · 2026-10-03

Bottom line: Ear infections are a top vet visit reason, and early signs — head shaking, odor, redness — are easy to catch. Drying ears after water and treating the underlying allergy prevent repeat infections; pain or swelling means see the vet.

A dog ear infection is one of the most common reasons pets end up at the vet, and the early signs are easy to miss if you only look once your dog is obviously uncomfortable. Catching it early usually means a simple drop-and-clean course at home — wait too long and it becomes painful, messy, and expensive.

Why dogs get ear infections

A dog's ear canal is shaped like an L, which traps moisture and debris better than a human ear ever could. Add floppy ears, swimming, allergies, or ear mites, and you have a warm, damp space where yeast and bacteria thrive. That's why some breeds — spaniels, retrievers, poodles, basset hounds — land in the clinic far more often.

The 6 signs to watch for

1. Head shaking or tilting — the classic early tell that something itches deep in the canal.

2. A sour or yeasty odor — healthy ears shouldn't smell. A foul scent is a strong infection signal.

3. Redness and swelling — look at the inner flap; inflammation means trouble.

4. Brown, black, or yellow discharge — color hints at the cause (dark waxy = yeast or mites; yellow = bacteria).

5. Scratching at the ears or rubbing on furniture — self-soothing behavior you can spot early.

6. Pain or sensitivity — yelping when the ear is touched, or pulling away, means it hurts.

What usually causes them

  • Moisture — baths, swimming, or humidity left in the canal.
  • Allergies — food or environmental allergies are a leading underlying driver.
  • Anatomy — floppy or hairy ears trap air and moisture.
  • Mites or foreign bodies — more common in puppies and outdoor dogs.

At-home care vs the vet

Mild, routine cleaning with a vet-approved solution is fine for maintenance. But once an infection is established, a proper diagnosis matters: the wrong drops on a ruptured eardrum can do real damage. Your vet will often take a swab to see whether the culprit is yeast or bacteria, then prescribe the right medication.

When to see a vet

See a vet if you notice heavy discharge, a foul smell, swelling, pain when the ear is touched, loss of balance, or symptoms that worsen or stay in one ear. Don't pour in random drops first — some ear medications are unsafe if the eardrum is compromised. This is guidance for spotting problems early, not a treatment plan; only your veterinarian can diagnose and treat an infection.

Preventing the next one

Dry ears thoroughly after swimming or baths, clean on your vet's schedule (not daily — over-cleaning irritates), and address underlying allergies. Dogs with chronic issues often need a maintenance routine rather than a one-off fix.

Sources: VCA Animal Hospitals — Ear Infections (Otitis Externa) in Dogs; AAHA — Canine Ear Care Guidelines.

FAQs

How do I know if my dog's ear infection is serious?

Serious signs are a foul smell, heavy discharge, swelling, pain when touched, loss of balance, or symptoms confined to one ear that worsen. Mild itching without those is often early — but if it lasts more than a day or two, a vet check is wise.

Can I use coconut oil or home remedies for my dog's ears?

It's best not to. Home remedies can trap moisture or irritate, and without knowing whether the cause is yeast or bacteria you may treat the wrong thing. A vet-approved cleanser and, when needed, prescription drops are the reliable path.

Why does my dog keep getting ear infections?

Recurring infections usually trace back to an underlying driver — most often allergies, excess moisture, or ear anatomy. Managing that root cause (allergy care, thorough drying, regular vet checks) is what breaks the cycle.

How does the vet treat a dog ear infection?

Typically a cleaning at the clinic, a swab to identify yeast versus bacteria, and prescription eardrops. Severe or deep cases may need oral medication. Following the full course — not stopping when it looks better — prevents quick relapse.
