Symmetrical Hair Loss in Dogs: Hormonal Causes Explained

Skin & Coat · 2026-10-27

Bottom line: Symmetrical, non-itchy hair loss in a dog usually has a hormonal cause, so skip the flea-and-shampoo guessing game and ask your vet for bloodwork - thyroid and adrenal testing find the cause in most cases, and treating it brings the coat back over months.

Symmetrical hair loss - the same pattern of thinning on both sides of the body, usually without any scratching - shifts suspicion away from fleas and allergies and towards a hormonal cause such as hypothyroidism, Cushing's disease or alopecia X. Fur disappears for two broad reasons: the dog destroys it by chewing and scratching, or the body stops growing it properly. When both flanks thin out in a mirror image and the exposed skin looks normal, quiet and unbothered, the hair cycle itself is the problem.

The fork in the road: itchy or not itchy

Start every hair-loss investigation with one question - is your dog scratching? Itchy hair loss means something on or in the skin is irritating it: fleas, mites, allergies, infection. Non-itchy, symmetrical thinning means the problem is hormonal. The distinction matters because the treatments could not be more different, and months of flea treatments and specialty shampoos will do nothing for a thyroid problem. If you are unsure which category your dog falls into, our guide to itchy skin in dogs covers the itchy side in detail, and shedding versus true hair loss explains what normal coat cycling looks like.

The hormonal usual suspects

1. Hypothyroidism - an underactive thyroid slows the whole hair cycle. The coat thins over both flanks, along the tail (the classic "rat tail") and behind the ears, sometimes with weight gain, sluggishness and a dull, scaly coat. Middle-aged dogs of breeds like golden retrievers, cocker spaniels and Dobermans are over-represented.

2. Cushing's disease (hyperadrenocorticism) - excess cortisol shuts down hair growth and thins the skin. The pattern is symmetrical with a pot-bellied look, increased thirst and urination, and sometimes calcium deposits in the skin. Middle-aged and older dogs, especially poodles, dachshunds and terriers.

3. Alopecia X - a coat-cycle problem seen mainly in plush-coated breeds such as Pomeranians, huskies, keeshonds and poodles. The coat falls out symmetrically and often turns jet black at the edges, while the dog stays completely well. It is cosmetic, not dangerous.

4. Sex-hormone-related hair loss - intact males with testicular tumours, and occasionally bitches with ovarian problems, can lose hair symmetrically. Neutering or treating the growth resolves it.

5. Pattern baldness - dachshunds, whippets and Italian greyhounds may simply grow thin hair on the ears, chest and belly from adulthood onwards. It is a normal variant, not disease, and needs no treatment.

How a vet narrows it down

No single test nails every cause, so vets work in layers. If you have photos of the thinning from a month or two earlier, take them along - the speed of progression is itself a diagnostic clue:

  • Skin scrapes and hair plucks (trichogram) come first, to rule out demodex mites and check whether hairs are broken (self-trauma) or shed whole with tapered roots, which points to a growth-cycle failure.
  • A general blood panel screens for the systemic fingerprints of thyroid and adrenal disease.
  • A thyroid panel confirms hypothyroidism, ideally interpreted alongside the dog's breed and clinical signs, because low thyroid readings alone are not proof.
  • Adrenal testing - a low-dose dexamethasone suppression test or an ACTH stimulation test - diagnoses Cushing's, since no single blood value is decisive.

What actually helps

Every cause has a real treatment, which is exactly why the diagnosis is worth chasing:

  • Hypothyroidism responds to daily levothyroxine, and the coat regrows over three to six months alongside the energy and weight improvements.
  • Cushing's is managed with medication that blocks cortisol production; the skin and coat recover gradually, and thirst and appetite normalise.
  • Alopecia X often regrows after neutering, melatonin supplementation or simply time - vets may recommend any of these, and because the dog is healthy, doing nothing cosmetically is a legitimate choice.
  • Coat support - omega-3 supplements and gentle grooming with the tools in our skin & coat guide help the regrowing coat look its best, but supplements alone will not restart a hormonally stalled hair cycle, so treat the diagnosis first and groom for finish.

One warning worth its own line: never give hormone tablets or "coat pills" without a diagnosis. Steroids, in particular, cause the very hair changes they are sometimes used to treat.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: take a symmetrical, non-itchy hair-loss pattern to your vet for diagnostics rather than another round of flea treatments - and go promptly rather than routinely if the thinning comes with increased thirst or urination, a sagging belly, weight change, low energy or skin that tears easily, because Cushing's and hypothyroidism both progress quietly but respond well to treatment.

The bottom line

Symmetrical, non-itchy hair loss in a dog usually has a hormonal cause, so skip the flea-and-shampoo guessing game and ask your vet for bloodwork - thyroid and adrenal testing find the cause in most cases, and treating it brings the coat back over months.

FAQs

Can hormonal hair loss in dogs grow back?

Yes, in most cases. Once hypothyroidism or Cushing's is treated with the right medication, the coat typically regrows over three to six months. Alopecia X may also regrow with neutering, melatonin or time, though some dogs keep the thin coat without harm.

What does alopecia X look like?

It appears as symmetrical coat loss on the body, often sparing the head and legs, with darkly pigmented skin in the bald areas. It mainly affects plush-coated breeds like Pomeranians, huskies and poodles, and the dog is otherwise completely healthy.

Is symmetrical hair loss ever just old age?

Age slows the coat a little but does not produce bald patches. Older dogs with symmetric thinning still deserve bloodwork, because hypothyroidism and Cushing's are both diseases of middle and older age and both are treatable.

Should I change my dog's food for hair loss?

Diet alone will not fix a hormonal hair-cycle problem, though a complete, balanced diet with adequate omega-3s supports regrowth once the underlying cause is treated. If the thinning is symmetrical and not itchy, testing comes before any food change.
