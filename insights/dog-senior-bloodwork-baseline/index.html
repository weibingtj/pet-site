Senior Dog Bloodwork: What the Numbers Actually Mean

General · 2026-11-16

Bottom line: Start a senior bloodwork baseline around age seven to nine and repeat it every six to twelve months, because the trend across panels matters more than any single number. Always include the urinalysis, keep copies of every result, and call your vet promptly when new symptoms appear between visits.

Bloodwork is the single most useful thing you can do for a senior dog, because it catches organ changes months before symptoms appear. But a number on a page means very little on its own. Here is what a senior panel actually checks, and how to read it in context.

When to start and how often

Most vets suggest a baseline panel around age seven for large breeds and age eight to nine for small breeds, then a repeat every six to twelve months. The value is not any single test. The value is the trend. A creatinine of 1.6 that has been stable for three years is a very different fact from a creatinine that jumped from 1.1 to 1.6 in six months.

If your dog is on long-term medication, including NSAIDs, seizure drugs, or thyroid replacement, the schedule is tighter and usually includes drug-level checks alongside the standard panel. Puppies and young adults rarely need a full chemistry panel, so this is genuinely a senior-phase conversation to have with your vet.

What a standard senior panel includes

1. Complete blood count (CBC) looks at red cells, white cells, and platelets. It screens for anaemia, infection, inflammation, and some cancers.

2. Chemistry panel covers liver enzymes, kidney values, blood glucose, and electrolytes. This is where early kidney and liver changes show up.

3. Thyroid testing is routine in dogs, since hypothyroidism is common and explains weight gain, coat changes, and low energy.

4. Urinalysis is often skipped by owners but is essential. It tells you how well the kidneys concentrate urine and catches infection, crystals, and early protein loss.

5. Focused tests as needed, including pancreatic values, a heartworm and tick-borne disease screen, and blood pressure.

One limitation is worth stating plainly: liver and kidney values can look normal until a large share of function is already lost. Normal bloodwork is good news, but it is not a guarantee of a clean bill of health.

Reading results without panicking

A result flagged high or low is a prompt to look further, not a diagnosis.

  • Mild elevations are common and often meaningless. Stress, dehydration, a recent meal, and even an exciting car ride can shift values.
  • The pattern matters more than any one line. A small rise in a single liver enzyme with everything else normal is often monitored. A rise across several related values gets investigated.
  • Reference ranges are statistical. Roughly five percent of healthy dogs fall outside the normal range on any given test by chance alone.
  • The trend beats the snapshot. This is why keeping copies of every panel matters. Ask for a printed or emailed copy each time.

Bring context to the appointment: what your dog eats, what medications and supplements it takes, how much water it drinks, and any change in energy or appetite. Those details often explain a number that would otherwise look alarming.

What you can do between visits

You cannot reverse most age-related organ changes, but you can make early detection easier and daily life better.

  • Track water intake and weight at home. Both are cheap early warning systems. Our guide on how much water a dog should drink shows practical ways to measure it.
  • Do a monthly body condition check so slow weight loss does not go unnoticed.
  • Keep a current medication list and bring it to every visit.
  • Feed for life stage. Senior diets are not pure marketing; some are genuinely lower in phosphorus and sodium, which supports kidney and heart health.

Routine maintenance for older dogs, from nail care to comfort changes around the house, is covered in our senior dog care guide. And remember that a sudden drop in energy is not always just ageing: our article on dog pain signs covers the subtle clues owners tend to miss.

What this is not

This article is general education about what these tests measure, not veterinary advice about your dog's results. Bloodwork has to be interpreted against your dog's history, physical exam, and other findings. Do not start, stop, or change any medication or supplement based on a number you read online.

When to see a vet

Contact your vet promptly if your senior dog is drinking or urinating much more than usual, vomiting, losing weight, has a sudden change in appetite or energy, or collapses. Do not wait for the next scheduled panel. Routine screening is for catching change early, not for symptoms that are already present.

The bottom line

Start a senior bloodwork baseline around age seven to nine and repeat it every six to twelve months, because the trend across panels matters more than any single number. Always include the urinalysis, keep copies of every result, and call your vet promptly when new symptoms appear between visits.

Sources: AAHA Senior Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; AVMA: Senior Pet Preventive Care; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine: Senior Dog Health Screening.

FAQs

How often should a senior dog have bloodwork?

Most healthy seniors do well with a panel every six to twelve months. Dogs on long-term medication, or with a known organ condition, are usually tested every three to six months instead.

Is fasting required before bloodwork?

Often yes, especially for glucose and lipid values. Ask your vet when you book the appointment, because a recent meal can raise some results and make them harder to interpret.

Can normal bloodwork still mean my dog is sick?

Yes. Organ values can stay normal until a large amount of function is lost, and many conditions, including most cancers and joint disease, do not show up on routine bloodwork at all.

What if one value comes back slightly high?

A single mildly abnormal value is common and often harmless. Your vet will usually repeat the test or add a focused test rather than start treatment based on one number.
