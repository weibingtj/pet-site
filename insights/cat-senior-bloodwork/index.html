Senior Cat Bloodwork: What to Test and When to Start

General · 2026-11-23

Bottom line: Senior cat bloodwork is prevention that pays off: establish a baseline around age seven to ten and repeat it every six to twelve months, because kidney disease and hyperthyroidism are far easier to manage when bloodwork finds them before symptoms do.

Senior cat bloodwork is the single most useful screening you can book, because cats hide illness until it is advanced. A baseline around age seven to ten, repeated every six to twelve months, can catch kidney disease and hyperthyroidism months before you would notice any sign at home.

When to start and how often

Most vets suggest a first baseline around seven to nine years of age, then a recheck every six to twelve months. A cat with a chronic condition, on long-term medication, or due for anesthesia is usually tested more often. Earlier testing is never wasted: having an old result to compare against is what turns a single number into a meaningful trend.

Why this matters more for cats

Cats are experts at looking fine while they are sick. Two of the most common diseases of older cats, chronic kidney disease and hyperthyroidism, can quietly progress for months before a cat drinks more, loses weight, or changes behavior. By the time the signs are obvious, valuable time has been lost. Bloodwork, together with a urine test and blood pressure check, finds these problems in their early, more manageable stages. Our senior dog bloodwork guide covers the same idea in dogs, but feline screening puts extra weight on the thyroid and kidneys.

What a senior feline panel includes

1. Complete blood count (CBC) checks red and white cells and platelets, screening for anemia, infection, and some cancers.

2. Chemistry panel covers kidney values (creatinine, BUN, and often SDMA), liver enzymes, electrolytes, and glucose.

3. Thyroid (T4) is routine in senior cats because hyperthyroidism is common and very treatable.

4. Urinalysis shows how well the kidneys concentrate urine and catches infection, crystals, and early protein loss.

5. Blood pressure is quick and important, because high pressure can damage the eyes, kidneys, and brain.

6. Fructosamine or glucose is added when diabetes is a concern, since a stressed cat's glucose can spike in the clinic.

Read the trend, not one number

A value sitting just outside a reference range is common and often harmless. What matters more is direction over time: a kidney value that has crept up over a year tells a different story from one stable number. SDMA can rise earlier than creatinine and is worth asking about. Keep in mind that hydration, a recent meal, and even the lab's reference range shift the picture, so a single abnormal flag is usually a reason to repeat or add a focused test, not to panic. Normal results are reassuring, but routine panels do not screen for every cancer or joint disease.

What the exam around the bloodwork should cover

Bloodwork is one piece. At the same visit, ask your vet to check body weight and body condition, since quiet weight loss is one of the clearest signals in cats. A dental check, a coat and skin once-over, and a heart and abdominal exam round it out. At home, keep a simple log of weight, appetite, water intake, and litter box habits. Those notes are often what help your vet interpret the numbers.

The three findings vets watch most

Kidney values are the big one. Creatinine, BUN, and SDMA together tell a vet how well the kidneys filter, and a rise across repeated tests matters more than one high reading. Early kidney change is managed with diet, hydration, and monitoring, all of which work better when caught before appetite drops.

Thyroid (T4) flags hyperthyroidism, which often drives weight loss, a racing heart, and restlessness in older cats. It is one of the more treatable senior diseases, with medication or other options your vet can explain.

Glucose and urine together sort out diabetes. Because clinic stress can push a cat's blood sugar up for the moment, your vet will read glucose alongside urine and sometimes fructosamine before making the call. None of these findings is a diagnosis on its own; each is a prompt for a conversation and, often, a focused recheck.

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit promptly if your cat is losing weight despite eating, drinking or urinating much more than usual, vomiting regularly, hiding more, or showing a sudden change in appetite or temperament; these can signal kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, or diabetes that needs treatment now. Seek care the same day for repeated vomiting, difficulty breathing, collapse, seizures, or a cat that cannot keep water down, as these can be emergencies. A routine panel is prevention; new signs are a reason to be seen sooner.

Making the visit easier

Cats stress easily in carriers, so a little preparation helps you get an accurate reading. Practice short carrier sessions at home, use a familiar blanket, and ask your clinic about a quiet room or a feline-friendly wait. Our cat carrier stress plan walks through a two-week plan that makes travel calmer. Bring your home log and any previous results so the comparison is immediate. If you use supports from our shop for routine care, mention them to your vet so the whole picture is clear.

The bottom line

Senior cat bloodwork is prevention that pays off: establish a baseline around age seven to ten and repeat it every six to twelve months, because kidney disease and hyperthyroidism are far easier to manage when bloodwork finds them before symptoms do.

Sources: AAHA Senior Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Senior Cat Health; VCA Animal Hospitals - Senior Cat Wellness Care.

FAQs

At what age should a senior cat start bloodwork?

Most vets recommend a first baseline around seven to nine years, then testing every six to twelve months. Cats with chronic illness or on long-term medication may need it sooner and more often. Starting early gives you a comparison point, which is what makes later results meaningful.

Does my cat need to fast before bloodwork?

Often a short fast of eight to twelve hours is requested, especially for glucose and lipid values, but this varies by clinic and by your cat's condition. Ask when you book. Never withhold water, and check with your vet before fasting a diabetic cat or a sick cat.

Can normal bloodwork still miss a serious problem?

Yes. Routine panels do not detect every cancer, and joint disease or early heart problems may not show up. Kidney values can also stay in range until a large share of function is lost. That is why your vet pairs bloodwork with urinalysis, blood pressure, and a physical exam.

What is SDMA and why is it tested in cats?

SDMA is a marker of kidney function that can rise earlier than creatinine, sometimes before other values shift. It is a useful addition to a senior feline chemistry panel because it can flag declining kidney function sooner, giving you and your vet more time to act.
