Senior Dog Care: What Changes After Age 7 and How to Help

General · 2026-10-13

A dog is generally considered senior from about 7 years in large breeds and 8 to 10 in small ones, and the most useful change you can make is to move from reactive care to a vet check-up every six months with bloodwork and a urinalysis. At home, weigh your dog monthly, check the ribs and waist, and watch how easily they rise, jump and climb stairs. Most of what looks like "just old age" is treatable pain, dental disease or organ changes caught early.

When a dog counts as senior

Ageing is not one number, because body size sets the clock. As a rough guide:

  • Small breeds under about 20 lb (9 kg) - senior from around 8 to 10 years.
  • Medium breeds between 20 and 50 lb (9 to 23 kg) - senior from around 7 to 8 years.
  • Large breeds between 50 and 90 lb (23 to 41 kg) - senior from around 6 to 7 years.
  • Giant breeds over 90 lb (41 kg) - senior from as early as 5 to 6 years.

The practical point is that a Great Dane and a Chihuahua of the same calendar age are not the same patient. The bigger the dog, the earlier prevention needs to start.

What actually changes with age

Older dogs change in predictable ways, which is exactly what makes early detection possible.

  • Joints. Cartilage thins, arthritis pain becomes common, and muscle mass drops. Slower stairs and stiffness after rest are the first clues.
  • Teeth. Periodontal disease is one of the most common problems in older dogs, and it hurts more than owners expect.
  • Kidneys and liver. Function declines quietly. Bloodwork and urine tests pick it up long before symptoms appear.
  • Heart. Murmurs and valve disease become more common from middle age onwards.
  • Metabolism and thyroid. Weight gain, or unexplained weight loss, both deserve a test.
  • Eyes and ears. Cloudy lenses, reduced night vision and gradual hearing loss change how a dog navigates the house.
  • Brain. Cognitive dysfunction affects a large share of dogs over 11 and looks like night waking, staring at walls, getting stuck in corners or new accidents indoors.
  • Immune system. Older dogs fight infection less efficiently and recover more slowly.

The twice-a-year check-up

Six months in a senior dog is roughly the equivalent of two to three human years, which is why annual visits are not enough from middle age onwards. Ask specifically for:

  • A full physical examination including weight and body condition score.
  • Blood count and biochemistry, to check kidneys, liver, blood sugar and proteins.
  • A urinalysis, which is the cheapest early warning for kidney disease and urinary infection.
  • Blood pressure, especially if a murmur has been heard.
  • A dental assessment with a plan for any cleaning or extractions.
  • A thyroid panel if there is weight change, coat change or lethargy.
  • Chest X-rays or an abdominal ultrasound if your vet suggests them.

Take a written list of what you have noticed at home. Behaviour and appetite changes that seem trivial to you can be the clue that reframes the whole visit.

What you can track at home

1. Weight, monthly, on the same scale. A slow drift down or up is one of the earliest signs of trouble in an older dog.

2. Body condition. You should be able to feel the ribs under a thin fat layer and see a waist from above. Ask your vet to show you a 4 or 5 out of 9.

3. Mobility. Watch your dog rise from rest, jump into the car, climb stairs, and whether they lag behind on walks or sit down mid-walk. Those are pain signs, not laziness.

4. Water and urine. A sudden increase in drinking or urinating is a red flag for kidney disease, diabetes or a urinary infection.

5. Appetite and chewing. Dropping food, chewing on one side, drooling or reluctance to take hard food points at the mouth.

6. Nights. Pacing, whining, staring or accidents overnight are common in cognitive decline and worth reporting.

7. Lumps. Run your hands over your dog monthly and measure any lump with a ruler. Fast growth or a change in texture needs a vet, not a wait.

8. Nails, ears and skin. Long nails change gait, and older dogs often cannot reach to scratch an itchy ear.

Small home edits that make a big difference

Most of these cost very little and change an older dog's day noticeably.

  • Traction. Rugs or matting over hard floors, because slipping makes a sore dog stop moving, and stopping moving makes the soreness worse.
  • Ramps and steps. For the car, the sofa and the bed, so they are not jumping down onto stiff joints.
  • Raised bowls. Easier on the neck and shoulders if there is arthritis.
  • Warm, padded bedding away from draughts, in a room you actually use in the evening.
  • Shorter, more frequent walks instead of one long one, plus a warm-up lap at the start.
  • A night light if vision is fading, and keeping furniture in the same place.
  • Lean body weight. Keeping an older dog slim is the single most effective thing you can do for their joints.

When to see a vet

Book an appointment if your dog is slowing down, stiff after rest, reluctant on stairs, losing or gaining weight without a diet change, drinking or urinating much more, has bad breath or a red gumline, is coughing, fainting or tiring quickly on normal walks, is confused or awake at night, or has any new lump. Same-day care is needed for collapse, difficulty breathing, a distended abdomen, repeated vomiting, a dog that cannot settle or is crying, sudden hind-limb weakness, or a first seizure. This article is general education and not veterinary advice, and it cannot diagnose your dog.

The bottom line

Book a senior check-up every six months with bloodwork and a urinalysis, keep your dog lean, and treat "slowing down" as a medical clue rather than an unavoidable part of ageing - because arthritis, dental disease and early kidney changes are all far easier to manage when they are caught at the first visit. FAQs

At what age is a dog considered a senior?

It depends on size. Small breeds under about 20 lb (9 kg) are usually considered senior from 8 to 10 years, medium breeds from about 7 to 8, large breeds from 6 to 7, and giant breeds from as early as 5 to 6. The practical takeaway is to move to twice-yearly check-ups and routine bloodwork once your dog passes that threshold, because large dogs age faster and develop problems earlier.

My dog is slower on walks. Is that just ageing?

Usually not just ageing. Slowing down, stiffness after rest, hesitating on stairs and lagging behind on walks are the classic early signs of arthritis, which is very common in older dogs and very treatable. Weight control, joint-friendly flooring, adjusted exercise and pain relief from your vet can all help. Ask for a mobility assessment rather than accepting it as normal.

What tests should a senior dog have?

A thorough physical examination with a weight and body condition score, a blood count and biochemistry panel covering kidneys, liver and blood sugar, a urinalysis, and blood pressure. Your vet may add a thyroid panel, chest X-rays, or an abdominal ultrasound depending on what they find and what you have noticed at home. Doing these every six months is what turns a problem from an emergency into a manageable condition.

How can I tell if my older dog is in pain?

Look at movement and behaviour rather than sounds. Signs include stiffness when rising, reluctance on stairs or into the car, panting at rest, restlessness at night, a tucked tail, lip licking, eating more slowly, and being withdrawn or unusually clingy. Dogs rarely yelp with chronic pain. If you suspect pain, book an appointment - your vet can examine the joints and discuss options, and this article cannot assess your dog.
