Dog Dry Eye (KCS): Signs, Daily Drops and Long-Term Care

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-11-03

Bottom line: Ask for a Schirmer tear test the first time discharge keeps coming back, then commit to twice-daily cyclosporine plus lubricant drops for life; with a recheck every three to six months, most dogs keep useful vision for years.

Dry eye in dogs - keratoconjunctivitis sicca, or KCS - is a shortage of tears, not a passing irritation. The tear glands simply stop producing enough fluid. Left untreated the cornea thickens, darkens and scars, and dogs lose vision in months rather than years. The test is quick and cheap; the damage is what costs you, which is why an eye that has looked a bit dull for three weeks deserves a Schirmer test instead of another round of wait-and-see.

What the tear film actually does

Tears are not just water. They carry oxygen and glucose to a cornea that has no blood supply of its own, flush out dust and bacteria, and dilute the inflammatory cells that chew up surface tissue. Once production drops, the eye loses all three protections at once, the surface dries and ulcerates, and the body patches the damage with pigment and scar tissue that light cannot pass through. That is why KCS is a race: treatment cannot restore vision already lost to scarring, only protect what is left.

Signs most owners catch late

  • A dull, cloudy or dirty look to the eye that does not clear after blinking
  • Thick, sticky discharge, often yellow or green, that returns within hours of wiping
  • Frequent blinking, squinting or pawing at the face
  • Red, irritated conjunctiva that looks infected but never responds to antibiotic drops
  • Repeated corneal ulcers, especially the same eye twice
  • In long-standing cases, a brownish haze creeping across the eye surface

Because the eye is not dramatically painful in the early weeks, plenty of dogs are treated for "allergy" or "a bit of conjunctivitis" for months. Any dog with recurring discharge should have tear production measured, even if the eye does not look terrible.

Why the glands quit

The most common cause in dogs is immune-mediated destruction of the tear gland itself. The body attacks its own tissue, which is why the problem tends to be permanent and why it usually reaches both eyes eventually. Other contributors:

1. Breed predisposition - cocker spaniels, Cavalier King Charles spaniels, West Highland white terriers, shih tzus, pugs, English bulldogs and Lhasa apsos are over-represented.

2. Drugs - certain sulfa antibiotics and some long-term medications can suppress tear production.

3. Nerve damage - injury or surgery around the ear or jaw can interrupt the nerve supply to the gland.

4. Hormonal disease - hypothyroidism and diabetes are worth ruling out.

5. Chronic inflammation - long-standing conjunctivitis can damage the glands themselves.

Cats get KCS too, often alongside herpesvirus, and it is just as serious in them.

How it gets confirmed

A Schirmer tear test takes about a minute: a thin paper strip sits in the lower eyelid and the vet reads how far moisture travels. Normal dogs run above 15 mm per minute, under 10 mm is suspicious and under 5 mm is diagnostic. Most vets also stain the cornea to rule out an ulcer and may measure tear breakup time. None of it hurts, and it settles in one visit what three rounds of guessing cannot.

Treatment that actually works

Two things need to happen at once: replace what is missing, and tell the gland to keep working.

  • Cyclosporine or tacrolimus - twice-daily drops or ointment that calm the local immune attack and, in many dogs, restart some tear production. Improvement takes four to eight weeks and stopping usually means relapse.
  • Artificial tears and lubricant gels - used alongside the prescription, not instead of it. Plain saline is not a substitute; it evaporates too fast and adds no lubricating layer.
  • Antibiotics or serum - only when there is an ulcer or active infection, and only short term.

The single biggest reason treatment fails is stopping the drops once the eye looks better. KCS is managed, not cured.

A routine that fits a real day

1. Wipe discharge with a damp cotton pad, one direction, fresh pad per eye.

2. Instill the prescription drop, wait two minutes, then add artificial tears.

3. Log which eye and what time - double dosing and skipped doses are the two most common slips.

4. Recheck tear production every three to six months, sooner if the eye turns cloudy or red.

When to see a vet

Book an eye exam within 24 hours if the eye is squinting, cloudy, red or producing thick discharge. Treat sudden pain, a blue or white haze, or a visible dent in the eye surface as an emergency - corneal ulcers can perforate within a day. A dog that suddenly refuses to open one eye needs to be seen the same day, not next week.

The bottom line

Ask for a Schirmer tear test the first time discharge keeps coming back, then commit to twice-daily cyclosporine plus lubricant drops for life; with a recheck every three to six months, most dogs keep useful vision for years.

FAQs

Can dry eye in dogs be cured?

Rarely. Most cases are immune-mediated and permanent, so the goal is management: prescription drops to keep the gland working plus lubricants to protect the surface. Some dogs regain partial tear production on cyclosporine, but stopping treatment usually causes a relapse within weeks.

Is dry eye painful for my dog?

Yes, though many dogs hide it. A dry cornea ulcerates and burns, which shows up as blinking, squinting and pawing at the face. Once a dog is on lubricants and the surface heals, owners often report the dog is brighter and sleeps better.

Can I just use human eye drops?

Plain saline or human rewetting drops will not treat KCS. They evaporate too quickly and contain no medication to address the gland. Prescription cyclosporine or tacrolimus is what changes the course of the disease; artificial tears only support it.

How often should tear production be rechecked?

Every three to six months once stable, and sooner if the eye turns cloudy, red or starts discharging again. Tear production can drift down over time, and a dose adjustment is far easier than treating a new ulcer.
