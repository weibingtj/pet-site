Cat Tooth Resorption: Why Cats Lose Teeth and What Helps

Dental Care · 2026-10-17

Bottom line: Ask your vet for an oral exam plus dental radiographs rather than a visual check alone, because resorption is diagnosed under the gum - and if extractions are recommended, book them instead of waiting, since the lesion never heals on its own.

Feline tooth resorption is the single most common cause of tooth loss in cats, and it hurts even when the cat keeps eating normally. Enamel and dentine are eaten away at or below the gum line, the tooth becomes fragile, and the only reliable fix is dental work under anaesthetic - which is why spotting it early matters far more than any home remedy.

What resorption actually is

Tooth resorption, often written FORL for feline odontoclastic resorptive lesion, is the progressive breakdown of a tooth's hard tissue by the cat's own cells. Odontoclasts - the same cells that dissolve baby teeth in kittens - start working on a permanent tooth, and the damage usually begins at the neck of the tooth where the crown meets the gum.

Because that zone sits at or under the gum line, most of the destruction is invisible from outside. You may see nothing at all, or a small pink or brown defect where the crown looks bitten out. Radiographic surveys of cats put the prevalence somewhere between a quarter and more than half of adults, rising steadily with age, so this is routine feline dentistry rather than a rare disease.

Why cats hide it so well

Cats are built to keep eating. A cat with a painful mouth will shift food to one side, swallow kibble whole or drift quietly toward wet food rather than stop eating, and owners read that as fussiness instead of pain.

The second reason is timing. Resorption can run for months with no lesion visible above the gum. The tooth weakens from the inside, then one day it fractures while the cat is chewing something ordinary, and the fracture is what finally gets noticed.

Signs worth taking seriously

  • Chattering or grinding the jaw while eating, or a jaw that trembles when the cat is relaxed.
  • Dropping food, chewing on one side, or tossing the head sideways to get a bolus down.
  • A sudden preference for wet food in a cat that used to crunch kibble without trouble.
  • Bad breath that has changed in the last few months - see cat bad breath causes for the other things this can mean.
  • Pink, red or brown defects at the gum line, most often on a lower canine or premolar, sometimes with gum tissue growing over the tooth.
  • Drooling, pawing at the face, or flinching when the cheek is touched.
  • Weight loss or a scruffy coat from eating less than you think.

None of these proves resorption. All of them justify a proper mouth exam.

What the vet needs to do

A conscious exam is only the start. Most resorption is diagnosed on intraoral dental radiographs taken under general anaesthetic, and those films are not padding on the invoice. They answer three questions that change the treatment completely:

1. How much root is left, and whether it has fused to the surrounding bone.

2. Whether the lesion behaves like type 1, with a distinct root and often inflammation, or type 2, where the root is being replaced by bone.

3. Whether periodontal disease is running alongside it, which is very common.

Cats with resorption often have several affected teeth at once, and they frequently have inflammation beyond the teeth as well. If the whole mouth looks angry rather than a few isolated teeth, your vet may also be considering cat stomatitis, which is managed differently.

Treatment: what actually helps

There is no paste, supplement or water additive that reverses resorption. The tooth does not recover and the disease keeps progressing.

  • Extraction is standard for type 1 lesions and for any tooth with a healthy, distinct root. The entire root has to come out; fragments left behind cause ongoing pain and infection.
  • Crown amputation with gingival closure is used for type 2 lesions where the root has already been replaced by bone and is effectively part of the jaw. The crown is removed and gum closed over it. It sounds drastic, and in that specific case it is genuinely the kinder option.
  • Monitoring is not a treatment. Watching an early lesion in a cat with no pain signs can be reasonable. Watching a cat that has stopped crunching kibble is not.

Short-term pain relief matters and your vet will prescribe it. Do not reach for human painkillers - paracetamol and ibuprofen are toxic to cats at doses people consider tiny.

What you can do at home

You cannot stop resorption, but you can catch it earlier and keep the rest of the mouth healthier.

  • Book an annual mouth check and ask for a conscious oral exam, then follow the advice on radiographs. The dental care guide explains what a full dental assessment includes.
  • Brush if your cat tolerates it. It will not prevent resorption, but it lowers the plaque load that makes periodontal disease worse. Our guide to brushing a cat's teeth is built around cats that hate being handled.
  • Log eating changes. Write down the date your cat stopped eating hard food. That single note is genuinely useful in the consult.
  • Skip hard chews and bones in a cat with known lesions, because that is how fragile teeth fracture.
  • Keep a soft brush and cat-safe paste on hand rather than improvising with gauze and human toothpaste - the shop has the basics.

When to see a vet

Book a vet visit within days if your cat is dropping food, chewing on one side, chattering its jaw, drooling, flinching when the face is touched, or has visible pink defects at the gum line - and seek same-day care if there is facial swelling, a fractured tooth with exposed pulp, bleeding that will not stop, or a cat that has refused food for more than 24 hours.

The bottom line

Ask your vet for an oral exam plus dental radiographs rather than a visual check alone, because resorption is diagnosed under the gum - and if extractions are recommended, book them instead of waiting, since the lesion never heals on its own.

Sources: AAHA 2019 Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats; Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Feline Odontoclastic Resorptive Lesions; VCA Animal Hospitals - Tooth Resorption in Cats.

FAQs

Can cat tooth resorption be cured?

No. The tooth does not heal and the process does not reverse, so treatment means removing or reshaping the affected tooth rather than restoring it. What you can control is how early it is found and how much pain the cat lives with in the meantime. Most cats do very well once the painful teeth are dealt with.

Will my cat be able to eat without teeth?

Yes. Cats with few or no teeth usually eat dry food perfectly well, because they swallow kibble whole rather than grinding it. Gums harden and adapt within a few weeks. Many owners report a cat eating better after extractions than before, simply because the mouth no longer hurts. Soft food for the first week or two is all most cats need.

Is tooth resorption painful for cats?

Yes, particularly when the lesion reaches the pulp or the tooth fractures. Cats are extremely good at hiding mouth pain, so a cat that still eats is not proof of comfort. Chattering jaws, one-sided chewing, dropping food and a new preference for wet food are all reasonable evidence of discomfort.

How is resorption different from gum disease?

Gum disease starts with plaque at the gum line and attacks the tissue and bone that support the tooth. Resorption attacks the tooth itself, from the inside or at the neck, and is driven by the cat's own cells rather than by bacteria alone. The two very often occur together, which is why a full mouth assessment with radiographs matters more than a quick look.
