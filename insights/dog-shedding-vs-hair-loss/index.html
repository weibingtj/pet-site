Dog Shedding vs. Hair Loss: How to Tell the Difference

Skin & Coat · 2026-10-06

Bottom line: Shedding is the coat letting go of old hairs evenly all over; hair loss, or alopecia, shows up as bald patches, thinning, or red, irritated skin in one spot. Even heavy shedding is usually seasonal, but patches, itching, or skin changes are not, and they need a vet.

Bottom line: Shedding is your dog's coat letting go of old hairs evenly all over the body, messy but normal. Hair loss, or alopecia, shows up as bald patches, thinning, or red, irritated skin in one area. Even heavy shedding is usually seasonal; patches, itch, or skin changes are not, and they need a vet.

Shedding is normal, here is what it looks like

Every double-coated dog, and most single-coated ones too, sheds. It is how the coat renews itself. You will notice:

  • An even thin-out across the back, sides, and belly, not one bald spot.
  • Seasonal surges in spring and fall, when the undercoat blows out.
  • More hair on the brush than on the floor, if you brush regularly.
  • Healthy skin underneath, pink, not red, flaky, or wet.

Short-haired dogs shed year-round in small amounts. Long-haired and double-coated breeds such as Huskies, German Shepherds, and Golden Retrievers tend to blow coat in big waves. Neither pattern is a disease. Puppies and seniors shed a little differently, but the even, whole-skin rule still holds: if the skin under the thinning fur looks normal, it is almost certainly shedding.

Hair loss is different, here is the red flag

True hair loss does not follow the even, all-over pattern. Watch for:

1. Bald patches - a clear circle or area with no hair where there used to be some.

2. Thinning you can see skin through, especially on the flanks, tail base, or face.

3. Red, scaly, or black-spotted skin where the fur is missing.

4. Asymmetry - one ear, one leg, or one side losing hair while the other stays full.

5. Hair that comes out in clumps when you barely touch it, leaving bare skin.

If you can run a hand over the coat and feel bare, irritated skin where there shouldn't be any, that is alopecia, not shedding.

The 30-second at-home check

Before you worry, do this:

  • Part the coat with your fingers in several spots. Shedding shows even, short regrowth; hair loss shows pink or angry skin.
  • Check both sides. Shedding is usually symmetric; one-sided loss points to a local problem.
  • Note the itch. Shedding dogs are not usually itchy. If the bare area is also chewed, licked, or scratched raw, something is wrong.
  • Smell it. A yeasty or foul odor from a hairless spot means infection, not renewal.

Common causes of real hair loss

When hair loss is real, the cause matters, because the fixes are completely different:

  • Parasites - mange mites (demodex, sarcoptes) and fleas can cause patchy loss, often with intense itch.
  • Allergies - food or environmental allergies make dogs lick and chew until fur falls out, classically at the paws, belly, and armpits.
  • Infections - bacterial or fungal (ringworm) infections create scaly, crusted bald spots.
  • Hormonal issues - hypothyroidism or Cushing's disease cause symmetric thinning, often on the trunk, with normal-looking skin.
  • Stress or boredom - some dogs over-groom one spot, a lick granuloma, out of anxiety.
  • Pressure sores - bald, thickened skin over bony points in older or large dogs that lie on hard surfaces.

This is why guessing at home fails: the bare patch from mites needs a different treatment than the bare patch from hormones. Our skin and coat guide covers daily habits that keep the coat resilient, and the deshedding walkthrough helps manage the normal stuff.

What you can safely do at home

For plain shedding, you are managing volume, not a problem:

1. Brush regularly with a tool matched to the coat - undercoat rakes for double coats, slicker brushes for long hair.

2. Feed a balanced diet with adequate protein and omega-3s; coat quality reflects nutrition.

3. Control fleas year-round, since itch-driven scratching thins fur fast.

4. Use a humidifier in dry winter homes, where static and dry skin worsen the shedding appearance.

None of these treat alopecia. They make normal shedding manageable and rule out the easy, external causes.

When to see a vet

See a vet within a few days if you notice any of these: bald patches or thinning (not just loose hair); red, scaly, or black skin where fur is missing; a bad smell from a hairless area; hair falling out in clumps with little contact; itching, chewing, or licking at the bare spot; or hair loss paired with weight change, thirst, or lethargy. If the skin is open, bleeding, or rapidly spreading, treat it as sooner rather than later - many skin conditions are easier and cheaper to stop early, and only a vet can tell a parasite from a hormone problem.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. Skin issues share symptoms, so a proper diagnosis, often a scrape, swab, or blood test, beats weeks of home guessing.

Keeping the coat honest

The simplest rule: shedding is even and whole-skin; hair loss is patchy and angry-skin. Brush often, rule out fleas, and let a vet be the one to name a bald spot. Most 'my dog is losing hair' scares turn out to be normal coat turnover once you know what even looks like, but the ones that aren't deserve a professional eye. FAQs

Is it normal for my dog to shed a lot?

Often yes. Breed, season, and coat type drive shedding volume. Double-coated breeds shed heavily in spring and fall. It becomes a concern only when the loss is patchy, the skin underneath looks red or irritated, or the dog is also itchy.

How can I tell shedding from hair loss quickly?

Part the coat and look at the skin. Shedding shows even short regrowth over healthy pink skin. Hair loss shows bare, red, scaly, or black skin in a defined spot, and it is often one-sided or asymmetrical.

Will brushing stop my dog from shedding?

Brushing does not stop shedding, but it removes loose undercoat before it lands on your floor and lets you spot bare patches early. It manages the mess and gives you a weekly look at the skin.

Can stress cause my dog to lose hair?

Yes. Some dogs over-groom a single spot from anxiety, creating a lick granuloma with hair loss. But stress is only one cause, so a vet check is still the right move to rule out parasites, allergies, and hormone problems first.
