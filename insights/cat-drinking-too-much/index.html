Cat Drinking a Lot of Water: Common Causes and When to Worry

General · 2026-10-24

Bottom line: Track the bowl and the litter clumps for a few days, never restrict water, and book blood and urine tests within the week - a lasting increase in a cat's drinking is most often kidney disease, diabetes or thyroid disease, and all three respond far better when found early.

A cat that suddenly starts drinking noticeably more water should be treated as a medical symptom until blood tests say otherwise - the three most common causes, chronic kidney disease, diabetes and an overactive thyroid, are all frequent in cats over seven and all far easier to manage when caught early.

There is a natural tendency to shrug this off. The cat seems fine, eats well, plays as usual. But increased thirst is often the first and only sign of a serious condition, and cats are specialists at hiding illness until it is advanced.

How much water is normal

A typical adult cat drinks roughly 50 ml per kilogram of body weight per day - about a quarter of a cup for a 4 kg cat. That number swings widely with diet:

  • Wet-fed cats get most of their fluid from food (around 70 percent moisture) and may barely touch the bowl.
  • Dry-fed cats drink more, and several top-ups a day can be perfectly normal for them.
  • Hot weather and extra activity push the numbers up temporarily.

The threshold vets care about is roughly double the expected amount, sustained for days. Two practical signals are more reliable than maths: the bowl that empties much faster than it used to, and litter clumps that are bigger, heavier or more numerous. Some owners notice the cat standing at the bowl repeatedly, or drinking from taps, showers and puddles it previously ignored.

The three causes that matter most

1. Chronic kidney disease. The kidneys lose the ability to concentrate urine, so the cat urinates more and drinks more to replace it. It is extremely common in cats over seven and, with modern management, diagnosed cats often live comfortably for years.

2. Diabetes mellitus. Excess blood sugar spills into the urine and drags water with it. Thirst comes with weight loss despite a good or even increased appetite, and sometimes with a plantigrade stance (walking flat on the heels).

3. Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism). The metabolism races: increased thirst alongside a ravenous appetite, weight loss, restlessness, night-time yowling and a fast heart rate. Common in senior cats.

Less common but real causes include urinary tract disease, certain medications such as steroids, and a handful of hormonal conditions. Drinking more because of actual dehydration (vomiting, heat) is different again - the body compensating, not the kidneys misbehaving.

What the vet will do

The workup is straightforward: a blood panel and a urine test. The blood measures kidney values (creatinine and SDMA), blood glucose and thyroid hormone; the urine checks how concentrated it is and whether glucose is present. Together these usually identify the cause in a single visit, occasionally followed by one or two targeted confirmations.

Bring the details that make the pattern obvious: how long it has been going on, roughly how much the cat drinks per day, any weight change, and a photo of the litter clumps if the change is dramatic. Those observations genuinely speed up the diagnosis.

What not to do

  • Never restrict water to test or manage the problem. A cat that is drinking more because its body demands it will dehydrate quickly, and in kidney patients this accelerates the damage.
  • Do not switch food to fix it. More wet food is good for hydration in general, but it will not treat kidney disease, diabetes or thyroid disease.
  • Do not wait for vomiting or collapse. Waiting until the cat looks sick is how early-stage, very treatable disease turns into late-stage disease.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: book a same-week appointment for any lasting increase in drinking, and a same-day visit if the cat is also losing weight, vomiting, off food, unusually restless or crying at night, or straining in the litter box. Sudden extreme thirst with weakness or collapse is an emergency - severe diabetes or kidney failure can decompensate fast.

Can it be caught early

Yes, and this is where routine care pays. An annual blood and urine panel - twice a year from age ten - picks up kidney disease and thyroid problems before thirst becomes obvious. Keeping a rough eye on the water bowl and litter clump size is the free early-warning system between visits.

A few small changes also make drinking easier to monitor: a wide ceramic bowl away from the food station suits most cats better than a narrow one next to their dinner (whisker-friendly options like this are easy to find in the shop), and a measurable fill level turns guessing into data. For the wider pattern of change signals in cats, cat not eating, cat constipation and cat peeing outside the litter box cover appetite, bowel and urinary changes respectively - thirst belongs on the same watch list. The parallel logic in ageing dogs, including what routine bloodwork catches, is in senior dog care.

The bottom line

Track the bowl and the litter clumps for a few days, never restrict water, and book blood and urine tests within the week - a lasting increase in a cat's drinking is most often kidney disease, diabetes or thyroid disease, and all three respond far better when found early.

Sources: Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine - Feline Health Center: Chronic Kidney Disease; VCA Animal Hospitals - Increased Thirst and Drinking (Polydipsia) in Cats; Merck Veterinary Manual - Hyperthyroidism in Cats.

FAQs

How much water should a cat drink per day?

Roughly 50 ml per kilogram of body weight, so about 200 ml for a typical 4 kg cat, but wet-fed cats may drink much less because food supplies most of the fluid. The warning sign is a sustained doubling of the usual amount, or litter clumps that are clearly bigger and heavier than before.

Can a dry food diet make a cat drink more?

Yes, and that is normal. Cats eating only dry food must source almost all fluid from the bowl and typically drink several times more than wet-fed cats. What is not normal is a further increase beyond that baseline, which is why the change over time matters more than any absolute number.

Is increased thirst in older cats always kidney disease?

No. Diabetes and an overactive thyroid are the other two common causes, and they need different treatment, which is why blood and urine tests are essential rather than optional. Assuming one cause and managing at home delays the correct diagnosis.

When is a cat drinking too much an emergency?

Seek same-day care if heavy thirst comes with vomiting, refusal to eat, weakness, wobbliness or collapse, which can signal diabetic ketoacidosis or advanced kidney failure. Also go quickly if the cat stops urinating despite drinking a lot, because that suggests a blockage rather than a metabolic problem.
