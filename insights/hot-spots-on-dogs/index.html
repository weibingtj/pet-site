Hot Spots on Dogs: Fast Relief and When to See a Vet

Skin & Coat · 2026-10-02

Bottom line: Hot spots are painful, fast-spreading sores driven by an itch-lick-infection loop - they can double in size in a day. Clip and dry the area, block the licking, and see a vet if it's larger than a coin, oozing, or spreading.

A hot spot is a wound, not a rash

A hot spot - vets call it *pyotraumatic dermatitis* - is a patch of inflamed, infected skin that shows up fast and spreads faster. One day it is a small red mark; the next it is a raw, weeping sore the size of a coin or bigger. The engine behind it is simple: something itches, the dog licks or scratches, the skin breaks, bacteria move in, and the itch worsens - a loop that feeds itself within hours. This is general guidance, not a diagnosis; a vet should confirm any sore that is spreading or won't settle.

Why they appear so suddenly

Hot spots love a trigger and warm, humid conditions:

  • Allergies - the most common root cause, whether pollen, dust, or food. The itch starts the whole cycle.
  • Moisture trapped in the coat - a dog that goes to bed wet after a swim or bath is primed for it, especially in thick or long fur.
  • Parasites - fleas, mites, or ticks that set off scratching.
  • Ear or anal-gland trouble - a dog rubbing its face or scooting can open the skin.
  • Boredom or stress - a dog that licks one spot out of anxiety keeps it wet and angry. Our calm & behavior guide covers stopping the lick habit.

Safe first aid at home

For a small, fresh spot, you can slow it while you decide on the vet:

1. Clip the fur around it. Exposing the area to air is the single most useful thing you can do - trapped moisture is fuel. Use blunt scissors and go carefully.

2. Clean gently. A vet-style skin-relief spray or a mild antiseptic wipe cools the area; skip anything with alcohol, which stings and delays healing.

3. Block the licking. A bitter no-chew spray or an e-collar stops the self-trauma that makes it grow. The skin & coat guide lists options.

4. Keep it dry. No swimming, no baths until it is closed.

When to see a vet: Get your dog in if the spot is larger than a coin, oozing or bleeding, spreading hour to hour, or if your dog is in obvious pain, feverish, or miserably itchy. Hot spots can double in size in a day, and deep ones need prescription meds - oral antibiotics or anti-inflammatories - that you cannot get over the counter. Any sore with swelling, heat, or rapid growth belongs with a licensed veterinarian, not a home remedy.

How fast is 'fast'?

Faster than most owners expect. A hot spot can go from a faint red patch to a weeping sore in 12-24 hours, and a deep one can widen across an afternoon. That speed is exactly why the guidance above says 'if it is bigger than a coin or spreading, call the vet' rather than 'wait a few days.' Early home care is for small, fresh, non-spreading spots only.

What makes them worse

  • Letting the dog keep licking. This is the big one. Every lick re-wets and re-injures the skin; an e-collar for a few days often turns the corner.
  • Alcohol or hydrogen peroxide. They feel like they should help; they actually sting and slow healing.
  • Thick ointments that seal in moisture. A hot spot needs to dry out, not be smothered.

Where hot spots show up most

They favor warm, folded, or frequently-wet areas: the cheek and neck (from scratching an ear), the flank and hip (from licking a sore spot), the chest, and the base of the tail. Dogs with thick double coats - Golden Retrievers, Labs, German Shepherds - and dogs with chronic allergies are the usual patients. Knowing your dog's favorite zone means you check it first at the first sign of scratching.

Myth: hot spots mean a dirty dog

They don't. Hot spots strike well-groomed dogs every bit as often - it is about moisture, allergens, and the lick-scratch loop, not hygiene. Bathing more won't prevent them and can even trigger one if the coat stays damp. The lever is drying, not scrubbing.

How a vet treats a real hot spot

Once a spot passes the home-care line, the vet typically clips and cleans it properly, then prescribes what the cycle needs: a short course of oral antibiotics if it is infected, an anti-inflammatory to kill the itch and pain, and sometimes a medicated rinse. The relief is usually rapid once the itch is switched off - which is why owners often say the dog finally settled after the visit. The follow-up question is always the cause: fix the allergy or the moisture, and the spot doesn't come back.

The prevention that actually sticks

  • Dry the coat after swims and baths, especially in long or thick fur - a towel and a low dryer setting beat a damp bed.
  • Stay on top of fleas with a vet-recommended product; one bite can start the itch in a sensitive dog.
  • Manage the root allergy with your vet, because treating the spot without the cause just means it returns.
  • Break the lick loop early with a bitter spray or a job to do - chews, snuffle mats, a walk.

FAQs

Can I treat my dog's hot spot at home?

Only small, fresh, non-spreading spots: clip the fur, clean gently with a non-alcohol product, and block licking with a bitter spray or e-collar. If it is larger than a coin, oozing, or growing, see a vet - it likely needs prescription meds.

How do I stop my dog licking the hot spot?

A bitter no-chew spray teaches taste-aversion, and an e-collar physically blocks access for a few days - often the turning point. Distract with a chew or a walk so the dog has a job other than the sore.

Will a hot spot heal on its own?

A tiny, caught-early spot can dry and close once the licking stops, but most hot spots worsen without breaking the itch-lick loop. Left alone they spread fast, so monitor closely and call the vet if it grows.

Are hot spots contagious to other pets?

The spot itself is not contagious - it is your dog's own skin reacting to itch and bacteria. But if the trigger is fleas or a shared allergen, other pets in the home can develop their own hot spots from the same cause.
