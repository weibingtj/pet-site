Cat Squinting One Eye: Causes and When It Is an Emergency

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-10-20

Bottom line: Book a same-day or next-day vet visit for a squinting eye so the cornea can be stained and the pressure checked, keep the cat indoors with a cone if it is pawing, and never use leftover or steroid drops before an ulcer has been ruled out.

Squinting in a cat is a pain signal, not a sleepy habit - and one eye half closed deserves a vet visit within 24 hours, because corneal ulcers and uveitis can go from treatable to sight-threatening in a couple of days. If the eye is cloudy, the pupil looks different from the other side, or your cat is hiding and off food, call tonight rather than waiting for the morning.

Cats hide discomfort well, which is why a squint is easy to dismiss as a cat dozing in a sunbeam. It is not that. The eyelid is acting as a bandage, and it only does that when the surface underneath hurts.

What squinting is actually telling you

A cat squinting one eye is showing blepharospasm - an involuntary clamp of the eyelid muscles. It is a reflex, and it means the eye or the lids around it are painful. Signs that usually come with it:

  • Watery eye or thick yellow-green discharge.
  • Pawing at the face, or rubbing the eye along furniture and door frames.
  • The third eyelid sliding part way across the eye.
  • Light sensitivity, with the cat turning away from a window or lamps.
  • A fully closed eye that will not open at all, which is more serious than a partial squint.

One eye only is a useful clue. It points at something local - a scratch, a foreign body, a single infected eye - rather than a whole-body problem. Two squinting eyes with a runny nose is a different story, and usually means an upper respiratory infection.

The causes, most common first

1. Corneal ulcer - a scratch or erosion on the clear surface. Common after a cat fight, a stray claw, or a piece of grit. Very painful, and it can deepen into a melting ulcer within days.

2. Conjunctivitis - inflammation of the pink tissue around the eye, usually with discharge. Feline herpesvirus and chlamydia are frequent causes in cats.

3. Uveitis - inflammation inside the eye. The eye looks dull, the pupil is often small, and the cat may be quiet and off food.

4. Glaucoma - raised pressure inside the eye. The eye feels hard, may look enlarged, and the pain is severe. This is an emergency.

5. Foreign body - a grass seed, a hair, or a fragment trapped under the lid. Far more common in summer.

6. Dry eye - too little tear film, so the surface stays irritated. More common in older cats.

7. Eyelid abnormality - entropion, or a lid that rolls in and rubs the surface.

8. Trauma - a bite or a blow to the head, which can also damage the eye from behind.

If the eye is running but not squinting, that is a different picture - see cat watery eye before assuming pain. Corneal ulcers: the one that cannot wait

An ulcer is a loss of the surface layer of the cornea. It starts as a scratch and, untreated, can progress to a melting ulcer where the cornea actually dissolves - sometimes inside 24 to 48 hours.

Two things make ulcers dangerous in cats:

  • Feline herpesvirus is involved in a large share of cat ulcers and behaves differently from a simple scratch. It needs antiviral treatment, not just antibiotic drops.
  • Steroid drops make an ulcer dramatically worse. If an earlier visit left a steroid drop in your cupboard, do not reach for it. Steroids suppress healing and can let the cornea perforate.

Signs that push this from "book an appointment" to "go now": a cloudy or blue-grey eye, a white or yellow patch on the surface, a pupil that stays small while the other one is normal, or the cat pressing its head against a wall.

Cats at higher risk

  • Flat-faced breeds - Persian, Exotic Shorthair, British Shorthair - have prominent eyes in shallow sockets, so the cornea is more exposed to scratches and does not close as effectively.
  • Cats with a herpesvirus history. Once a cat carries FHV-1 it tends to recur when the cat is stressed, and the eye is often where it shows.
  • Outdoor cats, because fights and grass seeds are the two most common ways a cornea gets damaged.
  • Senior cats, who are more likely to have dry eye and less able to groom discharge away.

How a vet works out which one it is

You cannot tell these apart by looking at the eye from across a room. The examination that matters takes minutes:

  • Fluorescein stain - a dye that turns bright green where the surface is damaged. This is how an ulcer is confirmed.
  • Schirmer tear test - a paper strip that measures tear production.
  • Tonometry - a gentle pressure reading that rules glaucoma in or out.
  • Ophthalmoscope or slit lamp - to look inside the eye at the anterior chamber. A cloudy chamber means uveitis.
  • Conjunctival swab - if herpes or chlamydia is suspected.

What you can safely do tonight

  • Keep the cat indoors so the eye is not exposed to more grit, or to a second fight.
  • Do not use leftover drops from another pet or an old bottle. The wrong drug, an expired drug, or a steroid on an ulcer can all cause harm.
  • Do not squirt water in with a syringe if the cat is struggling; you can turn a scratch into a deeper injury.
  • Wipe discharge with a soft damp cloth, always outward towards the ear, never across the surface of the eye.
  • Stop the pawing with a cone if you have one. A cat can do more damage in an hour than the original injury did.
  • Note the timeline - when it started, which eye, whether other cats in the house are sneezing.

When to see a vet

When to see a vet: any cat squinting one eye should be examined within 24 hours, and the same day if the eye is cloudy, the colour has changed, the pupil is fixed or unequal, the head is painful to touch, or the cat is off food - a corneal ulcer or glaucoma can destroy vision in 24 to 48 hours, and steroid drops must never be used until a vet has stained the eye and ruled an ulcer out.

The bottom line

Book a same-day or next-day vet visit for a squinting eye so the cornea can be stained and the pressure checked, keep the cat indoors with a cone if it is pawing, and never use leftover or steroid drops before an ulcer has been ruled out.

FAQs

Can I wait until tomorrow if my cat is squinting one eye?

Tomorrow is usually acceptable if the eye looks clear, the cat is eating, and there is no discharge - but not if the eye is cloudy, the pupil is abnormal, or the cat is hiding and off food. Those signs mean a possible ulcer, uveitis or glaucoma, and hours matter. Call the clinic and describe what you see rather than deciding alone.

What does it mean if my cat keeps one eye half closed after sleeping?

A brief squint on waking is normal. A squint that lasts more than an hour, comes back through the day, or appears with watery discharge is not sleepiness - it is pain. Cats do not hold an eyelid closed for comfort; the reflex only fires when the surface of the eye is irritated or injured.

Can I use human eye drops on my cat?

No. Human preparations often contain preservatives, decongestants or steroids that are unsuitable for cats, and steroid drops can make a corneal ulcer far worse. Until a vet has stained the eye, the only safe action is wiping discharge away with a damp cloth and getting the cat examined.

Is a squinting eye always an emergency?

Not always, but it is always urgent enough to be seen within a day. Conjunctivitis is uncomfortable rather than dangerous, while an ulcer or glaucoma can cause permanent loss of the eye within 48 hours, and you cannot tell which one you are looking at without a stain and a pressure reading.
