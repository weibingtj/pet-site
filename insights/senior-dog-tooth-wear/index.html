Senior Dog Tooth Wear: When Worn Teeth Need a Vet Visit

Dental Care · 2026-11-10

Bottom line: Ask for a dental exam with radiographs before you blame old age, then switch to soft chews and brush the cheek teeth three times a week.

Flat, worn teeth in an older dog are common and often harmless, but wear that reaches the pulp, a tooth that has started to loosen, or a dog dropping food while it eats needs a veterinary exam rather than a harder chew toy. The wear pattern itself usually tells you which of those three you are dealing with.

Why senior teeth wear flat

Enamel is the hardest tissue in the body and it does not regrow. A dog spends a decade carrying sticks, chewing, grinding and gnawing, and the crown slowly loses height. By eight or nine years old a lot of dogs have visibly flattened incisors, blunted canine tips and premolars that look sanded down.

Two different processes create that look, and they matter because they have different consequences.

  • Attrition is tooth against tooth. It comes with age, with a misaligned bite, and in dogs that chew their own flank or paws because of itch.
  • Abrasion is tooth against something harder than enamel. Tennis balls, antlers, hooves, rocks, cage bars and some nylon chews all sit close to or above enamel hardness.

Attrition looks symmetrical and develops slowly over years. Abrasion tends to be uneven, with a notch or a groove that matches the shape of whatever the dog has been working on. If one side of the mouth is drastically flatter than the other, that is usually abrasion, not ageing.

When wear actually becomes a problem

A worn tooth is only a problem once the wear crosses the enamel and exposes the soft tissue underneath. Dog enamel is thin, often under a millimetre on the cusp tips, so a few years of hard chewing can get there. Once dentine is exposed the tooth becomes sensitive to temperature and pressure, and once the pulp chamber is breached, bacteria have a direct route into the tooth root.

There is a rough clinical rule: if you can see a pink or grey dot in the middle of a flattened cusp, the pulp is at or near the surface. That is not something to watch at home. It is also the point where a tooth often dies quietly, discolours, and shows up years later as a root abscess with facial swelling.

The signs owners miss

Dogs do not limp on a sore tooth. They adapt, and the adaptation is easy to read once you know what to watch for.

  • Chewing on one side only, or turning the head to favour a side
  • Dropping kibble, or carrying food to the back of the mouth
  • Chattering or clicking the jaw after a hard bite
  • Reluctance to pick up toys the dog used to love
  • Pawing at the muzzle or rubbing the face on furniture
  • Bad breath that returns quickly after brushing
  • Eating more slowly, or gulping without chewing at all

Any of these alongside visible flat teeth justifies a proper look, and it is worth logging when each one started so the pattern is clear at the appointment.

The chew cupboard problem

The instinct when teeth look worn is to stop all chewing, which is the wrong move. Chewing is how a dog maintains jaw muscle and how plaque gets disrupted. The fix is to change what is on offer, not to remove the activity.

  • Retire antlers, hooves, bones, nylon bones and hard rubber destined for power chewers
  • Retire the tennis ball as a chew toy. The fuzz holds grit and the felt surface acts like sandpaper
  • Choose flexible rubber toys that give under pressure, or a rope toy used under supervision
  • Choose soft dental chews sized to the dog, and check for a Veterinary Oral Health Council seal
  • Supervise everything, and throw out any toy that has been chewed to a sharp edge

What the vet will check

A worn mouth needs more than a glance. Expect a full oral exam under good light, with periodontal probing around every tooth to measure pocket depth and attachment loss. Radiographs are the part owners most often skip and the part that matters most, because most of the tooth sits under the gum and a worn crown can hide a dead root.

1. Probe depths to map any periodontal pockets

2. Full mouth radiographs to check roots, pulp and bone

3. Assessment of pulp exposure on each flattened cusp

4. Bite evaluation, since worn teeth can shift contact points

5. A cleaning plan, often staged, for a dog who cannot tolerate a long procedure

If a tooth is fractured rather than worn, the pathway is different and covered in broken tooth in dogs. Fractures expose pulp immediately, wear may take years.

Home care that still works for a worn mouth

Brushing remains the single best thing you can do, and it works on worn teeth as well as intact ones. Use a soft bristle brush or a finger brush, an enzyme pet toothpaste, and aim for the outer surface of the upper cheek teeth where plaque accumulates fastest. Three sessions a week holds ground; daily is better. The cadence that actually sticks for most owners is set out in how often to brush dog teeth.

For sensitive worn teeth, warm water on the brush instead of cold, no scrubbing pressure, and a shorter session are usually enough. Skip human toothpaste entirely. Fluoride is not meant to be swallowed and the foaming agents upset dog stomachs.

Water additives and dental diets help at the margins but they do not replace mechanical disruption of plaque, and a dog with exposed dentine may find hard kibble uncomfortable. Soaking kibble briefly, or moving to a soft dental diet, is a reasonable compromise for a sensitive mouth.

Everything above sits inside a broader routine, and the dental care guide lays out the full sequence from exam to daily habit.

When to see a vet

Book an exam within a few days if you see a pink or grey dot in a flattened tooth, a loose tooth, facial swelling, bleeding gums, or a dog that has stopped chewing on one side. Treat chattering jaws, dropping food and sudden refusal of hard food as pain signals rather than old age. Facial swelling with discharge, a swollen eye, or a dog that will not eat at all is urgent and should be seen the same day, because a root abscess can track into the sinus or under the eye. Nothing in this article replaces an exam, and worn teeth cannot be diagnosed from a photograph.

The bottom line

Ask for a dental exam with radiographs before you blame old age, then switch to soft chews and brush the cheek teeth three times a week.

FAQs

Is it normal for an old dog's teeth to be flat?

Some flattening is expected with age, especially on the incisors and canine tips. It becomes abnormal when the wear is uneven, when you can see a pink or grey dot in the centre of a cusp, or when the dog shows pain signs such as chewing on one side or dropping food.

Can worn teeth grow back?

No. Enamel does not regenerate, and the tooth does not replace lost crown height. The goal of treatment is to protect what is left, seal or restore exposed dentine where possible, and prevent the pulp from becoming infected.

Should I stop giving my dog chews if the teeth are worn?

Stop the hard ones, not all of them. Antlers, hooves, bones, nylon bones and tennis balls cause most abrasive wear. Flexible rubber toys and soft dental chews with a VOHC seal keep the chewing habit without grinding the teeth down further.

Does a worn tooth hurt my dog?

It can, once dentine is exposed or the pulp is breached. Dogs hide dental pain well, so chattering jaws, eating on one side, dropping food and refusing hard items are the practical signs. A vet exam with radiographs is the only way to know what is happening under the gum.
