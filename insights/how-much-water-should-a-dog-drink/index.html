How Much Water Should a Dog Drink? A Daily Hydration Guide

General · 2026-10-11

Bottom line: A rough rule for a healthy adult dog is about 50 to 60 ml of water per kilogram of body weight per day, which works out at roughly one ounce per pound - so a 10 kg dog drinks around 500 to 600 ml, and a 25 kg dog around 1.2 to 1.5 litres. That total includes water in wet food, and it rises sharply in hot weather, after hard exercise or on a dry-food diet. Sudden increases, sudden decreases, or a dog that drinks constantly and still seems thirsty are the numbers that matter - those are vet conversations, because both too little and too much can signal real disease.

A healthy adult dog generally needs about 50 to 60 ml of water per kilogram of body weight per day - roughly one ounce per pound - so a 10 kg dog drinks around half a litre and a 25 kg dog around 1.2 to 1.5 litres. That is a starting range, not a rule, and the more useful skill is knowing what is normal for your own dog, because a sudden change in drinking is often the first sign that something is wrong.

The basic calculation

Use body weight, not size or breed:

  • 5 kg (11 lb) dog: 250 - 300 ml a day
  • 10 kg (22 lb) dog: 500 - 600 ml a day
  • 20 kg (44 lb) dog: 1.0 - 1.2 litres a day
  • 30 kg (66 lb) dog: 1.5 - 1.8 litres a day
  • 40 kg (88 lb) dog: 2.0 - 2.4 litres a day

Two things to keep in mind. First, this is total water intake, and wet food is roughly 70 to 80 per cent water, so a dog on a wet or raw diet may drink noticeably less at the bowl and still be perfectly hydrated. Second, puppies, nursing mothers, working dogs and dogs on medication can land well outside the range.

What pushes intake up and down

More water is normal when:

  • The weather is hot, or your dog has been running hard.
  • Your dog eats dry kibble rather than wet food.
  • They are on a high-salt treat or a new food.
  • They are a puppy or a nursing mother.
  • They are recovering from vomiting or diarrhoea - but see the vet callout below.

Less water is normal when:

  • Your dog eats mostly wet food.
  • The weather is cool and activity is low.
  • They drink more at night or in one big session after meals.

The pattern matters more than the millilitres. Most dogs drink several times a day, with peaks after meals and after walks.

The practical way to measure it

You do not need to test anything. Fill a marked jug each morning, top up during the day, and note how much you used. Do this for a few normal days and you have your dog's personal baseline. Then, if you ever wonder whether something has changed, you can compare instead of guessing.

If you have more than one pet, this gets messy fast. Separate them at mealtimes for a day or use a microchip-reading bowl so you can attribute the intake.

Water and the food bowl are linked

Hydration affects appetite, digestion and urinary health. A dog that is drinking less than usual may also be eating less, and the reverse is true. If you are managing a fussy eater, our guide to a dog that is not eating walks through how to tell a fussy day from a real problem. If instead your dog is suddenly drinking a lot and you have already read about a dog not drinking water, the two problems sit at opposite ends of the same question - what has changed, and how fast.

Adding a little warm water to kibble, or switching part of the diet to wet food, is a simple way to nudge a borderline-low dog upwards. Fresh water in a clean bowl, changed daily, matters more than most owners expect - many dogs refuse water that has gone flat or smells of plastic.

Signs of dehydration you can check at home

Run through these if you are worried, especially after a hot day or an episode of vomiting:

  • Gum check. Gums should be slick and wet. Tacky, sticky or dry gums suggest dehydration.
  • Skin tent. Gently lift the skin between the shoulder blades and release. It should snap back instantly. Slow return is a warning sign.
  • Eyes. Sunken-looking eyes, especially alongside lethargy, are serious.
  • Energy and appetite. A dehydrated dog is usually flat, not just thirsty.

If two or more of these are abnormal, treat it as a same-day vet visit rather than a top-up at the bowl.

When to see a vet

Call your vet if your dog is drinking noticeably more or less than usual for more than a day or two, if they drink constantly and still seem thirsty, if they are urinating far more often, or if they cannot keep water down. Same-day care is needed for vomiting or diarrhoea with refusal to drink, tacky or dry gums, skin that does not spring back, sunken eyes, collapse, or a dog that is weak and off their food.

>

Excessive thirst is not a quirky habit - it is one of the most consistent early signs of conditions such as diabetes, kidney disease and hormonal disorders, and it is easy to miss because a thirsty dog looks healthy. Never restrict a dog's water to "fix" excessive drinking without veterinary direction. That can be dangerous and it hides the diagnosis.

Getting a dog to drink more

For dogs that genuinely drink too little, the fixes are gentle and low-risk:

  • Add a splash of warm water or low-sodium broth to meals.
  • Offer water in more than one place, at more than one bowl height.
  • Try a fountain - moving water appeals to many dogs.
  • After walks, offer a drink before the next activity rather than only at home.
  • Keep bowls clean and refreshed twice a day.

Persistent low intake, especially with weight loss or a dry nose and dull coat, deserves an examination. Skin and coat problems often track back to hydration and diet, and our skin and coat guide covers what to look for alongside it.

The bottom line

Aim for roughly 50 to 60 ml per kilogram of body weight per day and learn your dog's own normal by measuring for a few days - then treat any sustained change in drinking, in either direction, as a reason to call the vet rather than to adjust the bowl. Sudden stops and sudden surges both carry meaning. For bowls, fountains and low-sodium toppers, see the shop.

FAQs

How much water should a dog drink per day?

A common guideline for a healthy adult dog is about 50 to 60 ml per kilogram of body weight, which is roughly one ounce per pound - so a 10 kg dog around 500 to 600 ml and a 30 kg dog around 1.5 to 1.8 litres. That total includes water from wet food, and it rises with heat, exercise and a dry-food diet.

Is it bad if my dog drinks a lot of water suddenly?

A sudden, sustained increase in drinking deserves a vet visit. It can follow a change in diet, weather or medication, but it is also one of the earliest signs of diabetes, kidney disease and some hormonal conditions. Do not restrict the water - measure how much they drink and book an appointment.

How can I tell if my dog is dehydrated?

Check the gums - they should be slick and wet, not tacky. Lift the skin between the shoulder blades; it should snap back instantly. Sunken-looking eyes, lethargy and refusing water are more serious signs. If two or more checks look wrong, treat it as a same-day vet visit.

Should I add water to my dog's dry food?

Yes, it is a simple and safe way to raise total water intake, especially for dogs that eat kibble and drink little. A splash of warm water or low-sodium broth mixed into the meal works well. Introduce it gradually if your dog is fussy, and make sure the food is eaten within a short window rather than left out.
