Entropion in Cats: Rolled Eyelids and When Surgery Is Needed

Eye Care & Tear Stains · 2026-11-15

Bottom line: Have any squinting cat stained for an ulcer the same day, treat the eye pain before agreeing to eyelid surgery, and reserve the scalpel for a lid that stays rolled once the eye is comfortable.

Entropion in cats is an eyelid margin rolled inward so the hair rubs the surface of the eye. In dogs it is usually a breeding and conformation problem; in cats it is more often secondary, which changes the whole plan. A painful eye makes the lids clamp shut and roll in, so the first job is treating the eye, not the eyelid.

Get this distinction wrong and a cat ends up in surgery for a lid that would have corrected itself once the ulcer or the herpes flare was under control.

Spastic versus anatomical entropion

Spastic entropion is a reflex. Something hurts the eye, whether a corneal ulcer, a foreign body, conjunctivitis or uveitis, the cat squints hard, the orbicularis muscle contracts, and the lower lid rolls inward. Block the pain and the lid sits normally again. This is the common version in cats.

Anatomical entropion is structural. The eyelid is genuinely too long for the eye, or the eye sits too deep in the socket. Flat-faced breeds like Persians and Himalayans, plus Maine Coons and British Shorthairs, carry more risk. Here the lid stays rolled even when the eye is comfortable, and surgery is the only permanent answer.

There is a third category worth knowing: eyelid hair that points the wrong way (trichiasis) or an eyelid margin that developed abnormally, which produces the same scraping without an actual roll. A vet can tell these apart under magnification.

What owners actually notice

  • Squinting or holding one eye half shut, often worse in bright light
  • Watery eye with a brown tear track down the muzzle
  • Rubbing the face on carpet, furniture or your leg
  • Hiding, head-shy behavior, or resisting being picked up
  • In long-standing cases, a grey or brown haze over the eye surface

The tearing is worth a note. Persistent epiphora in a flat-faced cat gets blamed on anatomy, but a rolled lid or misdirected lashes are also frequent causes, and the staining routine in our tear stain guide will not fix either one.

What the exam checks before anyone operates

A responsible workup rules out the things that make surgery the wrong move:

  • Fluorescein stain to look for a corneal ulcer, which is present in a large share of these cats
  • Schirmer tear test to check tear production, since a dry eye makes any surgery riskier and needs managing first
  • Reassessment after topical anesthesia: this is the key step. If the lid margin returns to a normal position once the eye is numb, the entropion is spastic and medical treatment comes first
  • Conjunctival swab or PCR when herpesvirus is suspected, because FHV-1 causes recurrent conjunctivitis and can leave adhesions between the lid and the cornea
  • Tonometry in any cat with a cloudy or painful eye, to exclude glaucoma

The treatment ladder

1. Medical first. Lubricant drops or gel to cushion the cornea (available in our shop), topical antibiotic if there is an ulcer, pain relief, and famciclovir when herpes is involved, which the feline herpes eye guide covers. A soft contact lens or a temporary third eyelid flap protects a damaged cornea while it heals.

2. Temporary tacking. A few sutures that evert the lid margin, often used in young cats whose head shape is still changing. It may need repeating, and it buys time.

3. Permanent surgery. A Hotz-Celsus procedure or a wedge resection removes a sliver of lid and turns the margin outward. Done on a quiet eye, by someone comfortable with feline lids.

4. Aftercare. Cone for about two weeks, no clay litter dust near the face, recheck at ten to fourteen days for suture removal.

The risk of operating too eagerly is over-correction: a lid that ends up rolled outward cannot blink properly, the cornea dries, and you have traded entropion for a lifelong lubricant habit. That is why the spastic cases get treated medically first.

What happens if it is left alone

Chronic hair contact does not stay comfortable. The cornea responds with blood vessel growth, then pigment, then scarring. An ulcer can deepen into a descemetocele and perforate. In herpes-positive cats, adhesions can form between the conjunctiva and the cornea, and those are difficult to undo.

When to see a vet

Any cat squinting, tearing or holding an eye closed needs a same-day exam with a fluorescein stain, because a corneal ulcer can worsen within 24 hours. Go immediately if the eye is cloudy, the pupil looks different from the other side, there is green or yellow discharge, or the cat is pawing at its face. Never use a leftover steroid drop from another pet: steroid on an ulcer can speed up a perforation.

The bottom line

Have any squinting cat stained for an ulcer the same day, treat the eye pain before agreeing to eyelid surgery, and reserve the scalpel for a lid that stays rolled once the eye is comfortable.

FAQs

Can entropion in cats fix itself?

Sometimes, when it is spastic. If the inward roll is caused by squinting from a painful eye, numbing the eye and treating the underlying ulcer or herpes flare usually lets the lid sit normally again. Anatomical entropion, where the lid is structurally too long, does not resolve on its own.

Which cat breeds are prone to entropion?

Flat-faced breeds carry the highest risk, including Persians, Himalayans and Exotic Shorthairs, because the eye sits prominent relative to a short lid. Maine Coons and British Shorthairs are also over-represented. Any cat with a history of chronic eye irritation should have the lid margins checked.

How much does entropion surgery cost, and is it curative?

Cost varies widely by region and whether both eyes are done, and it is a procedure that needs an experienced surgeon rather than a general quote. When the diagnosis is anatomical entropion, the result is usually permanent, provided the correction is conservative enough to leave the cat able to blink.

Is it safe to use my other pet's eye drops while I wait?

No. Steroid drops on an undiagnosed corneal ulcer can lead to perforation, and some canine products are not appropriate for cats at all. Lubricant gel is the only thing safe to use before an exam, and even that should be confirmed with your vet.
