Dog Yeast Infection: Why Skin Smells Musty and Itches

Skin & Coat · 2026-11-03

Bottom line: Get cytology before buying another shampoo, treat with a medicated wash two to three times a week for the full course, and fix the moisture or allergy driving it - otherwise the smell comes back within weeks of the last bath.

A musty, corn-chip smell plus greasy, thickened, itchy skin is the classic picture of a yeast overgrowth in dogs, usually *Malassezia pachydermatis*, and it is a symptom rather than a disease in its own right. Treating the yeast without finding out why it bloomed almost always ends in a relapse, which is why a good vet looks at allergies, moisture and skin folds alongside the infection itself.

What yeast overgrowth looks like

Yeast lives on normal dog skin in small numbers. When the barrier is damaged or the skin stays damp, it multiplies and drives inflammation. The picture differs from a bacterial infection: instead of pustules and crusts you get thickening, grease and a colour shift.

  • Greasy, waxy or flaky skin, often with a yellow-grey cast
  • Skin that feels thick and leathery, like the hide of an elephant
  • Rusty brown staining in the armpits, groin, neck folds and between the toes
  • Constant licking, chewing, scooting and head shaking
  • Patchy hair loss exactly where the dog can reach
  • An odour that survives a bath by a day or two

Ears are a favourite site. A waxy, smelly ear that flares again a fortnight after every treatment is usually yeast sitting on top of an allergy nobody has addressed.

Why the skin lets yeast take over

Yeast is an opportunist, so the real question is what damaged the barrier:

1. Environmental allergy - pollen, dust mites and moulds cause the chronic scratching that starts the cycle.

2. Food allergy - less common than owners expect, but real, and it rarely improves on its own.

3. Trapped moisture - swimming, humid summers, deep skin folds and tight harnesses.

4. Antibiotics and steroids - both disturb the skin's normal flora and local immunity.

5. Hormonal disease - hypothyroidism and Cushing's disease both predispose to recurrent skin infections.

6. Breed - basset hounds, cocker spaniels, Westies, bulldogs and German shepherds carry more skin folds and more atopic tendency.

A smell is not a diagnosis

Cytology settles it. A tape preparation or swab is stained and examined under the microscope, and the number of yeast organisms per field guides treatment. This matters because bacterial and yeast infections frequently run together, and a shampoo aimed at only one of them will stall. If your dog has had three "yeast shampoos" with no lasting result, ask for cytology before buying a fourth.

What treatment involves

  • Medicated shampoo with miconazole, ketoconazole or chlorhexidine, two to three times a week at first, with ten minutes of contact time before rinsing.
  • Leave-on products - rinses, mousses or sprays - to extend the effect between baths.
  • Ear cleaner or drops if the ears are involved, used separately from the bath water.
  • Oral antifungals such as ketoconazole or itraconazole for generalised or stubborn cases, prescribed and monitored by a vet.
  • Trigger control - an allergy workup, a diet trial, or a drying routine, depending on what the history points to.

Expect four to six weeks for thick, darkened skin to soften. The grease and smell improve first; the leathery texture takes longer.

Home care that helps, and what to skip

What helps: drying every fold and paw after walks and swims, washing bedding weekly, clipping the coat short over affected areas, and keeping the bath schedule even after the skin looks normal.

What to skip: yogurt and probiotic powders as a treatment (no evidence they clear skin yeast), apple cider vinegar (it stings broken skin and does not penetrate the follicle), coconut oil (occlusive, and it feeds the greasy environment yeast prefers), and human dandruff shampoo (wrong active ingredient, too harsh for dog skin).

Keeping it from coming back

Once the skin is clear, drop to a weekly or fortnightly medicated bath through humid months, dry the folds daily and stay on whatever allergy plan your vet set up. If the smell returns within two weeks of finishing, that is a signal the underlying trigger was never controlled rather than a new infection.

When to see a vet

Book a visit within a few days for any greasy, smelly, thickened skin that has not improved after two weeks of medicated bathing. See a vet the same day if the skin is weeping, bleeding or raw, if there is spreading redness or fever, or if the dog stops eating - open sores on top of a yeast infection can mean a deep bacterial infection underneath.

The bottom line

Get cytology before buying another shampoo, treat with a medicated wash two to three times a week for the full course, and fix the moisture or allergy driving it - otherwise the smell comes back within weeks of the last bath.

FAQs

Does a musty smell always mean a yeast infection?

Not always, but it is the strongest single clue. Bacterial infections and ear disease can smell unpleasant too, and the two often overlap. Cytology - a stained swab or tape sample under the microscope - is what separates them.

Can I treat dog yeast with yogurt or probiotics?

Not as a treatment for skin yeast. There is no good evidence that oral probiotics clear a Malassezia overgrowth, and while they may support gut health, relying on them delays cytology and proper medicated treatment.

How long until the skin looks normal again?

Grease and smell improve within one to two weeks of consistent medicated bathing. The thickened, darkened, leathery texture takes four to six weeks to soften, so do not stop the bath schedule just because the smell is gone.

Why does my dog's yeast keep coming back?

Because the trigger was never controlled. Allergies, trapped moisture and hormonal disease all let yeast bloom again. A dog with three or more episodes a year needs an allergy workup or thyroid check, not just another shampoo.
